## 1. For the author: the rows that are not SUPPORTED, worst first

Each item gives the sentence as it stands, what the sources support, and one or two plainer sentences they would support. The options are suggestions. You rule on each: revise, find a source, or cut.

### 1. The colleague: what the company could do, and whether it was legal (lines 22 and 26)

**As it stands:**
- "there was nothing he could do about it. I do not mean he chose to do nothing. I mean there was nothing he could do."
- "If he pressed for a raise, the company could send him back across that line, out of the country, and away from his family."
- "There was no invoice he could send and no court that recognized the debt. The whole thing was legal and ordinary."

**What the sources support.** The only source for his situation is your own note of 2026-06-04 (`research/story-drafts/ch1-bone-sense-moments_2026-06-04.md`): "a non-citizen whose green card was tied to continued employment … who knew leaving (or pressing for fair pay) risked deportation." Three things follow.

- An employer cannot remove anyone from the country. It can end the job and withdraw its sponsorship. For a worker on an employer-sponsored visa, losing the job ended lawful status, and the grace period of "up to 60 consecutive days" (8 CFR 214.1(l)(2)) dates only from January 17, 2017. So the fear was well founded, but the act that sends a person out is the government's, and the company's act is the firing. Your note says "risked." The chapter says "could send him back."
- "Nothing he could do" is wider than the law. Since 2000 the statute has said an H-1B worker "is authorized to accept new employment upon the filing by the prospective employer of a new petition" (8 U.S.C. 1184(n)). In practice a pending green-card case tied to one employer made moving very costly, which matches your note. The chapter does not say which visa he held, and the project does not record it.
- "The whole thing was legal" has no source in the project, and the law cuts against it if he held an H-1B. The regulation requires an H-1B employer to pay "the greater of the actual wage rate" or the prevailing wage, and defines the actual wage as "the wage rate paid by the employer to all other individuals with similar experience and qualifications" (20 CFR 655.731(a)). The Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division enforces it. Paying the most credentialed person half of what a junior developer made may have broken that rule. Whether it did depends on facts the project does not hold (his visa type, his job classification). "No court that recognized the debt" runs into the same rule.

**Options.**
- Line 22, the raise sentence: "If he pressed for a raise, he could lose the job, and without the job he could not stay in the country." Or keep your sentence and change one verb: "the company could have him sent back across that line."
- Line 22, "nothing he could do": "there was almost nothing he could do about it," or leave it and let the next sentences carry the reason.
- Line 26: "As far as any of us could tell, the whole thing was legal, and it was ordinary." Or cut "legal": "The whole thing was ordinary."
- One fact only you can supply: was it a work visa, a pending green card, or both? The answer settles which of these sentences can stand as written.

**Also:** he is a living person and the chapter gives his pay, his immigration status, and details a former coworker would recognize (the rooftop habit, the greetings, Washington, Paris, New Delhi). Your June note says to anonymize him and drop identifying specifics. He has no row in the consent tracker.

### 2. "The pricing is new." (line 134)

**As it stands:** "The framework this book presents extends the move into the pricing of commons. The pricing is new. The move is old."

**What the sources support.** The project's own positioning check (`tools/workstream-handoffs/verified-positioning-findings_2026-06-24.md`, Verdict 3) found no earlier framework that assembles these parts "into a per-extraction-event operational corrective," and calls that a "negative-existence finding, bounded by the search." It names the closest earlier work: TEEB and True Cost Accounting, and Costanza and Perrings (1990), whose bond was also set in advance. The Technical Appendix v2.0.1 opens by saying the claim "is narrow. It is not a claim to have found a new cost or a new number: the damage figures are other people's." Putting a price on a commons is not new (Pigou, the social cost of carbon). What is new is this way of doing it. The chapter's sentence claims the wider thing.

**Options.**
- "The pricing, done this way, is new. The move is old."
- "What is new is pricing each act of extraction and asking who paid. The move is old."

### 4. "the top one to two percent of household incomes in the United States for those years" (line 126)

**What the sources support.** Nothing yet. The chapter carries a note to pin this at endnote time, and the pin is still empty. The choice of table matters, because the published thresholds differ by more than a hundred thousand dollars. The IRS figure for the top 1 percent in 2018 is adjusted gross income of $540,009 and above, but that counts tax returns, not households. A household threshold has to come from a household table. The Census Bureau's household table (HINC-06) cannot settle it: its top bracket is "$250,000 or more," with no percentile line above that. The Congressional Budget Office's household-income series is the likely source, and cbo.gov refused the fetch (see "Limits"). Only you can set your own figures against the table.

**Options.** Keep the sentence and fill the pin from one named household table for 2017 and 2018 (the chapter's pin names the Census tables first; they will not do it). If the household table puts you outside the top 2 percent in either year, "the top few percent of household incomes" is the sentence it would support.

### 5. "proves" (line 132)

**As it stands:** "it gave me the arithmetic that shows the system as it is, and that proves the decisions we made would not all have been the same if we had known the real cost."

**What the sources support.** The arithmetic shows costs that were not counted. It cannot prove what people would have decided. If "we" means you (the apartment, the house), you can say it of yourself. If it means everyone, nothing in the project proves it.

**Options.**
- "…and that makes me doubt we would have made all the same decisions if we had known the real cost."
- "…and I know I would not have made all the same decisions if I had known the real cost."

### 6. "as it always does" (line 72)

**As it stands:** "the fame and fortune of it went to the enterprise he served, as it always does in the patient, ordinary machinery of salaried invention."

**What the sources support.** For your grandfather, yes: U.S. Patent 3,670,890 names him as an inventor and gives the assignee as NASA. Under Executive Order 10096 (1950) the government takes "the entire right, title and interest" in inventions made by its employees on the job. "Always" is wider. Since 1986 the law has said the agency "shall pay each year the first $2,000, and thereafter at least 15 percent, of the royalties or other payments" to the inventor (15 U.S.C. 3710c), and NASA runs an inventor awards program. The patent also puts his name on the record, which is a kind of credit.

**Option.** "…as it nearly always does…" or "…as it did for every salaried inventor of his time…"

- **"stretches of three hours and then half an hour."** Your answer was about three and a half hours, then naps of thirty to forty minutes. Close enough to stand; noted so you know it was rounded.

- **"traveled on a diplomatic passport."** Today's rule (22 CFR 51.3) issues an official passport to "an officer or employee of the U.S. government traveling abroad to carry out official duties" and a diplomatic passport to "a Foreign Service Officer" or a person with diplomatic or comparable status. A Navy civil servant could have held either, depending on the assignment, and I read the current rule, not the one in force when he traveled. Worth one question to your father.
