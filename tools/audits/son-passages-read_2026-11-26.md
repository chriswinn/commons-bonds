# Chapter 1: are the passages about the author's son right for this book?

**Verdict: keep the son in the chapter; cut the last mention (line 140).** Line 106 and the hospital scene
(lines 110–120) are the chapter's argument made visible and should stay as written. Line 140 is the one place
the chapter stops showing a severed cost and asks the reader to grieve; cut it, and cut line 62 with it.

**Status:** internal; one reader, one read, 2026-11-26, per
`tools/workstream-handoffs/son-passages-read-kickoff_2026-11-25.md`. Read cold: Chapter 1 start to finish, then
`tools/commons_bonds_guiding_constraints_v1_0_0.md`, then the consent policy. No earlier rigor pass, redline
note or handoff about Chapter 1 was read. **No chapter text was changed. The author decides.** Line numbers
are the chapter as it stands on main today (they match the kickoff's).

## Passage by passage

| Lines | What it is | Call |
|---|---|---|
| 62 | "My son would never meet my grandfather." | **Cut** (small) |
| 106 | The pattern "would cost me the early years with my son, and it would cost me my marriage." | **Keep, as written** |
| 110–120 | The leave running out, the flight from Savannah, the hospital room | **Keep, whole** |
| 140 | "The son I once held is somewhere in the world now. … We have no contact." | **Cut** |

**Line 62: cut.** It opens a section that is about the grandfather, and it made me expect the son to matter in
that section. He does not appear again for forty-four lines. The fact itself is ordinary (most
great-grandfathers are gone before the great-grandchildren come), so the sentence only carries weight as a
shadow thrown backward from line 140. The nephew passage at line 86 already does the handing-down across
generations, and does it better. Line 106 does not need the earlier plant: the marriage has none and lands
fine. This is a craft call, not a privacy one. If line 140 goes and he likes this sentence, it can stay
without harm. If it goes, the next sentence needs the words "my grandfather" in it, which is his edit to make.

**Line 106: keep.** This is the hinge of the chapter. Without it the pages on the 138-hour weeks read as a
record to be proud of. With it they read as a price. It is one sentence, it claims only what the chapter then
shows ("the early years"), and it is as plain as the book's own vocabulary. Nothing to change.

**Lines 110–120: keep.** This is the best writing in the chapter and it is doing the book's work, not asking
for sympathy. Three reasons. First, it pays off the opening: the colleague's rooftops return as the airplane
window, and the thing seen from above is the cost. Take the scene out and that image lands on nothing.
Second, it is the chapter's one full demonstration of "no villain": the company was correct, the colleagues
were kind, the nurses tried, and the cost still fell on a newborn who could not refuse it and on no invoice
anywhere. "A warm chest instead of a plastic bed" is a severed cost a reader can hold. Third, it is what makes
the top-five-percent paragraph bite; that argument needs a stake, and this is it. For the reader the book is
for, someone weighing work that will take time from their own family, this scene is the most useful thing in
the chapter. Reducing the son to the single clause at line 106 would leave Chapter 1 an argument with its
proof removed.

One optional note for his own pen: "the stuff of daydreams" is the only stock phrase in the scene. Everything
around it is exact.

**Line 140: cut.** Four reasons, in order of weight.

1. **It claims more than the chapter has shown.** The chapter earns "the early years." Line 140 reports a
   total and present loss, and its position (straight after "It does not undo the damage") tells the reader
   that this too is what the work pattern cost. The chapter does not show that, and at line 122 the author
   refuses exactly that kind of claim about the marriage: "To say the work alone ended it would be a lie."
   Line 140 gets no such care. In a chapter whose discipline is the smallest honest number, it is the one
   entry larger than the evidence on the page.
2. **It leaves the reader with the wrong question.** I finished the chapter asking why there is no contact,
   not what the full accounting of my own choices would be. The book never answers the first question and
   should not. An unanswered question helps when the book means to answer it later. Here it only competes
   with the last two lines, which hand the ledger to the reader.
3. **It shifts the chapter's last weight onto the author.** The chapter is careful about proportion: the
   Tubman paragraph, "I had options a coal miner does not have." The book's heaviest costs belong to McDowell,
   Libby and the watermen. Ending the opening chapter on the author's own loss, however real, tilts that
   balance at the moment a scholar or a press editor is deciding what kind of book this is.
4. **The chapter has already said it, better.** "The house is not the thing I miss" (line 128) tells the
   reader everything line 140 does, and trusts them. With line 140 gone, "This is an honest accounting. It
   does not undo the damage" runs straight into "I am writing for the ones still ahead of us," and "the ones
   still ahead" means the generations the book is for. That is the stronger ending.

## The three versions, tried

- **As it stands:** the strongest emotional close, and the weakest for the argument. The reader leaves
  thinking about this family.
- **Hospital scene kept, line 140 cut (recommended):** the cost is shown once, fully, where the chapter has
  the evidence for it; the ending returns to the reader and the ledger.
- **Son reduced to the clause at line 106:** safest and emptiest. The altitude image, "no villain" and the
  income paragraph all lose what they rest on. This would hurt the book.

## The case for keeping line 140

It deserves a fair hearing. "It does not undo the damage" wants a referent, and a chapter that calls itself an
honest accounting while leaving out its largest entry could be said to be doing what the book indicts:
absorbing a cost quietly and not thinking much of it. The four sentences are as bare as prose can be; nothing
in them performs. They are what a reader will remember, and they make "the ones still ahead of us" a person
before it is a principle. For the reader weighing a job, it is the plainest warning the chapter can give. And
it is his life to tell.

I still come down on cutting it, because the warning is one the chapter cannot stand behind as cause and
effect, and the author says as much himself at line 122. The cost is on the page at lines 106 and 116–120. The
ledger is honest without this line.

## The consent point

`tools/memory/feedback_named_subject_consent.md` is written for named people, and no name is given here. But
the author's name is on the cover, so "my son" identifies one living person to anyone who knows the family.
The policy has no rule for a relative identified by relationship; that is a gap worth one sentence in the
policy later. Applied in its spirit:

- **Lines 106 and 110–120 raise little.** They are the author's own experience as a father. They do disclose
  that his son was born early and spent time in hospital, which is a health fact about a living person; he
  should know he is putting that in print, but it is a fact about a newborn, told with tenderness.
- **Line 140 is the one that raises it.** It states a fact about a living person's present life and his
  relationship with his father. The policy's default for a living subject is to hold back until consent can be
  sought, and to treat consent as a window that can open later. By the sentence's own account he cannot be
  asked, so that window does not exist. Under the policy the safe default is the only state available, and
  the safe default is to leave it out. A book also stays in print: this sentence would be on the public
  record about him permanently, and he has no way to answer it.

The policy and the craft reading point the same way.

## Two things noticed in passing (not asked, his to weigh)

- **Line 126**, "the year and more of surgery and rehabilitation that life eventually required," does not say
  whose. Coming straight from the hospital room, I first read it as the son's. If it is the author's own, a
  word would stop the misreading; if it is not, it is a second health fact about a living person.
- **Line 122** on the marriage ("that is not my story to tell") is the model for how this chapter handles
  what belongs partly to someone else. It is why line 140 stands out.
