## 1. For the author: the rows that are not supported, worst first

Each gives the sentence as it stands, what the source supports, and one or two sentences the source would support. You rule on each: revise, find a source, or cut.

### 1. What Kennedy blamed in Canton (¶4, and the chapter's frame)

- **As it stands:** "Then he laid the blame where a candidate lays it: on his opponent, on the administration in power, on a policy that had failed." Then: "He had the wrong defendant."
- **What the speech says** (full text read, American Presidency Project, Sept 27, 1960): "I ran in the primary in West Virginia. I spent some time in McDowell County in West Virginia. McDowell County mines more coal than it ever has in its history, probably more coal than any county in the United States and yet there are more people getting surplus food packages in McDowell County than any county in the United States. **The reason is that machines are doing the jobs of men**, and we have not been able to find jobs for those men." A few sentences later: "This administration has opposed both area assistance bills."
- **The difference.** He named a cause, and it was automation. He blamed the administration for not helping, not for causing it. The chapter's own town section gives the same cause he did ("The mines mechanized"). A reader who looks up the speech will find the chapter's opening unfair to him. **This is the most important row, because the "wrong defendant" frame opens and closes the chapter.**
- **Options the source supports:**
  - "He told the crowd the reason: 'machines are doing the jobs of men.' And he faulted the administration, which had opposed two bills to aid places like it. He had the cause right as far as it went. He had no name for what lay under it."
  - Keep "the wrong defendant," but aim it at the machines: "He named the machines. The machines were how the work left. They were not why the cost stayed."

### 2. What Kennedy saw (¶1 and ¶2)

- **As it stands:** he "drove through McDowell County" in May 1960; "children standing in doorways with the swollen bellies of the underfed"; "surplus cheese and flour… stacked in cabins"; "men sitting on porches in the middle of a working day"; "Some of those men had carried rifles in a war."
- **What the project holds:** nothing. The only match is the case-study file, which is the chapter copied. The Canton speech supports "I spent some time in McDowell County" during the primary, the surplus food packages, and men out of work. It does not give a month, a drive, children, cabins, porches or veterans.
- **Status: no source in the project.** These are scene details about a real person that nobody has traced. A source may exist (Theodore White's *The Making of the President 1960* describes Kennedy in the West Virginia coalfields; I have not read it, and it is not in the bibliography).
- **Options:** (a) find and read a source before any outside reader sees the chapter; (b) open on what the record holds: "In the spring of 1960 John F. Kennedy, then a senator running in the West Virginia primary, spent time in McDowell County. What he remembered was this: the county was mining more coal than it ever had, and more of its people were living on government surplus food than in any other county in the United States."

### 3. Where Kennedy's remarks about "the children" came from (¶4)

- **As it stands:** "He named the want, the idle men and the children."
- **Source:** the Canton passage names surplus food and men without jobs. No children.
- **Option:** "He named the surplus food and the men without work."

### 4. When Robert Bailey died (¶16)

- **As it stands:** "Bailey died in February of 2019, twenty-eight days after his last interview."
- **Source** (West Virginia Public Broadcasting, read in full): "He shared his final story with Inside Appalachia host, Jessica Lilly, on February 15, 2019… Robert Bailey passed away 28 days after this conversation."
- **Status: contradicted.** Twenty-eight days after February 15 is in mid-March 2019. The interview was in February; the death was not.
- **Option:** "Bailey gave his last interview in February of 2019. He died twenty-eight days later."

### 5. The strike date (¶15)

- **As it stands:** "miners across the coalfields walked out in a wildcat strike in 1968."
- **Sources:** nothing in the project dates it. Outside accounts found this session date the West Virginia black lung walkout to February 18, 1969, lasting about three weeks, and the Black Lung Association's founding to December 1968 or January 1969.
- **Status: contradicted** by the outside accounts; none of them was a primary record. Derickson's *Black Lung* (in the bibliography, not read) is the place to settle it.
- **Option:** "in a wildcat strike in 1969."

### 6. Years of life lost (¶18)

- **As it stands:** "Each man who dies of it loses between eight and thirteen years of the life he would otherwise have had."
- **Source** (CDC, *MMWR* 67(30), Aug 3, 2018): "During 1999–2016, the mean YPLL per decedent increased 55.6%, from 8.1 to 12.6 years per decedent." Overall mean for the period: 8.8 years.
- **The difference.** Eight and thirteen are not a range across men. They are the average in 1999 and the average in 2016. The true finding is stronger and more alarming than the sentence: the loss per death is growing.
- **Options:** "In 1999 a miner who died of black lung lost about eight years of life on average. By 2016 he lost more than twelve." Or: "A miner who dies of it now loses more than twelve years of life on average, half again as many as in 1999."

### 7. Why the disease is rising (¶18)

- **As it stands:** "rising fastest among younger miners, men with fewer than twenty years underground, because the newer machines cut faster and throw a finer and more dangerous dust."
- **Sources:** the CDC report says the rise in years lost is "consistent with observed increased severity and rapid progression of disease." The bibliography's Blackley 2018 entry is summarized as a resurgence "among younger miners." Nothing read gives "fewer than twenty years" or the machine explanation. The research literature I saw in passing points to silica from cutting through rock; I did not read it.
- **Status: says more than the source** on the tenure threshold, and **no source in the project** for the cause.
- **Option:** "It is rising again, it is more severe, and it is progressing faster, including in younger miners." Leave the cause out until one source is read.

### 8. The price of "the coal" (¶8)

- **As it stands:** "In 1960 the coal sold at the mouth of the mine for just under five dollars a ton." ("The coal" is McDowell's.)
- **Source** (EIA Table 7.9, verified in the project 2026-06-08): $4.71 is the **United States average** for bituminous coal in 1960. It is not a price for McDowell's metallurgical coal, which generally sold for more than ordinary bituminous.
- **Status: says more than the source** (a national average presented as this county's price).
- **Option:** "In 1960 bituminous coal sold at the mouth of the mine, on average across the United States, for just under five dollars a ton."

### 9. Bailey's company, and what bankruptcy does (¶13)

- **As it stands:** "The company Bailey had worked for went bankrupt. When a company goes bankrupt its corporate shell dissolves, and the obligations inside the shell dissolve with it… It moved onto a federal fund, which means it moved onto the public."
- **Source:** the company was Patriot Coal. "When the bankruptcy was finalized, the company had shed these responsibilities. So, Bailey said the money came from the Black Lung Disability Trust Fund, which covers black lung medical benefits for bankrupt coal companies." Bailey "began working in McDowell County, and… retired from a mine owned by Patriot Coal **in Boone County**."
- **Three differences.** (a) The general rule is too broad: many bankruptcies reorganize a company without dissolving it, and what the source supports is that this company shed this obligation. (b) "Onto the public": the fund is financed by a tax on coal and by borrowing from the Treasury (GAO: it "has borrowed from the U.S. Treasury's general fund almost every year since 1979"). "Onto a federal fund that borrows from the Treasury" is exact; "onto the public" is the chapter's reading. (c) Bailey's last mine was in Boone County. He began in McDowell. The chapter can say coal took his lungs; it cannot say McDowell did (the chapter on main does: "The county had taken his lungs").
- **Option:** "Patriot Coal, the company Bailey retired from, went bankrupt, and in the bankruptcy it shed its duty to pay for his care. The duty moved to a federal trust fund that has borrowed from the Treasury almost every year since 1979."

### 10. Where and when Bailey said it (¶14)

- **As it stands:** "Bailey testified before Congress twice about the backlog of black lung claims, about the men who waited and died while their paperwork moved through the system. He said that most laws have been written with blood."
- **Source:** he "spoke twice to Congress during Senate hearings, urging lawmakers to act quickly in order to reduce the backlog of black lung cases." The words about blood are from his last interview, on hospice, not from testimony: "Most laws across the nation, most laws [have] been written with blood. I don't want the things I went through just to drop and no knowledge to be gained."
- **Status: says more than the source** twice: "waited and died" is not in the account of his testimony, and the placement makes the quotation read as testimony.
- **Option:** "Bailey spoke twice at Senate hearings, urging Congress to clear the backlog of black lung claims. In his last interview he said that most laws have been written with blood."

### 11. What caused his cancer (¶16)

- **As it stands:** "The cancer that killed him was brought on, in part, by the drugs that had kept his transplanted lungs alive."
- **Source:** "He said that doctors told him his liver cancer was probably linked to medications he took as part of his treatment after his lung transplant."
- **Status: says more than the source.** The source is Bailey reporting what doctors thought probable.
- **Option:** "He died of liver cancer. His doctors told him it was probably linked to the drugs he took after the transplant."

### 12. The reclamation figures (¶23)

- **As it stands:** "Across the coalfields more than 633,000 acres are waiting on reclamation. Doing the work would cost between $7.5 billion and $9.75 billion."
- **Source** (Appalachian Voices, *Repairing the Damage*, 2021, summary read): "a total of 633,000 acres in need of some degree of reclamation"; "$7.5 to $9.8 billion dollars across all 7 states"; "Total available bonds across the seven eastern states amount to about $3.8 billion dollars." The report covers modern-era mines in seven eastern states.
- **Status: contradicted** on one figure ($9.8 billion, not $9.75 billion) and **says more than the source** on scope ("across the coalfields" for seven eastern states) and on "more than 633,000."
- **Option:** "In seven eastern coal states, 633,000 acres of mined land still need some degree of reclamation. The work would cost between $7.5 billion and $9.8 billion. The bonds available to pay for it come to about $3.8 billion."

### 13. The opioid markets (¶59)

- **As it stands:** "The drug found its largest markets where the wage economy had already collapsed, where physical work had left chronic physical pain…" and "No single document confesses to a plan."
- **Sources:** the one study the bibliography lists for this (Alpert, Evans, Lieber and Powell, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2022; abstract read) explains where OxyContin went by something else: states with stricter prescription paperwork got "less marketing," and distribution there "was about 50% lower." It does not say the drug went where wages had collapsed. The bibliography's note that the paper backs "the geographic-distress half" of the claim does not match the abstract. Van Zee (2009), named in the June citation notes, was not read.
- **Status: contested.** The project's own listed source gives a different reason from the chapter's.
- **Options:** (a) read Van Zee 2009 and keep only what it says; (b) "Researchers and prosecutors later traced where the pills went. Counties like McDowell were among the hardest hit," which the drug-death figure supports without a claim about why.

### 14. The income figures (¶30)

- **Status: no source in the project.** The June notes say the federal county income series was to be pulled and never was. The figures replaced an earlier "two-thirds in one year," which also had no source.
- **Option:** cut the two percentages until the series is pulled; the population figures carry the paragraph.

### 15. The steel wind-down (¶29)

- **As it stands:** "The big steel operation that had anchored the county wound down across the 1980s, and more than 1,200 jobs went with it."
- **Sources:** the *West Virginia Encyclopedia* (read this session) says U.S. Steel's successor "ceased all its operations in Gary in 1984." The June notes record 1982, 1984 and 1986 in different places and no employment record for 1,200.
- **Status: contested** on the year; **no source read** for the jobs. "Across the 1980s" is safe. "More than 1,200" is not yet.
- **Option:** "U.S. Steel, which had anchored the county since 1902, ended its operations at Gary in the 1980s."

### 16. "I am coining them here" (¶47)

## 2. Sources the project has found, or that turned up in this check, that bear on the chapter and are not used

1. **Kennedy's own words in Canton.** "Machines are doing the jobs of men" is quotable, on the record, and is the same cause the chapter gives. Using it would make the opening stronger and fair.
2. **The food stamp pilot was Kennedy's.** USDA: "in fulfillment of a campaign promise made in West Virginia, President Kennedy's first Executive Order called for expanded food distribution and, on Feb. 2, 1961, he announced the initiation of Food Stamp pilot programs." The chapter opens with Kennedy and later reports the first food stamps in Welch, and never connects the two. The first purchase was "a can of pork and beans at Henderson's Supermarket," for a household of fifteen.
3. **Evidence was withheld from sick miners.** The same public-radio report that gives Bailey's story cites the Center for Public Integrity's finding that "the industries' go to law firms had been withholding evidence that proved miners qualified for benefits," and that "certain doctors rarely and sometimes never diagnosed patients to have the disease." The chapter says "There may have been bad men." Your rule from Chapter 5 is that where the record shows wrongdoing, the book says so. This is such a record, and it sits one paragraph from Bailey.
4. **A quarter of McDowell was Black in 1950.** The encyclopedia: McDowell "became the state's center of Black population in the industrial era… A fourth of the population was Black in 1950. By 2020, African Americans made up about eight percent." The chapter does not mention it. Given who will read this chapter first, that absence may be noticed.
5. **When the decline began.** The encyclopedia: McDowell was "the leading coal-producing county in the nation, until it was surpassed by neighboring Logan County in 1955," and "After 1950, mine mechanization led to widespread job losses." The chapter's town section reads as though the work thinned in the 1980s. Kennedy saw the result of the 1950s wave.
6. **Derickson** (two works, in the bibliography) on the union and the recognition of black lung. Would settle the strike date and support "denied as a real disease."
7. **The House Energy and Commerce Committee's 2018 report** on opioid distribution in West Virginia (not in the bibliography). Reported findings: nearly nine million pills in two years to one pharmacy in Kermit, population about 400. It would put a number under "could not have consumed them honestly."
8. **Alpert and colleagues** (in the bibliography). Qualifies row 13.
9. **Case and Deaton** (in the bibliography, listed for Chapter 2's mortality data). Not used.
