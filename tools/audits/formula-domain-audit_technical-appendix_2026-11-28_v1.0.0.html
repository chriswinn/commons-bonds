Part 1. The answer in one page

Part 2. The eight, taught step by step

A1. Theorem 10.3: the threshold τ

The formula in plain words. c(A) = c₀ · (τ / (A − τ))ξ. A is how much of a commons there is. τ is a threshold. c is a cost. When A is far above τ the fraction is tiny and the cost is close to nothing: abundance hides it. As A comes down toward τ the bottom of the fraction shrinks toward zero and the cost shoots up.

Step by step at the edges (take τ = 1 and c₀ = 1 so the numbers are easy):

  1. A = 100: cost 0.01. Hidden.
  2. A = 2: cost 1. A = 1.1: cost 10. A = 1.01: cost 100. Rising fast.
  3. A = 1 exactly: the bottom of the fraction is zero. Division by zero. No answer.
  4. A = 0.9, below the threshold, with ξ = 1: the cost is minus 10. A negative cost.
  5. A = 0.9 with ξ = 2: the cost is plus 100, and it falls as A falls further: 4 at A = 0.5, and 1 at A = 0. The emptier the commons, the cheaper.
  6. A = 0.9 with ξ = 1.5: a negative number raised to a fractional power. Not a real number at all.

So the function is defined only for A above τ, which is what Assumption (ii) says in symbols ("c: (τ, ∞) → ..."). Assumption (i) lets A be anything above zero. The stretch from zero up to τ is inside the theorem's world and outside its cost function. The McDowell label put a real case in that stretch.

Three more things an economist would say.

  • "No cost is infinite." In resource economics a price cannot rise past the cost of the next-best alternative, which Nordhaus named the backstop technology. Heal (1976) is the standard model: extraction cost rises as more is taken, up to the point where the backstop takes over, and then stays flat. I confirmed the paper and its abstract today; I have not read the body. The appendix's own Assumption (iii) already says this: "marginal extraction cost approaches the resource's reservation price near scarcity threshold." A reservation price is a finite number. Assumption (ii) says the cost goes to infinity at the same point. The two assumptions contradict each other.
  • "Whose cost is c?" Two different costs are run together. One is the extractor's cost of getting the next ton out. The other is the cost to the people who depend on the commons. They have different thresholds.
  • "What is τ for coal?" Not stated. McDowell still holds about 1.7 billion recoverable tons (county page, read 2026-11-15), so the coal is not near physical exhaustion.

Your suggestion holds up, and it does more than you asked of it. If τ is the point where a commons stops doing its work (a seam no longer worth mining; a fish stock too small to renew itself), then:

  1. For the extractor's cost, extraction stops at the threshold, because past it the ton costs more than it sells for. Extraction can push A down to τ and no further. The stretch below τ is never reached that way, and the division by zero is approached but not crossed.
  2. A commons can still end up below its threshold when the threshold moves. Cheap gas lowers the price of coal; seams that were worth mining yesterday are not today. The stock did not change. The threshold rose past it. So τ is not a constant. It depends on price and technology.
  3. The theorem is stated per commons ("multi-threshold commons... applies locally to each transition"). McDowell has several. The coal in the ground is at or near an economic threshold. The county's commons of work, tax base and community (the things §7.6 prices) went through their thresholds decades ago: 98,887 people in 1950, 19,111 in 2020. "McDowell, A below τ" was wrong about the coal and arguably right about the community.

Your two-path test on the candidate remark ("above the threshold the framework prices forward; past it, the case is read backward"). This is my run of your test, for you to check. It is not a ruling.

  • Path 1, allocation. Does crossing the threshold change who took the value and who bears the cost? No. The asymmetry is the same on both sides. What changes is the state of the cost: before the threshold it is a foreclosure still to come; after it, the loss of that commons has happened and belongs in Cost Severance Damages. On this path the remark holds for the commons that crossed.
  • Path 2, shield. Does crossing the threshold remove the shield? No. The theorem's mask is abundance, and the mask comes off at the threshold: the cost becomes visible. The shield is distance, and it stays. McDowell is the example: the cost became plain to everyone in the county and was still not paid by those who gained.
  • Result. Both paths agree on a narrower sentence than the candidate: past its threshold, the loss of that commons is read backward, as damage done; costs still running forward from the same extraction, such as its carbon, are still priced forward. The remark as first drafted would have dropped McDowell's forward carbon term, which §5.3 keeps ("carries both"). The reef in §11.12 fits the narrower sentence.

Options.

  1. Bound the domain and say so. Two or three sentences in Assumption (ii): τ is the level at which the commons stops doing its work, set per commons, and it can move with price and technology; the cost function describes the approach from above; a commons past its threshold is read on realized damage. Remove "to infinity" from Assumption (iii)'s neighbour or reconcile the two.
  2. Define the edge. Give the cost a ceiling: c rises until it meets the cost of replacing the commons function (Method 1), and stays there. Where no replacement exists at any price, the case goes to the Asymmetric Regret Rule, as α = 1 already does. Conclusion (II) becomes "the cost rises to the replacement cost" in place of "the cost grows without bound."
  3. Change the form. Use c₀ · (τ/A)ξ, which is defined for every A above zero and blows up only when the commons is entirely gone. τ then is the level at which the cost equals c₀. Simple, but it loses the idea of a threshold short of empty, which is the idea you want.
Recommendation: (a) and (b) together. They are your suggestion written out, they remove the contradiction between Assumptions (ii) and (iii), and they tie the theorem to Method 1 and to the backward reading the appendix already has. Sources to read before the sentences are written: Heal 1976 (body); Pindyck 1978 (read); Krautkraemer 1998, "Nonrenewable Resource Scarcity," Journal of Economic Literature 36(4) (exists; body not opened); and for a fish stock too small to renew itself, the fisheries literature on critical depensation (Clark's Mathematical Bioeconomics is the text I would look in; from memory, not verified).

A2. The scarcity multiplier runs backwards

The formula in plain words. scarcity_multiplier(ς) = 1 + 0.05 × ln(1 + ς). Method 3 multiplies the market price by this. The bigger ς, the bigger the premium.

Step by step.

  1. What is ς? The appendix calls it "reserves-to-production-grounded." The project's June grounding file defines it as stock divided by yearly flow, measured by the reserves-to-production ratio: the number of years the reserves would last at the current rate.
  2. So a large ς means many years of supply left. That is the opposite of scarce.
  3. Put in the appendix's own figures. Oil, about 50 years: multiplier 1.20. McDowell coal, about 278 years on the county's figures: 1.28. National coal, about 422 years: 1.30. A resource with one year left: 1.035. A resource with none left: 1.00, no premium at all.
  4. Read down that list: the less there is left, the smaller the scarcity premium.
  5. Asteroid iron is given ς of 1 to 10 because it is abundant (§11.8). Coal is given 220 to 490 because it has centuries of supply. The same symbol is being used in two opposite senses.
  6. One more thing, from the engine. For McDowell ς is not an input. The engine takes the multiplier (1.27 to 1.31) as given and solves backward for the ς that would produce it. The claims check found the same from the other side (§1b, the scarcity rows).

What an economist would say. "Your scarcity premium is largest for the most abundant resource." It takes one line to show and it is the kind of error that ends a reading.

Options.

  1. Drop the multiplier. Method 3 becomes price ÷ (1 − α). The appendix says three times that the multiplier "never carries the result" and stays between 1.0 and 1.3. The multiples move from 8.5 to 26 down to 6.7 to 20.
  2. Turn it over. Make the premium fall as years of supply rise, for example with ln(1 + T/ς) for a stated horizon T. This needs a new constant and a new justification, for a factor that moves the answer by a quarter at most.
  3. Rename it. Call it what the June file half-says it is: a weight for how much stock is foreclosed ("total-recoverable centuries are foreclosed for this community"). Then it is not scarcity, and the asteroid-iron row has to be redone.
Recommendation: (a). It removes a factor that has no source, no derivation, a wrong direction and no effect on any conclusion.

A3. Irreversibility: α, Method 1, and §15.1.7

The formula in plain words. irreversibility_premium(α) = 1 / (1 − α). α is defined as "the probability the loss cannot be restored at any finite cost."

The edge you asked about is handled. At α = 1 the premium is infinite. The appendix says so in five places (§0, §3.5, §8.3, §11.6, §11.8), restricts α to less than one, and hands that case to the Asymmetric Regret Rule. The engine refuses α = 1. Intended, and said.

What is not handled. Step by step.

  1. The band for coal and oil is α = 0.85 to 0.95, grounded on "atmospheric carbon-cycle disruption irreversible" and on "the barrel is gone."
  2. Method 1, two sections earlier, prices putting that same carbon back: $100 to $1,000 a ton by direct air capture. A loss that can be undone for a price is, by α's own definition, restorable at finite cost. That is the appendix's "restorable at a cost" row, α = 0.1 to 0.5, premium 1.1 to 2.
  3. And "the barrel is gone" is certain, not 85 to 95 percent likely. By the definition that is α = 1 and an infinite premium.
  4. So on its own definition the carbon gives a low α and the barrel gives α = 1. Nothing in the appendix produces 0.85 or 0.95. The band is a chosen weight.
  5. The band decides everything. α = 0.85 gives 6.7; 0.95 gives 20; 0.99 gives 100; 0.999 gives 1,000. The "centre about 15" is the centre of a range nobody derived.
  6. §15.1.7 has the same clash in one sentence: "atmospheric carbon-stability cannot be re-engineered at any finite cost above some threshold." Read with Method 1 beside it, it says the cost is infinite in one place and $300 to $600 in another. I think the sentence meant "past some tipping point," not "above some price."
  7. One more loose end: the appendix says the premium "is a weight, not an expected loss." That is right, and it is worth knowing why: if there is any chance at all that the cost is infinite, the expected cost is infinite. So α cannot be used as a probability in the usual way, and the definition calls it one.

Options.

  1. Show Method 3 as a table over α, with no band and no centre. "If you judge the loss half restorable, the premium is 2; if nine-tenths lost for good, 10." The reader picks. The 8.5 to 26 and the "about 15" come out of the summary tables. Fix §15.1.7 to: carbon can be removed only at a price (Method 1), and past a tipping point the climate it disturbed cannot be restored on any human timescale.
  2. Tie α to Method 1. Define α as the share of the documented damage that has no priced cure in Method 1. Carbon then counts as curable at a price; a removed mountaintop does not. The two methods can no longer contradict each other. This is real work and gives a number for one case.
  3. Move Method 3 out of the cross-check tables into a short note beside the Asymmetric Regret Rule, as an illustration of how heavily irreversibility can be weighted.
Recommendation: (a), with the §15.1.7 sentence. It keeps the method, makes no claim the text cannot support, and takes away the single free number that sets Method 3's whole result. With A2 and A6 it reduces Method 3 to one honest line: the premium over price is α ÷ (1 − α) times the price, for whatever α the reader accepts.

A4. The foreclosure term inside the headline integral

The formula in plain words. The integral adds two things for every future year: what later generations lose by not having the ton (foreclosure), and the damage the ton goes on doing. The foreclosure part is [1 − S(t)] · U: the chance no substitute exists yet, times what the ton would be worth to that year. The calibration sets U at its lower bound from Definition 1.3, U = r(t) · P: the discount rate times the price.

Step by step.

  1. Think of the ton as an asset worth P. An asset worth P that earns the going rate r pays r × P a year. That is the bound: the ton left in the ground is worth at least its "rent" each year.
  2. Now add up r × P a year, forever, discounting at the same r. You get P back, exactly. That is what a price is. So with no substitute ever, the foreclosure term equals the price. By scratch script: $74.7 against the engine's P of $75.
  3. With substitutes arriving (S rising to 0.85), about half of that: $38.5 at the baseline. This is the "7 percent" the appendix calls the framework's own term.
  4. So at its lower bound the foreclosure term can never be more than the market price. It says: taking the ton costs the future, at most, what the ton sells for.
  5. Now move the discount rate. A higher rate puts more weight on the early years, when substitutes are still scarce. So the term gets bigger: $26 (Stern, 1.4%), $38.5 (4%), $43 (Nordhaus, 5.5%). This is why the integral reads $526, $537, $541 in that order. A present value that rises with the discount rate is the reverse of what every economist expects.
  6. Push the rate toward zero. The bound r × P goes to zero and the term goes to zero. Theorem 10.4 says that at a zero long-run rate, with no full substitute, the cost is infinite, and calls that "the correct answer." Formula and calibration point in opposite directions at the same edge.
  7. The engine uses P = $75 and a substitution rate λ = 0.05. Neither is in the text (the claims check noted this). $75 is not the 1960 price ($4.71) and not the price you ruled on 2026-11-27 ($129.33). See Part 6.

What an economist would say. First: "Your own term is the market price counted a second time. The buyer already paid P to the owner." The answer to that is not mathematical. It is the framework's premise that the stock is a commons and later generations had a claim on it (Definition 1.1), and it should be said where the term is introduced. Second: "Your headline goes up when I discount the future more." Third: "Where does $75 come from?"

One sentence you have already ruled is touched by this. Stop 15, item 1: "it gives a lower RCV than treating later generations equally would." True of the formula in general. In the calibrated numbers a lower rate gives a lower figure ($526 at Stern's rate against $541 at Nordhaus's). Not applied yet; worth a second look when it is.

Options.

  1. Keep the bound and say what it is. Three sentences in §11.1: at its lower bound the foreclosure term cannot exceed the price of the ton; it is entered at that bound, about half the price once substitutes are allowed for; because the bound moves with the rate, this term is larger at higher rates, and the figure should not be read as sensitive to time preference. State P and λ. Rule which P.
  2. Take foreclosure out of the headline. The headline becomes the damage total ($504 to $518), all of it other people's sourced work, and foreclosure is reported beside it as "at most one times the price; entered here as Open." Nothing in the headline then depends on P, S, λ or the discount schedule. This reverses your 2026-11-11 decision that the integral is the headline, so it is an architectural call.
  3. Change the bound so it does not move with the rate, for example the long-run rate times the price. The term then stops rising with r₀, but it is still capped at a fraction of the price.
Recommendation: (a). The number is defensible once it is described truthfully, and the appendix already says the framework adds "very little" to the level. Option (b) is the stronger position against a hostile reader and I would not argue against it; it is yours to weigh because it moves the headline.

A5. Observation 10.5: the sign of the price

The formula in plain words. Two paths. Path A: extract the ton and receive its price P. Path B: do not extract; spend an amount I on developing a substitute. The appendix writes the difference in welfare as

SW(B) − SW(A) = (three avoided costs) − (I − P)

Step by step.

  1. On Path A society has P in hand. On Path B it does not: the ton stays in the ground.
  2. On Path B society has spent I. On Path A it has not.
  3. So Path B is worse off than Path A by I and by P, before any avoided costs are counted. The line should read: (avoided costs) − (I + P).
  4. The appendix has − (I − P), which is − I + P. It counts the price given up as a gain.
  5. With that sign, and the premise that I is no more than P, the bracket is zero or negative and Path B wins automatically. That is the "weak dominance under Premises (ii) to (iv)." With the sign corrected it does not follow. Path B wins only if the avoided costs are at least I + P.
  6. The paragraph after the formula argues it the right way round ("the foregone extraction revenue P is less than the social opportunity cost RCV"). So the prose and the displayed line disagree with each other.

Also in this observation (row 13 of the wording fixes). "Weitzman 2001 declining-rate framework establishes CS > 0 conservatively." Weitzman's paper is about which discount rate to use. It establishes nothing about cost severance. And the label is wrong twice over: the sentence calls Premise (i) "CS > 0" two paragraphs after the text explains that Premise (i) is RCV > P and that the two are different. Last, "high constant discount rates that drive CS toward zero" is not what the calibration does: the integral rises with the starting rate (A4). A high rate lowers the result only by lowering the social cost of carbon one chooses.

Options.

  1. Correct the line and state the condition: substitute investment beats extraction when the cost avoided is at least the price forgone plus the investment. For McDowell, on the appendix's figures, that is a wide margin. Drop "weak dominance." Rewrite the Weitzman sentence as a statement about Premise (i).
  2. Demote it from a numbered observation to a remark of four sentences.
  3. Cut 10.5 and its corollary. Nothing in §11 depends on them.
Recommendation: (a). The result survives with an honest condition. The corollary needs one more clause (B8 below).

A6. Method 3 measures a different thing from the integral

Step by step.

  1. The integral adds up costs that fall on others: foreclosure and damage. If nothing is foreclosed and nothing is damaged, it returns zero.
  2. Method 3 starts from the price and multiplies by numbers that are at least one. It can never return less than the price. The appendix says so plainly.
  3. Take asteroid iron: substitutes everywhere, no one harmed. The integral gives about zero. Method 3 gives about the price. Same case, same symbol "RCV," two answers.
  4. Carry it into CS = RCV − B. With Method 3, an unbonded ton of asteroid iron shows cost severance equal to its whole price. The appendix wants this case to come out as "extract freely."
  5. The text gets round this by saying "no premium over the market price." That is the tell. What Method 3 actually measures is the premium: its result minus the price. The premium is zero for asteroid iron, which matches the integral.

Every place Method 3 is read (seed 4). Correctly fenced: §3.5, §3.6, §9.1, Observation 10.2, §11.11 ("not evidence of direction"). Still read as evidence, or mislabelled:

  • §11.6, "The cross-checks": "what they establish is that the integral is not an overstatement." Method 3 cannot establish that. Its level is the price times a chosen α.
  • §11.6, the range line: "(IPG ~8.5–26×)." §3.2 says an IPG above one "indicates underpricing," and §11.11 says Method 3's multiple does not. The label "IPG" should come off.
  • §15.1.3: "each can contradict it." Method 3 can contradict the integral only from below.
  • §11.8, "RCV ≈ their (high) market value," and Corollary 10.5.1: Method 3's reading stated as RCV.

Options. (a) Define Method 3's reading as the premium over price, and correct the four places. (b) Correct the four places only. (c) As in A3(c), move Method 3 out of the tables.

Recommendation: (a). With A2 and A3 it leaves Method 3 small, consistent with the integral at the abundant end, and unable to be quoted against you.

A7. The two halves of the decomposition overlap, and two headlines subtract across unlike costs

First problem, step by step.

  1. RCV is defined from the moment of extraction, t₀, forward to infinity (Definition 1.6). For McDowell, §7.6 writes the integral from 1960.
  2. CSD is "the realized cost of extraction that has already occurred" (Definition 1.11).
  3. For a ton mined in 1960, the years 1960 to 2026 have happened. The damage in those years is inside the integral, and it is also, by definition, realized damage.
  4. total CS = (CSD − B₁) + (RCV − B₂) adds the two. For every retrospective case, which is every case in §11, the years between extraction and today are in both halves.
  5. No number in the appendix is wrong because of this, since no case computes both halves for the same extraction. But the formula as written double counts, and an economist will see it in the definitions.

Options. (a) State a dividing date: CSD covers extraction to the analysis date; RCV runs from the analysis date on. (b) Keep RCV from t₀ and say CSD is the part of it already realized, so the total is RCV − B₁ − B₂ with no double count. (c) One sentence at §5.5 saying the two are never both computed for the same years.

Recommendation: (a). It is one sentence in Definition 1.11 and one in Definition 1.6, and it is what "backward" and "forward" already mean in the prose. It also helps A1: a commons past its threshold falls on the CSD side of the date.

Second problem. Definition 1.7 says "A payment counts only against the cost it was set against," and that CS is reported by component "because an aggregate can hide an uncovered cost behind a well-covered one."

  • McDowell. The headline gap subtracts black-lung payments and reclamation bonds from a figure that is 92 percent carbon. Nothing has been paid against the carbon. By component: carbon, about $496 a ton, with nothing set against it; the other damages ($8 to $22) against a bond several times their size, which is the national-payments-over-local-tons mismatch the appendix already admits. The aggregate ($425 to $450) is conservative, and the text says so, but by the framework's own rule the component line should be shown.
  • Norway. §11.5 says it outright ("credits the fund against a cost it was never meant to pay") and still reports $70 − $36 = $34. By component: carbon $70, bond zero; rent, fully bonded.
  • The Hotelling split. (RCV − H) is described as "the gap that would remain even if every dollar of rent were bonded." With RCV a carbon cost and H a rent, bonding the rent pays none of the carbon; by the same rule the gap that would remain is the whole $70, not $34 to $39.
Recommendation. Keep the aggregate, labelled conservative, and add the component line beside it in each summary table. No figure is retired; one is added.

A8. The foreclosure integral is infinite on the appendix's own Hotelling path

  1. Definition 1.3: U(t) is at least r(t) × P(t), the rate times the price at that later date.
  2. §14.1 writes Hotelling's path as P(t) = P(0) · ert: the price grows at the rate of interest.
  3. The discount factor shrinks at the same rate: e−rt.
  4. Multiply: the growth and the shrinkage cancel. Each future year contributes r × P(0) × (chance of no substitute), for ever. If S never reaches one, that sum has no limit. By scratch script it passes $500 at 2,000 years and $2,300 at 10,000 and keeps going.
  5. Theorem 10.4 avoids this by assuming U grows no faster than a polynomial. That assumption rules out the price path the appendix credits to Hotelling in §4 and §14.1. §7.3's own table says a cost growing at the discount rate does not converge.

Your stop-15 ruling (item 6) already changes §14.1 to speak of the rent and not the price. That helps the wording. It does not remove the point, because a rent growing at r drags the price up with it in the long run.

Options. (a) Write the bound on the price at the time of extraction: U ≥ r(t) · P(t₀). This is what the engine does. (b) Keep P(t) and add an assumption that real resource prices have no long-run trend, with a source (Krautkraemer 1998 is where I would look; not yet read). (c) Leave it and note that the theorem's growth assumption excludes a pure Hotelling path.

Recommendation: (a), with (c)'s note as one clause. It makes the definition match the computation.

Row 21. §14.3: "act as if the low rate applies"

  1. Weitzman's result is about the far future. If we are unsure whether the right rate is 1, 2 or 4 percent, then for costs centuries away the 1 percent world dominates the average, because in the other worlds those costs have been discounted to almost nothing.
