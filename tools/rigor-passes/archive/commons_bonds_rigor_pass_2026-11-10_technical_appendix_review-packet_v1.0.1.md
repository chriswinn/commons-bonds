# Part 1. The eight decisions (updated)

Changes since v1.0.0 are marked **CHANGED**. "Yes to all" is still a complete answer.

**1. The name.** Keep "Three Ways of Counting." Add one sentence: two ways estimate what is owed, one reads what
was paid. No rename before the Chapter 6 rebuild.
*Why:* the name is accurate under that gloss, and a rename touches Chapter 6's title and dozens of files.

**2. Where "who bears it" lives. CHANGED.**

- Amend Definition 1.7. The bond counts cost that was **priced with the cost in view** when the deal was made:
  by an instrument that forces payment, by a wage premium for a risk the worker could see, or by a choice made
  knowing the cost. Cost severance is what was borne but never priced.
- Add formal definitions of Cost Severance Damages and of the Restitution Bond as measured, with a split between
  what the extractor paid and what the public paid.
- State in §7 that the gates do not test causation; each case states its own attribution rule.
- **No fifth gate.**
- **The commute stays inside the framework.** I withdrew my advice to treat it as a teaching case outside the domain.
- §12's "cost severance requires distance" becomes: the distance can be geographic, temporal, social or
  **informational**.

*Why the change:* my first wording (the bond counts any cost the beneficiary bears itself) would have set the
commute's gap to zero. That erases the book's second goal, people taking work "with open eyes and honest
accounting." The revised wording gives the right answer in all three test cases: the colony that mines its own
ore knowingly (zero), the commute (positive), and the worker whose wage paid for the danger but not for what he
could not see (positive).
*Caution:* this wording has not been tested against every case in §11, and it makes "what was known at signing" a
fact each case must establish. I will test it in Phase 2 before you ratify the final text.

**3. The McDowell headline.**

- (a) "Method 1" means replacement cost only. The §9 damage audit gets its own name.
- (b) The integral ($525–540 per ton) becomes the headline value. Methods 1 and 3 are cross-checks. Headline
  cost severance falls from about $1,025–1,065 to about $440–490 per ton.
- (c) **CLARIFIED.** Restate the Hotelling Identity as a split of the forward term:
  **RCV − B₂ = (RCV − H) + (H − B₂)**, where H is the Hotelling rent per unit. B₁ and B₂ are not removed or
  replaced. H appears only in §4.

*Why:* the integral is the definition, it is the conservative figure, and it survives if a reviewer rejects
Method 3. The split is a true identity: rent that was earned but not bonded, plus the gap that would remain
even if all of it were.

**4. The multiples.** Headline the figure on a consistent price basis (about 4–13 times on the integral; 8.5–26

*Why:* §11.6 and §11.11 already call the 1960-price ratio an inflation artifact. It is the first arithmetic a
hostile economist checks.

**5. Inversions and gates.** Keep seven inversions; sharpen Institutional (can the rules be enforced?) against

*Why:* §7.1 currently states the single-form test that §15.1.8 names as the rejected alternative.

# Part 2. What the framework adds (my honest view)

**Not new:** the damage totals (McDowell's value is 92% the EPA social cost of carbon); the individual
components; the idea of a bond posted in advance (Costanza & Perrings, 1990); the Method 3 formula, which is a
calibrated construct and should not be presented as a result.

**Added, in my judgment:**

1. **The gap as the object.** Existing work asks what the true cost is. The framework asks what was paid against
   it, and by whom. "Who paid" is the sharpest part.
2. **One ledger for both directions.** Past harm and future foreclosure in one equation, with separate instruments.
3. **The inversion test as an admission rule,** including its ability to say no.
4. **A reserve sized to a per-unit figure.** Real, but not yet shown in the TA.
5. **The Open, Zero and Filled states.** Naming a cost and deliberately leaving it unpriced.
6. **Vocabulary.** "Severed cost" and "accountability gap" compress a paragraph into two words. By your own
   success test this may matter most.

**For decisions not yet made** (your colony question): with one party deciding for itself, the original part of
the framework is silent and the borrowed parts (regret rule, substitutability) do the work. With a second party,
or with a signer who cannot see the cost, the original part applies in full. The framework can say what belongs
in a reserve and who owes it. It cannot yet say how large with much confidence.

# Part 3. New research: long-absence, high-risk work

Full note with every source and link:
`research/citations/long-absence-high-risk-work_literature_2026-11-10.md`.
**Verification level:** abstracts and bibliographic records only. No full texts read. Read each source before
quoting it.

## What the research supports

| Claim | Support | Main sources |
|---|---|---|
| The work is far more deadly than average | Strong | BLS 2023: fishing workers 86.9 deaths per 100,000 against 3.5 for all workers. NIOSH: Bering Sea crab fleet 260 per 100,000 a year (2000–2009); Alaska shellfish 407 (1991–1999). Rates have fallen sharply since. |
| Workers on long rotations report more distress | Good | Parker et al. 2018 (about 3,000 fly-in fly-out workers): 33% high distress against 17%. |
| Separation is the stressor seafarers rank first | Good, mostly cross-sectional | Reviews in *BMC Psychology* 2022, *BMC Public Health* 2022, *International Maritime Health* 2012. Mellbye & Carter 2017 warn the causal evidence is thin. |
| Partners are lonelier and sleep worse on away days; the newly separated and those with young children are hit hardest | Moderate | Taylor et al. 1985; Asare et al. 2023. |
| These jobs damage marriages and children | **Weak or mixed** | Asare et al. 2023: effect "remains unclear." Taylor et al. 1985: effects "have been exaggerated." Reviews found no good evidence of more family breakdown. Military-deployment meta-analyses: small or negligible effects on children. |
| The wage pays for the absence | **Not measured** | The wage literature finds a premium for fatal risk. I found no study testing pay for family separation. |
| A person cannot price it before living it | Supported in theory, contested in evidence | Loewenstein, O'Donoghue & Rabin 2003 (projection bias); Kahneman et al. 1997; L. A. Paul 2014. Note the forecasting literature also finds people overestimate how bad things will feel. |

## What this means for your point

Your instinct is right in structure and too strong in fact. The research does not show that the crabber's or the
rig worker's family is reliably harmed. It shows the cost is **real for some, uneven, hard for one person to
predict, and unmeasured in the wage.**

That is a better fit for the framework than the stronger claim. It is exactly the case the Open state was built
for: a cost admitted to the ledger, named, and deliberately not priced.

## How I would integrate it

**In the TA (short).** Three or four sentences in Definition 1.10, where the information boundary already sits:

> *A cost can be hidden from the person who bears it in three ways. It can be concealed: someone knew and did not
> say. It can be unknowable until lived: a wage premium prices the danger a worker can picture, and research on
> long-rotation work finds the cost of absence real for some families, uneven, and not measured in wages. Or it
> can be knowable but unseen, as when nothing prompts the question. In all three the cost is borne without
> having been priced, and the framework records it: Filled where it can be measured, Open where it cannot.*

Cite BLS and NIOSH for the danger; Parker et al. 2018 and Asare et al. 2023 for the absence; Loewenstein et al.
2003 for the forecasting problem. Add no dollar figure. These are three modes of one shield (the Epistemic
commons), not a new category, so nothing is added to the layer set.

**In the book.** The natural home is **Chapter 1**, beside your commute, because that chapter carries the
reader-facing goal: one question to ask before signing. A single paragraph: the crabber's wage is high because
the sea is dangerous, and everyone can see that; what nobody prices is what six months away does, and the
research says nobody can tell him in advance which family he will be. Then the commute as the small, ordinary
version of the same thing.

**Not in Chapter 5 now.** It is mid-redline, and adding a case would restart the loop.

**What I cannot supply.** The first step missed, the phone call from the boat. That texture has to come from your
own material or from an attributed, citable account. The research gives rates, not scenes.

**Still to source.** The football player. Concealment needs the litigation and settlement record and the
published reporting. I did not research it today and would not write it from memory.

# Part 5. How to finish the chapters

## The problem

Every chapter has its content. What is missing is pull, pacing, clean prose, citations and checked facts. A long
chapter fills most of a session by itself, and the TA fills one alone.

## What I suggest

**1. One unit of work per session, and make the unit a section, not a chapter.**
A session loads a one-page chapter map and one section of 3,000–4,000 words. It never loads the whole chapter and
never the TA. The map carries the through-line: what each section must hand to the next. Chapter 5's
`question-map.md` is already this document; every chapter gets one.

**2. Settle structure before prose, from the map alone.**
You decide order and cuts on one page, in ten minutes. Redrafting before that decision is where rework comes
from. Chapter 5 went through three rounds largely for this reason.

**3. Build a house style sheet from your own redlines.**
Your Chapter 1 redline and today's Chapter 5 redline show what you change: which constructions you cut, how you
end paragraphs, what you will not say. One session turns those into a page of rules with before-and-after
examples. Every later redraft starts from it, so each chapter arrives closer to your voice and your redline
gets shorter. This is the single highest-return step.

**4. Take fact-checking out of the reading.**
A script pulls every number, date, name and quotation from a chapter into a table and checks it against the
ledger and the gate patterns. I read the table, not the chapter. Citations are done once, at the end, through
the bibliography build, not chapter by chapter.

**5. Give you less to read, not more.**
For a one-to-two-hour week: a clean PDF, the ten places I think a reader stops, and no more than seven questions.
You redline those and anything else that catches your ear.

**6. Order the work so nothing is done twice.**

| Order | Work | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Chapter 5 redline, then polish | In hand; on the Darity path |
| 2 | TA Phase 2 (after your eight answers) | Fixes the numbers Chapters 6–9 depend on |
| 3 | Style sheet from the Chapter 1 and 5 redlines | Feeds everything after |
| 4 | Chapters 2, 3, 4 and 10: cleanup pass, then your redline | They need only a redline; short sessions |
| 5 | Chapters 6–9: map, your structure call, section-by-section redraft | Longest; wait for the TA and the style sheet |
| 6 | One citation and cross-reference pass over the whole book | Cheapest done once |

**7. Keep the three-candidate comparison for chapters that need structural change only.**
Two fresh variants plus the current version is expensive in context. It earned its cost on Chapter 5. For
Chapters 2, 3, 4 and 10 a single cleanup pass against the style sheet is enough.

## What this does not solve

It will not make the prose yours. Chapter 5's redraft moves well and still has habits that are mine, the
closing-line rhythm above all. The style sheet narrows that gap. Your hand closes it.
