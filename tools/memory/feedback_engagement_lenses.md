# Engagement lenses (author, 2026-11-09)

**What these are.** Guidelines for writing prose that pulls a reader forward. They are **lenses, not
rules.** Never turn them into targets, counts, thresholds or checker rules. The anchor for that
warning is the 2026-11-09 sentence-length rule: a length target reached the drafter and produced
clipped Ch 5 drafts (see the amendment in `feedback_em_dash_overuse.md`). The only hard rules for
sentence construction remain the author's two: no clause split by an insertion, and no insertion
carrying its own commas.

**The goal, in the author's words:** "an engaging book that people want to read, and with smoothly
flowing prose that compels them to keep reading once they have started," with good grammar.

## The lenses

1. **Open a question before giving answers.** Curiosity comes from noticing a gap between what we
   know and what we want to know. Naming a strangeness ("seemingly unrelated") turns a reader's
   confusion into the question the passage then answers. Prose that answers questions the reader
   hasn't asked yet feels chunky.
2. **Make the reader a participant, not an audience.** An invitation ("Imagine…") gives the reader
   something to do. A report ("This one shows up in…") only asks them to receive it.
3. **Put the strongest thing last,** in the sentence and in the paragraph. Don't spend the end on a
   hedge or an afterthought.
4. **Bring in people and stakes before the catalogue or the abstraction.** A list of equal-weight
   places or nouns has no pull until something the reader cares about is in it.
5. **Let lists build.** A list can open a piece if it is framed as a puzzle and moves toward its
   strongest item, which can open out into an image. Dropping the "and" before the last item can keep
   the momentum. Avoid tacking a clause on after the list closes.
6. **Put the reasoning in the joints.** Words like "because," "so" and "but" carry the reader from
   one sentence to the next. Sentences that only sit side by side make the reader build the bridge,
   and that effort is what chunky feels like.
7. **Pull beats polish, so do both, separately.** Pull carries a reader over small rough spots, but
   polish cannot rescue a passage with no pull. Draft for pull; polish for grammar afterward.

**Candidate lens 8 (Claude's diagnosis, 2026-11-09; awaiting the author's confirmation): every case
enters as a scene, wherever it falls in the chapter.** People and place first, then what is happening to
them, then the system, then the numbers as the answer to the question the scene raised. The anchor is
Ch 5 round 3: the case each version opened on (Baotou in Earned roadmap, Butler in Out and back) was
written as a scene and read well. The case reached mid-chapter was written as a step in the argument
and read flat: it led with the scale or the mechanism, put statistics before people and arrived after a
page of numbers. A scene buys the reader's patience for the explanation that follows; explanation
before any scene spends patience the reader hasn't given yet. Scene mode never means invented detail.
It is an ordering of documented facts. **Process notes stay out of the prose:** sourcing rules ("it
should be rendered strictly within what she wrote"), corrections of claims the reader never made ("the
accurate statement, then, is not…") and announced hedges ("honesty requires a word…") turn the reader
from witness into auditor. Keep the discipline, drop the announcement, and state the narrower claim
plainly.

**A reader's test:** at the end of each paragraph, ask whether you want to know what comes next.
Where the answer is no, that's usually where the chunkiness is. (The author dropped a second half,
"and do you know why," on 2026-11-09: it could push drafting toward explaining or signposting why
the reader should keep reading, which is the announcing problem this file warns against.)

## Safeguard for the grammar and punctuation polish

The author drafts in stream of consciousness and revises several times for grammar; Claude helps
with the grammar and punctuation polish (mechanical only, every change listed). **Check each polish
change against these lenses before making it.** A correct change that removes a passage's pull is
the wrong change; find a grammatical form that keeps the pull instead.

Anchor: Claude's smoothing of the author's Ch 5 opening fixed the list's grammar but put an "and"
back before the last item and swapped "seemingly unrelated" for a wordier phrase, giving back some
of the pull (lenses 1 and 5). The re-revision kept all five strengths and the grammar fixes.

## Worked example (Ch 5 opening, 2026-11-09)

- **Flat (Claude, round 3):** "This one shows up in a Montana mining town, on an oil rig in the Gulf
  of Mexico, … on a pay stub and in a hospital room, and there is a good chance it is also in the house
  where you are reading this." A fact with no question in it, the reader as audience, a hedge in the
  final position, places only, and a list that closes and then trails off.
- **Pulls (author):** "Imagine a pattern that shows up in places seemingly unrelated such as a Montana
  mining town, … in a hospital room with your loved ones sitting as an unannounced and uninvited
  guest." A puzzle, an invitation, the strongest image last, people and stakes, and a list that builds.
- **Pulls, with grammar polished:** "Imagine one that shows up in seemingly unrelated places: a Montana
  mining town, an oil rig in the Gulf of Mexico, a rare-earth plant in Inner Mongolia, a retirement
  account, a pay stub, a hospital room where it sits beside your loved ones like an unannounced and
  uninvited guest."
