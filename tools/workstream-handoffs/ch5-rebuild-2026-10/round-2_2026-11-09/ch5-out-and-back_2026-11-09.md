# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 5: The Accountability Gap

## One body

In the last years of his life, the father of the journalist Katy Butler was given a pacemaker. Butler later published an account of those years. It is a documented case, and what follows stays strictly within what she wrote.

As her account makes plain, the architecture of medical payment points relentlessly in one direction. Medicare pays a doctor very little for a long office visit spent weighing whether an intervention is wise. It pays far more for the procedure itself. By Butler's account, Medicare would have paid her father's doctor $54 for that kind of long conversation. It paid $461 to the surgeon who implanted his pacemaker, on top of a hospital fee of roughly $12,000, nearly half of which went to the company that made the device. The liability system points the same way: performing a procedure protects a clinician against the malpractice exposure that declining it could create.

So the device went in. It kept her father's heart beating reliably through years of the cognitive decline that followed. Had the long conversation been the well-paid act, the patient might have chosen to forgo those years.

Through those years the unpaid work of caregiving fell on an elderly spouse, hour after hour. At seventy-seven, Butler's mother found herself on duty more than eighty hours a week. That work appeared on no ledger, was counted by no system and was reimbursed by nothing.

The surgeon and writer Atul Gawande described the systemic version in *Being Mortal*. The payment system rewards intervention at every decision point. It underfunds the palliative care and the training that would let medicine attend well to the end of a life. Added up, that incentive structure produces the over-treatment of the dying: one family's case repeated across a whole system. No individual clinician has to choose extraction over compassion for the pattern to appear. It comes from adding up the rational responses of decent people to the incentives in front of them, and those incentives are themselves a design choice. Change the payment architecture and the overall behavior changes, without anyone's character changing at all.

Once you have seen a pattern, you start noticing it everywhere. The one in that room is easy to see. Value was drawn from the procedure and the device. A cost came with it: years of decline, and a caregiver's exhausted weeks. And the cost was cut loose from the institutions that profited and fastened onto a household at its most defenseless.

It works like this. A company, a generation or a government draws value from something it depends on but did not produce. It might be a mineral seam, a fishery, a river or the air. It might be the labor of others, the trust between citizens or the future itself. Drawing that value creates a cost. The cost is then cut loose from the people who benefited and pushed onto people with less power, less mobility and less ability to refuse.

The cost comes due later. By then the mine has closed. The company has reorganized. The executives have cashed out. The politicians who made the promises have left office. The cost stays with whoever was left standing where it landed.

The one reliable thing about this pattern is that the bill comes due. It is never cancelled, only deferred and handed to someone else. The only open question is who ends up holding it. Step back from that room, one scale at a time, and the same thing keeps happening.

## A household

The room belongs to a household, and the household is where the cost lands next. Over the 2010s, raising money for medical bills on online fundraising platforms grew from a curiosity into an institution. By 2020, more than a third of the campaigns on the largest such platform were medical. From roughly 2016 to 2020, there were about 438,000 medical campaigns. Together they drew more than $2 billion from some 21 million donations. Yet the outcomes tell the real story. Only about 12 percent of campaigns met their stated goals, and roughly 16 percent received nothing at all. Read the first figure from the other side: nearly nine in ten fell short of what they asked for.

The platform's own chief executive has said it was never designed to be a healthcare financing system. The chief executive has also said it became the default backstop by accident, filling a hole a working safety net would have filled. Medical crowdfunding exists in other wealthy countries too, including Canada, the United Kingdom and Germany. What sets the United States apart is the scale and the role. Peer-reviewed comparative work finds that the country uses medical crowdfunding far more than those comparison countries, as a default substitute for coverage. One example is the analysis by Saleh and colleagues in *JAMA Network Open* in 2020.

There is a further layer, and it is the cruelest, because it shows the informal backstop reproducing the very inequities the formal safety net was meant to reduce. Studies of medical crowdfunding find that campaigns run by people with distinctively Black or Hispanic surnames raise less money and are less likely to meet their goals, even after controlling for place and time. In a 2022 analysis in the *American Journal of Public Health*, researchers including Kenworthy and Igra documented the scale of these campaigns and the structure of who succeeds.

With grim reliability, a system of mutual charity that depends on the size and wealth of a person's social network delivers the least to those whose networks were already thinnest. It sends the most help to those who need it least, and the least to those who need it most. The backstop does not correct the inequity. It launders it.

Beneath the fundraising pages sits a slower pressure. At the Economic Policy Institute, the economists Lawrence Mishel and Josh Bivens have documented how the typical worker's wages came apart from overall productivity in the early 1970s. After that, the economy kept growing more productive while the median wage stalled. Over the same span, healthcare costs grew at several times the rate of general inflation. At bottom, the pressure that lands a family on a fundraising page is the widening gap between those two curves: flat wages below, soaring medical costs above, and households caught in the opening between them.

The political scientist Jacob Hacker named the broader version of this in *The Great Risk Shift*: the long, systematic transfer of economic risk off institutions and onto families. Medical bills that end on a fundraising page are one of its sharpest forms. The same shift will appear again further out, at the scale of a whole national economy, wearing different clothes.

## A town

Widen the view to a town. If your home was built or insulated while W.R. Grace was selling Zonolite, there is a real chance some of it is above your head right now. It is a loose, gray-brown fill that lies quietly between the joists. Millions of American attics still hold it. The fill came from a mine above the town of Libby, in the far northwest corner of Montana.

For roughly twenty-seven years, from 1963 to 1990, W.R. Grace ran that vermiculite mine. Vermiculite is a useful mineral. When heated, it puffs up into a light, fire-resistant material that makes good insulation. Grace sold it under the name Zonolite. The Libby mine was no marginal operation. For much of its life it produced most of the vermiculite used in the United States. It was profitable, and by the measures a company uses to judge itself, it was a success.

The ore was contaminated. Threaded through it was a particularly lethal form of asbestos. The fibers lodge in the lining of the lungs. Decades later they produce cancers and scarring that slowly suffocate a person.

This was not a hidden surprise. Internal company documents later released in litigation show that the contamination was identified early. They also show that workers' exposure was measured throughout the mine's life. The mining continued. Miners carried the dust home on their clothes, into kitchens and onto the laps of children. The company let the town use waste from the mine as fill for the high-school running track and for home gardens. The dust did not stay at the mine. It became the air the town breathed.

The toll is documented, and it is large. Hundreds of Libby residents have died of asbestos-related disease. Thousands more have been diagnosed with the lung damage that marks exposure. The Environmental Protection Agency eventually declared a public-health emergency in Libby, the first it had ever declared under the federal Superfund law. In a town of a few thousand people, the oxygen tank rode in the grocery cart next to the bread. Funerals for the relatively young were not rare. Deaths from asbestos disease in Libby have run at many times the national rate.

Set the company's gains beside the town's losses and the severance becomes visible. Over its life the mine brought in revenue on the order of $100 million. Against that sits a long and still-growing tail of cost. There is medical care for the sick and dying, and the cleanup of a town saturated with fibers. There are legal settlements, lost property values and the monitoring a poisoned population needs.

Pinning one clean ratio to all of this is harder than the round numbers that sometimes circulate suggest. The documented public spending on Libby comes to roughly $900 million to $1 billion, most of it federal Superfund cleanup. Against the mine's revenue, that is already a ratio of about nine or ten to one. And that figure leaves out the deaths and the disease themselves: the years of life lost, the hours of caregiving, the futures closed off. No settlement ever fully priced those. Add even a conservative value for them, and the total plausibly reaches into the low billions.

The honest way to say it is as an order of magnitude built from disclosed parts, not as one authoritative number. A company took in roughly $100 million in revenue. It left behind costs at least ten times greater, borne almost entirely by people who never saw the revenue.

What matters most for the rest of this chapter is not the harm but the architecture that absorbed it. In 2001, with liabilities mounting, Grace declared bankruptcy. The corporation reorganized, and its obligations were channeled into the slow machinery of a bankruptcy trust. Shareholders and executives moved on with their lives. The town could not move on, because the town could not move. The fibers were in its soil and in its people's lungs.

In 2005 the federal government indicted W.R. Grace and several of its senior executives. At the time it was the largest environmental criminal prosecution the country had attempted. In 2009, after a long trial, the defendants were acquitted.

The acquittal is easy to misread. It is not evidence that the harm did not happen. Death certificates, cleanup budgets and the EPA's own emergency declaration document the harm. The acquittal shows something else: criminal law is not built for this kind of mechanism. A prosecution looks for an identifiable act, committed knowingly, at an identifiable moment, by an identifiable person. It is built to catch a decision.

The mechanism at Libby did not live in one decision. It lived in thousands of ordinary operating choices over twenty-seven years, each defensible on its own. None of them was the single provable act the law requires. The law looked for a moment and found none, because there was no moment. There was a process.

The legal scholar Katharina Pistor makes this point with great force in *The Code of Capital*. She argues that capital is not a natural substance. It is a legal artifact, assembled from legal modules: property, collateral, trusts, the corporate form, bankruptcy and the choice of which jurisdiction's law applies. These modules do more than describe wealth. They create and protect it, and they decide who can be reached when something goes wrong.

Seen through Pistor's eyes, the bankruptcy that capped Grace's liability and the acquittal that ended the prosecution were not failures of the system. They were the system working as designed. Those modules let a corporation limit its liability, shed its obligations and dissolve the responsible actor into a process. The same modules make large-scale extraction financeable in the first place. Pistor diagnoses the modules that encode and protect the severance. This book's work is to price what those modules sever.

## A coast

Widen again, to a coastline. On April 20, 2010, the Deepwater Horizon rig suffered a blowout while drilling a well operated by BP in the deep Gulf of Mexico. Eleven workers were killed. Over the next eighty-seven days, before the well was finally capped, roughly 4.9 million barrels of oil poured into the Gulf.

BP had drawn enormous value from the Gulf over the years. The value was real, banked and paid out to shareholders. Then the well blew, and the cost of that value went looking for a place to land.

It landed in every dimension the word severance can carry. One was distance in space. Oil from the Gulf was refined and burned in cities far from the water, while the poison stayed behind in marshes where fishing families had worked the same grounds for generations. Another was distance in time. A quarterly earnings report looks three months ahead, but oil in a marsh and in the tissue of fish lasts for years. A third was ecological. The toxins entered the small organisms at the base of the food web and the larger animals that depend on them. They depressed reproduction for years and rippled out through the whole system. No balance sheet was built to register those costs, because the creatures bearing them cannot file a claim.

What did BP pay against what the Gulf lost? This is the case in which the polluter paid the most. In 2016 BP put its own final estimate of its total cost at about $61.6 billion, and the figure passed $65 billion by 2018 as charges kept accruing. That total covers a 2015 settlement with the federal government and the Gulf states, a 2012 criminal penalty of $4 billion, the cleanup and the litigation. It is the largest payment any polluter makes in this book's cases, and litigation forced it.

It still left costs unpaid. Beyond what BP paid, ongoing damage to the Gulf's ecosystems and fisheries is estimated at another $8 billion to $12 billion. Fifteen years after the spill, Gulf fisheries remain impaired.

And some costs never appeared on any ledger BP was made to balance. Fishing knowledge held in particular families broke where the fishery broke: how to read tides and grounds, passed from grandparents to grandchildren. Communities organized for generations around the water lost the thing they were organized around. None of that had a line on any document BP had to reconcile, which is exactly why it was free to lose.

There is a sharper version of the same point at the border. The slick did not respect national lines, and Mexico pressed claims for damage on its side of the Gulf. BP's settlement with Mexico came to about $25 million. Measured against comparable lengths of damaged coastline, that is smaller by orders of magnitude than the American settlements. The oil did not distinguish between nationalities. The accounting did.

The difference was not where the slick went. It was the legal architecture each set of claimants could use. American claimants could reach for the Oil Pollution Act of 1990, for state tort and environmental law, and for class actions that let many small injuries combine into a force a corporation must answer. Claimants in Mexico had a far weaker set of tools for pressing a claim across a border against a foreign corporation. It was the same oil, the same chemistry and the same fish, with two entirely different prices. The price was set by which legal modules the injured could invoke. Pistor's point is not abstract here. It is the difference between $25 million and tens of billions.

The same coastline story has an older chapter in Alaska. In 1989 the tanker Exxon Valdez ran aground on a reef in Prince William Sound. It spilled millions of gallons of crude into one of the richest cold-water ecosystems on the continent. The legal aftermath ran for the better part of two decades. At the end of it, a very large award of punitive damages was cut on appeal to a fraction of what the jury had set.

Meanwhile the Pacific herring collapsed in the years after the spill and did not recover. The herring were a foundation of that sound and of the fishing economy built on it. The families who depended on them found no way to file for the loss of an entire fishery. A fishery that simply stops returning is not the kind of harm the law is built to compensate. A season that had organized whole families' years stopped arriving, and for most of the decades since, it has stayed gone. That is all this chapter needs from Valdez. Chapter 6 gives it the complete accounting it deserves.

## A supply chain

Widen once more, and the people who pay drop out of sight entirely. The phone in your pocket depends on a set of elements most people never think about. So does the motor of an electric car, and so does a wind turbine. They are the rare earths. They are in the magnets that drive electric motors and turbines, and in the speakers and screens of phones. Wealthy economies treat the devices built from them as ordinary, and increasingly as clean.

China supplies on the order of 60 percent of the world's rare earths. Roughly half of China's own output comes from one place: the Bayan Obo deposit and the processing complex at nearby Baotou, in Inner Mongolia. So the correct statement is not that this one place produces 60 percent of the world's supply. It is that this one place produces a large share of the output of the country that supplies most of the world.

The processing is filthy in a way the finished products are not. Separating the valuable elements from the ore takes large volumes of strong acid. It produces enormous amounts of toxic waste, laced with heavy metals and residual acid. Rare-earth ores are often bound up with thorium and other radioactive elements, so the waste is also mildly radioactive. A conservative accounting puts the wastewater at roughly ten tons for every ton of finished rare-earth oxide, and that is the low end of the published estimates. The waste flows into vast tailings ponds. Residents of nearby villages report raised rates of cancer, respiratory disease and birth defects. Farmland around the plants has gone out of use, because the soil can no longer grow food.

The severance runs in every direction at once. It runs across space: the waste that makes the distant technology possible sits in Inner Mongolia, out of sight of the engineer who designs the device and the household that charges it. It runs across time. The feedstock is paid for now, while the health and environmental costs unfold over generations. For the radioactive waste, they unfold over something closer to geological time. And it runs across the social distance between who gains and who pays. The value flows to shareholders and to consumers in rich countries with strong institutions. The cost settles on rural communities with little political voice and few ways to refuse.

The evidence here needs a word of honesty. Data on health and environmental conditions around Baotou is limited, because China's information environment restricts what can be gathered and published. A careful writer should say so plainly rather than overstate what is known. But limited is not the same as silent. Peer-reviewed work exists, and it deserves specific citation. Zhang and colleagues documented the environmental costs of the rare-earth industry in 2022, and Zhou and Ge did similar work in 2021. Their studies record the waste intensity and the regional burden.

What cannot honestly be stated is a tidy total for the cleanup. A round figure of about $100 billion sometimes attaches to Baotou, but it does not trace to any source that survives checking. The right thing to do with an unsourceable number is to set it aside. What can be said is narrower and defensible. The industry's annual environmental cost has been estimated in the billions. Its revenue is on the order of $10 billion to $12 billion a year. And the share of that revenue spent on remediation has historically been a small fraction of the harm.

There is a reason this case belongs here and not only in a catalog of poisoned places. Rare earths are the book's clearest example of a resource with almost no substitute. Oil can be left in the ground, or its rents can be invested forward, as Norway's fund does. What is drawn down is then at least partly answered by what is built up. Rare earths do not offer that bargain. No fund can rebuild a depleted deposit, because there is no engineered stand-in for the elements at the scale the technologies need. When substitutes run that thin, the framework's verdict sharpens. The severed cost cannot be made good by reinvestment. It can only be prevented or paid. Baotou is where the limit of substitution stops being a parameter in an appendix and becomes a field of tailings ponds.

And then the point the whole book turns on. The person at the end of this supply chain is not a villain. That is not a softening of the case; it is the center of it. The consumer shares in the value the extraction produced, and the entire length of the supply chain separates the consumer from the cost. Private virtue or guilt is beside the point. You could be a saint and still stand at the bright end of this severance. Spatial distance through a global supply chain is not an unfortunate side effect of the arrangement. It is what makes the arrangement livable for those who benefit, because it keeps the people who pay permanently out of view. The mechanism does not need anyone to be cruel. It needs only distance, and it manufactures distance for a living.

## A nation

At the scale of a whole national economy, the alibi fails. Every scale so far has offered the comfortable reader one: someone else's household, someone else's town, someone else's coast, a supply chain half a world long. The financial crisis of 2008 took the alibi away. It struck the whole economy at once and reached into the retirement accounts of the same comfortable readers who had felt safe.

I was one of the people it reached. I was doing well at the time. I worked in sales, which gave me a particular view of the crisis. When the shock hit every company's finances at once, companies stopped spending. Every expenditure not already signed got sent back for review. I watched demand freeze from inside the sales pipeline.

I had also done what the system told someone in my position to do. I had no pension. What I had instead was a 401(k), with a number in it that had been rising slowly. I believed what the system had taught me to believe: with no pensions anymore, that slowly rising number was how I would one day retire. When I opened my 401(k) in the middle of the crisis, the number had been revised to essentially zero. The crash had already wiped out the rest of my investments.

The rest arrived in a pile, the way these things actually arrive. A costly medical emergency hit my own finances. My car broke down, and I replaced it by tapping my retirement savings early, which meant paying the tax penalty that punishes exactly that. The company I worked for failed, and I had to pay for my own move to another city to find work. Owning a home got postponed. Retirement got pushed back by about a decade. Together they reset me to starting over financially.

I do not tell this because mine was the hard case. It was not. Plenty of people lost far more and had far less cushion to lose it from. I tell it because of what it takes away. Until now the cost has landed on someone else, somewhere else. In 2008 it landed in the accounts of millions of people who had saved. "I did everything I was told, and it reached me anyway" closes the distance more completely than "I was already struggling" ever could.

The crisis did not require you to have gambled. It required only that you had saved, in the instrument built for saving. So the real question is not who was reckless. It is how the instrument came to be built so that a single year could erase years of ordinary prudence, and who stood on the far side of that transfer.

The crisis was the meeting of two transfers, set in motion decades apart. The first began quietly in 1978, when section 401(k) of the tax code created the legal scaffolding for a new kind of retirement saving. Over the years that followed, American employers steadily replaced the defined-benefit pension with the defined-contribution account. Under a pension, the company promised a worker a fixed income in retirement and carried the responsibility of funding it. Under the new account, the worker saved into an individual account and carried the outcome. It was sold to workers as freedom and ownership. You would control your own money. You would not be tied to one employer for a pension. You would own capital instead of depending on a promise.

What was not said as loudly was whose freedom it really was. The pension had placed three serious risks on the institution. Market risk meant the investments might fall. Longevity risk meant retirees might live longer than planned. Behavioral risk meant people might save too little or sell at the wrong moment. The new account moved all three risks off the corporate balance sheet and onto the individual. Unlike the institution, the individual cannot pool risk across thousands of lives or hedge a thirty-year horizon. The freedom that mattered most in the transfer was the corporation's freedom from liability. This is the great risk shift from the household scale, now operating across a whole retirement system. For about thirty years it produced no crisis on its own, because no shock had yet come to reveal where the risk now sat.

The second transfer was compressed into a few years, roughly 2001 to 2008. In that window the financial industry built a machine for turning home mortgages into securities. It then bundled and sliced those securities into ever more elaborate instruments. Their complexity was no accident. It was structural and necessary to the business, because only an instrument too complicated to evaluate could be sold at scale to buyers who could not judge what they were buying. Among other places, these instruments were packaged into the very retirement accounts the first transfer had filled with ordinary workers' savings. When the housing market turned and the instruments collapsed in 2008, the losses did not stop at some abstract institution. They landed in those accounts.

Now consider the scale. American household net worth fell by roughly $11 trillion, by the Federal Reserve's own count in its flow-of-funds accounts. In *House of Debt*, the economists Atif Mian and Amir Sufi reconstructed how that loss was distributed. The distribution is the whole point, because the headline figure hides the cruelty in it.

Mian and Sufi show that the losses fell hardest, by a wide margin, on households with the least wealth and the most debt. The mechanism is the arithmetic of leverage. Picture two homeowners. One owns a house outright. The other put down a tenth of the price and borrowed the rest. Now let the price of the house fall by a tenth. The first owner has lost a tenth of the value, a real loss but a survivable one. The second has lost the entire down payment. The equity is gone. The same price drop that dents a wealthy balance sheet wipes out a modest one.

Across the country, families with the thinnest cushions and the heaviest mortgages lost a multiple of what wealthier families lost. They had not behaved worse. Leverage simply passes a price decline through to equity with merciless efficiency. Mian and Sufi also show how differently households respond to such losses. For each dollar of housing wealth lost, households with the least wealth cut their spending by about three times as much as households with the most. That is why a shock concentrated on the indebted poor spread into a general collapse of demand, and a deeper recession for everyone.

Now look at what the system did mobilize, and how fast. The Troubled Asset Relief Program committed $700 billion. In 2011 the Government Accountability Office audited the Federal Reserve's emergency lending. Added up across all its programs, the cumulative total of that lending exceeded $16 trillion. That cumulative figure is the one large 2008 number that is well sourced and can be stated with confidence. Within roughly eighteen months, bonuses at the rescued institutions had resumed.

On the other side of the ledger, there were millions of foreclosure filings in the United States between 2008 and 2012. By CoreLogic's count, about 4.1 million of them ended in completed foreclosures through 2012, with families put out of their homes. The system found trillions of dollars in a matter of days for the institutions at the center of the crisis. The households had absorbed the manufactured complexity in their retirement accounts, and their homes were being taken. For them, it organized nothing of comparable scale or speed. In 2011 the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission concluded that the crisis resulted from human action and inaction. It came from choices, not from forces beyond anyone's control.

It would be too easy to leave the case there, with the contrast stated and the indignation invited. The case is stronger if it survives its best objections, so take the four most serious ones in turn, each at full strength.

The first objection is about agency. Many of the mortgages that defaulted went to borrowers who could not have repaid them under any reasonable assumption, and those borrowers signed the papers. No one held a pen to their hands. This is true, and it should not be waved away. But agency depends on information, and the information around these loans was built to work against the borrower. The people who originated the loans were paid by the volume they wrote, not by whether the loans were ever repaid. That is an engine for writing loans that should not be written. The terms in the marketing often differed from the terms buried in the contract. Adjustable-rate loans were marketed to exactly the borrowers least likely to understand how the rate would reset. The later enforcement record of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau documents the pattern case after case.

The borrower's agency was real. The imbalance of information was the lever that turned that agency against the borrower. That does not erase the borrower's responsibility. It puts it in proportion, beside the responsibility of the people who built the imbalance on purpose.

The second objection is about geography. This objection says the crisis was global, not a story about American policy. European banks were heavily exposed, Asian exporters were hammered by the collapse in demand, and mortgage securities were held across borders. All true. But the global reach of the crisis strengthens the finding about the American mechanism rather than dissolving it. The instruments that spread the damage were mostly originated in the United States. The fee-on-origination model that rewarded volume over quality was an American design. American policy permitted the conflict at the heart of the rating agencies, in which the issuer of a security paid the agency that rated it. That the poison spread worldwide does not make it less American in origin. It makes the origin more consequential.

The third objection is about repayment. TARP was largely repaid. The Treasury's accounting shows substantial recovery, and on some measures even a modest profit. Did the public not get its money back? TARP is simply the wrong unit of account. This chapter is not pricing the government's cash flow on one rescue program. It is pricing the welfare lost by households put out of their homes. It is pricing the lifetime compounding lost by workers whose balances reset to a lower base and never caught up. It is pricing regional economies that shrank and stayed shrunk, and the trust between citizens and institutions that an uneven rescue used up. None of that comes back when TARP is repaid. None of it appears on the accounts the repayment argument cites.

The fourth objection is about reform. The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 was a serious response. It created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the Financial Stability Oversight Council. Through the Volcker Rule it restricted banks' trading for their own accounts, and it imposed tougher capital and liquidity requirements. Was the gap not closed? The honest answer is that Dodd-Frank is best read as an early, partial installment of the accountability this book argues for. That is a real compliment, not a dismissal. But a reform that is partial and reversible has not closed the gap. The Volcker Rule was later relaxed. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau was later weakened. A door pushed partway open can be pushed shut again. That is not the same as a wall rebuilt. Chapter 9 returns to Dodd-Frank as one of the entering wedges, the partial but real beginnings of a fuller architecture.

One more thing about 2008 matters most, because it answers the quiet assumption under all four objections: that the system did only what any system would have had to do. It did not. The scope of a rescue is a choice. A society can choose to rescue both the institutions and the households, and history shows it.

In the 1930s, Sweden faced banking pressure similar to what the United States faced from 1929 to 1933 and again in 2008 and 2009. Sweden paired its bank rescue with substantial relief for households. In 1931 it left the gold standard and let the krona fall, which restored export competitiveness and eased the real burden of household debt. It coordinated the rescue of its banks with the modification of mortgages, so the relief reached households instead of stopping at balance sheets. Through the middle of the decade it ran public-works programs that put income straight into households. And in 1938 the Saltsjöbaden Agreement set the settlement between labor and capital that became the architecture of the Swedish welfare state.

This comparison comes from the comparative literature on banking crises, in the tradition of Charles Kindleberger and Barry Eichengreen. That literature treats it as standing proof that rescuing banks and relieving households are not an either-or. The United States chose otherwise in 2008 and 2009, and similarly again in 2020. The choice was contingent then and remains contingent now. Chapter 9 develops the fuller comparison. What matters here is that the road taken was chosen, not forced.

## Time

Here the distance stops being a matter of space. The next distance is time. The sharpest version of the mechanism hides inside the system Americans treat as the opposite of extraction: the compact between generations.

Social Security is the promise that each working generation will care for the one before it. It was built deliberately to express the idea that a society does not abandon its old. And it genuinely is that. It is not a fraud and it is not a Ponzi scheme. The critique that follows is not aimed at the program. It is aimed at one specific feature of how the program's promises were funded. Hold that distinction firmly. Named carelessly, the mechanism this book traces is exactly the kind of thing that gets turned into a weapon against a program worth defending.

The mechanics are straightforward. Social Security was designed in 1935 as a pay-as-you-go system: today's workers' payroll taxes fund today's retirees' benefits. For decades the system collected more than it paid out, and the surplus was held in special Treasury bonds in a trust fund. The baby-boom generation's arrival at retirement age was not something anyone had to forecast. It was arithmetic. The cohort was already born and already counted, and the years it would begin to retire could be read off a calendar decades ahead.

By the early 2020s the system was paying out more than its payroll taxes brought in. Under current law and projections, the trust fund is expected to run out around 2033. After that, incoming payroll taxes would cover roughly three-quarters of promised benefits, about 77 percent.

Now comes a number that has done real damage to honest discussion of this question, and it has to be handled with discipline. A figure circulates claiming that something like one hundred and eight trillion dollars in compounding returns was lost because the trust-fund surpluses were held in low-yielding Treasury bonds instead of stocks. It is sometimes paraded as the largest cost severance in human history. That figure is dishonest. Naming it as dishonest is part of the work here, because a framework that prices severed costs has no business smuggling in a number it cannot defend.

The hundred-and-eight-trillion figure cannot be rebuilt from its own inputs. It sits roughly two orders of magnitude above the nearest serious estimate. It exceeds the largest size the trust fund ever reached. It exceeds the total income the program has ever taken in. It comes from compounding money that was never actually available to compound, and it should never appear as a real measure of severed cost.

So set it aside and build the honest version from its parts. Over the program's whole life, total contributions come to something on the order of $26 trillion, and total benefits paid come to about the same. Those two enormous flows nearly cancel, because the system was designed for them to. Money came in from workers and went out to retirees, year after year. The money ever actually available to invest was not that gross flow. It was only the running surplus, the reserve that built up when income exceeded payouts. At its peak, that reserve was on the order of $2.7 trillion.

That is the base on which a lost-return estimate can honestly be computed. Compound that surplus at a realistic, disclosed real return on stocks, net of the payouts the system actually had to make, and the lost return lands at a few trillion dollars. Not a hundred trillion. A few. The method matters: which base compounds, at what assumed return, net of which payouts. A number you cannot show your work for is a number you should not assert.

One more figure has to be kept separate, because blurring the two is its own kind of dishonesty. The program's Trustees publish a seventy-five-year actuarial shortfall of roughly $22.6 trillion. That is the projected gap between promised benefits and projected revenue over the long horizon. It is a real and important figure. It is also a different quantity from the lost-return estimate, and the two must never be added into one impressive total. The shortfall is what the program is projected to owe beyond what it will collect. The lost return is what the surplus might have earned. Keep them labeled and keep them apart.

Here is the move that makes Social Security the sharpest case in this chapter. It relocates the severance away from where people instinctively look for it. The instinct says the severance lives in the investment decision, in the choice to hold the reserves in Treasury bonds instead of stocks. It does not. Holding the reserves in Treasury bonds was prudent. A public fund that gambled retirees' security on the stock market would take on exactly the risks a retirement system exists to absorb. The portfolio choice was sound. The severance lives somewhere else. It lives in how the promises were made in the first place, and there is a documented moment where you can watch it happen.

In 1972, Congress passed a large, across-the-board increase in Social Security benefits of about 20 percent. The same legislation created the automatic annual cost-of-living adjustment, which ties benefits to inflation from then on. The increase took effect for September 1972. That meant the larger checks reached beneficiaries in the first week of October, about a month before the November election. The payroll-tax increase meant to pay for the higher benefits did not take effect until after the election.

Put the timing beside the funding and the structure is plain. The reward of a generous promise reached voters before they voted. The cost of the promise was scheduled to land after they had. The indexing method in the 1972 law also turned out to be flawed, and it raised benefits by more than Congress intended. In 1977 Congress corrected it, raising taxes and trimming benefits to do so. By the early 1980s the program was in trouble again, with estimates showing the retirement trust fund running out of money as early as August 1983. Congress passed another rescue that spring, again raising taxes and cutting benefits. Each time, the bill landed on whoever was working, paying in or about to retire when it came due.

I want to be careful about what this shows, because the documented version is strong enough without embellishment. It does not require anyone in 1972 to have privately costed out the deficit of the 2030s and hidden it. It requires only what the record plainly holds. A benefit expansion was timed to an election. Its funding was deferred past that election. It was enacted while the ratio of workers to retirees was favorable, and the later decline of that ratio was a matter of public arithmetic, not secret foresight.

That is the severance. The political reward of the promise was collected when the promise was cheap to make, by people who would be out of office before the bill fully arrived. The funding was pushed onto the workers of later decades, many of whom were not yet born and none of whom could refuse.

There is a contrast that shows the architecture was a choice, not a fate. The 1935 design was one option among several: pay-as-you-go, with surpluses parked in Treasury bonds. Chapter 4 described Norway's sovereign wealth fund, which channels the country's oil and gas rents into a compounding investment fund for future generations instead of spending them as they arrive. The problem is the same: what one generation owes those that follow. The architectural answers differ, and they produce different outcomes. National wealth and demography do not explain the difference. The architecture chosen at a specific moment by specific people does. A fund-based design existed as a live alternative, which means the pay-as-you-go design was selected, not inevitable.

Two objections deserve a full hearing here too. The first is the public-finance objection, and it should be conceded entirely. Holding the reserves in Treasury bonds is exactly what a sound fund manager does. Exposing retirees' core security to the stock market would have added precisely the volatility a retirement system exists to remove. The objection is correct, and it leaves the argument untouched, because the argument operates upstream of the portfolio. It is not a claim about how the surpluses were invested. It is a claim about how the promises were made. A defense aimed at the portfolio does not reach a claim aimed at the making of the promise. The two pass each other.

The second objection is about tractability. This objection says the gap can be closed through ordinary, well-understood adjustments. Congress could raise the payroll-tax rate or lift the cap on taxable wages. It could trim the benefit formula at the top, or raise the eligibility age as people live longer. The arithmetic is conceded. The gap can be closed, and closing it is the responsible thing to do.

But notice what every item on that menu does. Each one moves the obligation onto the people living through the years of deficit: the workers who will pay a higher rate, receive a smaller benefit or work extra years. None of it reaches the generations who collected the political reward for making the promise, most of whom are now beyond the reach of any adjustment. Tractability is not the absence of severance. It is severance arriving exactly on the schedule the calendar set decades ago. That the bill can be paid does not change who is handed it.

## The unborn

Past the living generations lies the farthest distance the mechanism reaches, and no one stands there yet to object. The cost of what is extracted now can be pushed onto people who do not exist. A habitable atmosphere is being spent. Irreplaceable materials are being drawn down. Options are being closed off for people who have no way to refuse. This is the same severance as all the others. The people who pay have simply been moved to another time, the one place from which no claim can ever be filed back.

From here both faces of the cost are visible at once. One is the harm already done, strung back along every scale the chapter has passed through. It includes a mother's eighty-hour weeks, a town's lungs and a coast's marshes. It includes the herring of Prince William Sound and the foreclosed households. The other is the harm not yet done, which fills the view at this farthest distance. On the way back in, the chapter will name an instrument for each.

## The same object, named honestly

From this distance every scale is in view at once: a body, a household, a town, a coast, a supply chain, a nation, a lifetime of promises and the unborn. Now the chapter can say what it has been looking at.

Ask what, exactly, was being drawn on in each case. The answer is more unified than the cases suggest, and naming it is the most important move this chapter makes.

A reef. The atmosphere. A community of fishing families. A body of human labor. A generation not yet born. These look like different kinds of things. Define a commons by its structure instead of its substance, though, and they become one kind of thing. Structurally, a commons is anything people depend on and share that was not produced as a commodity for sale. Nobody manufactured a reef; it was inherited. Nobody built the atmosphere to specification. A community's knowledge and trust were never made to be sold. The care that raises the next generation was never produced as a market good. And a future generation is the ultimate thing inherited rather than made.

By that definition, the reef and the atmosphere and the community and the unborn are the same object. Each is a commons whose very abundance hid the fact that drawing on it had a cost. One apparatus can price all of them. It prices forward, against a future being foreclosed. It prices backward, against extraction from communities that has already happened.

Putting a price on any of these invites a serious objection, and the philosopher Michael Sandel has made it as well as anyone in *What Money Can't Buy*. Sandel warns that to price a sacred landscape, a community or the unborn is to launder the priceless into a transaction. It concedes the extractor's frame, in which everything is interchangeable and has a number. Worse, a price can make a harm legitimate. A violation becomes a fee, something you may do as long as you pay. That worry has to be answered here, because no later chapter answers it.

The answer turns on what the alternative to pricing actually is. The choice is not between a sacred thing left unpriced and a sacred thing reduced to a number. It is a choice between two numbers, because the thing already has a number, and the number is zero. Zero is what the extractive accounting already assigned to the reef, the community and the future. Zero is the number that made the extraction profitable. A reef destroyed at a cost of zero is exactly how the extraction got financed.

The framework refuses that zero. It insists the cost was never zero, and it sets out to say what the cost actually is. Pricing something in order to sell it and pricing something in order to reserve it are different acts. Pricing to sell turns a forest into board feet. Pricing to reserve names what the forest is worth, so its destruction can no longer be booked as free. They are opposite operations that happen to share a word.

This refusal of the zero has a direct ancestor. In 1944 Karl Polanyi published *The Great Transformation*, built around the idea of the fictitious commodity. Polanyi argued that the market treats labor, land and money as if they were produced for sale, when none of them was. Labor is human life. Land is nature. Money is a token of social trust. Running a society as though these were ordinary goods does violence to them, and to the people and places they are.

The definition of a commons used here descends from that thesis. So does this chapter's test: imagine the commons gone, and ask what cost appears. Polanyi's deepest idea was the double movement. A society that has something turned into a market good out from under it pushes back, and ties that thing to social obligation again. For Polanyi, the double movement was a historical tendency he could describe. Here it becomes an instrument.

The closest living ally for this argument is Nancy Fraser, and her work repays a debt this book took on several chapters ago. Fraser distinguishes exploitation from expropriation. Exploitation takes from workers through the wage, through a contract, however unequal. Expropriation takes without even a contract. It is capital's reliance on unpaid care work, the labor that keeps a society going. It is also capital's reliance on nature's resources and sinks, which sit outside the wage relation entirely and are simply taken. Fraser maps this ground in her essay "Behind Marx's Hidden Abode" and in her book *Cannibal Capitalism*.

The reef taken without a contract, the community's knowledge taken without a contract, the caregiver's labor taken without a contract: these are expropriation in Fraser's exact sense. So are the options of a future generation, which can never be party to any contract at all. Chapter 2 left a thread loose in its discussion of where the word severance comes from. This is where that thread ties off, and the name it was waiting for is Fraser's.

There is a caution to state honestly, because it answers the sharpest hostile reading of the whole project. A critic might say that pricing the commons is financialization by another route, with the commons repackaged as a new asset class for someone to trade. Polanyi and Fraser would not endorse putting a price on nature, and this book does not claim they would. They refused to treat nature as an asset. The framework stands inside that refusal. Accounting for a cost is not the same as creating an asset to trade.

Katharina Pistor, whose account of legal modules made sense of the Libby bankruptcy, supplies the test. She names the legal attributes that turn an ordinary asset into capital: priority, durability, universality and convertibility. Priority lets a claim rank ahead of others. Durability lets it hold that rank over time. Universality makes it binding on the whole world. Convertibility lets its holder cash it back into state money.

An accountability bond is built to lack the two attributes that let capital circulate. It is not convertible: it cannot be redeemed for money, or for any claim except discharging the specific liability it records. And it is not transferable: it cannot be sold, traded or repackaged into a security. A debt that can only be paid, never traded, is the opposite of a coded asset. That is the difference between creating an asset and accounting for a cost, shown in the code's own terms.

The most forceful living version of the financialization worry belongs to Brett Christophers. His work traces how a financialized economy misprices whatever it turns into a tradable asset. In principle, the answer to him is the one just given. A later chapter takes up his specific argument in full.

## The pattern, named

Set the cases side by side and the same skeleton shows through each one. Something shared was drawn on as if it were free: a mineral seam, a coastline, a fishery, a supply chain, the nation's savings, a promise between generations, a dying body. Drawing on it produced a real cost. Then the cost was cut loose from the people who gained and fastened to people who could not refuse it.

A word about the word itself, because it has a second meaning that can mislead. Severance is also what a company calls the check it writes when it lets a worker go. A reader who hears that meaning first might think the term smuggles in the commodified view of labor this book means to resist. It does the opposite. Both meanings share a root because both name a cut: the worker cut loose from the wage, the cost cut loose from the party who caused it. The book keeps the word because the cut is the thing. A gentler word would do in language what the accounting does in fact. It would make the severing disappear.

The party that benefited escapes under a handful of recurring covers. Distance is one: the Baotou tailings sit half a world away from the people charging their phones. Time is another: a 1972 Congress collected the reward for a promise whose bill falls due in the 2030s. There is the gap in what each side knows, between the company that measured the asbestos and the family that breathed it. There is the gap in who can leave, since capital moves and a poisoned town cannot. There is the way a cost becomes routine, until a dying fishery or a family's bankruptcy turns into background noise. And there is the look of abundance. Cheap insulation, cheap electronics and a rising retirement balance all make a real cost look like none at all. Chapter 7 names the full set precisely. Here they only show how the escape is managed.

Call the roll of who absorbed the cost. A town that could not move. Fishing communities that could not relocate. Villages with no exit. Foreclosed households. Future taxpayers who cannot opt out of an obligation they inherit. Families whose fundraising pages fall short, and an elderly spouse whose labor appears on no ledger. In every case the beneficiary departed. The company went bankrupt, the politicians left office, and the generation that made the promise grew old. The cost stayed with whoever could not escape it.

Stated without a villain, the claim is this. These cases do not show a system failing. They show a system doing what it was built to do. No one needs to intend harm. The structures reward whoever draws the value, and they push the cost onto whoever is least able to resist.

That claim is not new, and credit belongs where it is due. K. William Kapp argued in 1950, in *The Social Costs of Private Enterprise*, that shifting costs onto third parties is a routine feature of private enterprise rather than an occasional market failure. The traditions of political ecology and environmental justice that followed documented who those third parties reliably turn out to be. In the work of scholars such as Joan Martínez-Alier and Robert Bullard, they are the poor, the rural and the politically voiceless. The diagnosis is theirs.

What this book adds is narrower, and it should be stated plainly. It puts a price on the commons drawn down by a single extraction, and it posts that price as a reserve before the cost comes due. Existing tools come close without doing this. Natural-resource damage assessment, the method used to assess the Gulf after Deepwater Horizon, works only after the harm has happened. In 1990 Robert Costanza and Charles Perrings proposed posting a bond in advance, but against the worst damage that could possibly occur. The framework posts against the specific cost each extraction severs. The diagnosis is old. The contribution is the price.

## Who shaped the system?

The framework so far has left one question aside, and it is worth bringing back on purpose, because it complements everything above. The apparatus of this book accounts for who bore the cost. That is the ledger. A separate question sits beside it: who shaped the architecture that sent the cost where it went? The ledger shows where the cost landed. It does not show who built the chute it slid down.

A rigorous toolkit for that second question already exists, and it would be a mistake of pride not to use it. James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock built the Public Choice tradition to analyze how concentrated interests shape institutions to serve themselves at a diffuse cost to everyone else. Tullock's analysis of rent-seeking is the engine: spending real resources to capture a privilege rather than to create value. Mancur Olson described the underlying imbalance in 1965. Concentrated interests organize, diffuse interests do not, and the rules drift toward whoever shows up.

Where the people who extract value and the people who bear the cost are different populations, the chain runs cleanly. You identify the interests that shaped the institutions. You trace the lobbying and the coalitions that produced the design. Then you ask whom the design serves and whom it costs. Run that chain across these cases and a roster appears.

At Libby, internal company documents released in litigation show lobbying and an effort to delay regulation of asbestos. In the Gulf, offshore drilling was overseen by the Minerals Management Service, a textbook captured regulator. Its staff had accepted gifts and travel from the industry, and people moved back and forth between the agency and the companies it oversaw. After the blowout the agency was broken up and reorganized. The Interior Department's own inspector general had reported on the capture before the disaster, not only after it.

In 2008, the deregulation that allowed the crisis had three main parts. The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999 removed the wall between commercial and investment banking. The Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000 exempted derivatives from regulation. And federal rulings pushed aside state consumer protections. Financial-industry lobbying shaped all three, as the Center for Responsive Politics and congressional hearings documented at the time.

In healthcare, the pharmaceutical industry has consistently led American industries in lobbying spending. For decades Medicare was barred from negotiating drug prices, a ban only recently modified. It is as clean a rent-seeking outcome as the literature contains. Chapter 8 adds the coal economy of McDowell County, West Virginia, where an industry spent generations shaping a state's laws and regulations to its own advantage.

The chain does not run cleanly everywhere, and it is more honest to say so than to force it. At Baotou, global consumer demand shapes the extraction as much as any lobby does, and the actors blur. In Social Security, the people who will bear the future cost were partly the payers of an earlier era. That dynamic across time does not reduce to a roster of interests. Where the chain blurs, the structural account of who bore the cost carries the weight, because it does not depend on finding a lobby.

How this toolkit is used matters, and it is easy to misread. This book draws on the rent-seeking vocabulary. It does not endorse Buchanan's larger political project, much of which points in directions this book does not follow. The tools belong to the toolkit, not to the opposing camp. The framework's ledger of who bore the cost is exactly what Public Choice's analysis of who shaped the architecture needs, and neither swallows the other. Readers who want the architecture side developed in full should read Buchanan, Tullock and their successors directly. They will find that the rent-seeking vocabulary survives many readings of Buchanan's broader commitments.

This is a deliberate invitation to readers at the American Enterprise Institute, Cato, Mercatus and George Mason University, where the Public Choice tradition is most at home. The ledger this book builds is the natural complement to the rent-seeking analysis they already do. The two halves are stronger joined. Chapter 9 develops the complement at greater length.

## Restitution and foreclosure

Now the view comes back in, toward the room where the chapter began, and it can name what honest accounting would require.

The cases raise one question, and it turns the chapter from diagnosis to prescription. If all of this is one mechanism, what would honest accounting for it require? The framework answers with a pair of instruments, named here for the first time and built out in later chapters. Chapter 6 supplies the method, and Chapter 9 takes up the politics. The task here is only to name the two instruments and the lineage they stand on.

The accountability gap has two faces in time. One is harm already done: the lungs of Libby, the marshes of the Gulf, the herring of Prince William Sound, the foreclosed households. The other is harm not yet done. That is the cost current extraction is foreclosing right now, for people who do not yet exist. A habitable atmosphere is being spent. Irreplaceable materials are being drawn down. Options are being closed off. An architecture that addresses one face and ignores the other addresses half the problem.

So the framework proposes two instruments. The Restitution Bond discharges harm already done. It looks backward, and it is owed to particular people and communities who exist and have been hurt. The Foreclosure Bond discharges the cost current extraction imposes on the future. It looks forward, and it protects outcomes for people who do not exist yet. Together they make up the aggregate accountability bond the framework requires.

Each instrument is measured against its own face of the cost. The Restitution Bond is measured against damage already done. The Foreclosure Bond is measured against the remaining value of the commons, minus whatever has already been posted to protect it.

Why call them bonds? The word was chosen over the alternatives on purpose. A tax goes into general revenue, with nothing tying the amount to any specific future obligation. A fee buys permission to proceed; it is a toll, not a remedy. A fund pools money without discharging any particular liability. A bond differs from all three. It is posted before the full cost is borne. It carries forward across the long tail of that cost. And it discharges something observable: a specific posted amount set against a specific identified liability.

The structure already exists in the world, which is part of why the word is honest. Mining companies post reclamation bonds under the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977, held against the cost of restoring the land. Oil and gas companies post decommissioning bonds against the future cost of closing a well. Norway's sovereign fund works like a bond at national scale, with capital held against future need. The economics of reparations treats a historical injustice as a documented debt to be discharged. And the civic-republican obligation to future generations finds its natural accounting form in a bond. The term gathers all of these together.

A note is owed on why the backward instrument is called Restitution when its main source is the reparations tradition. In the old legal sense of *restitutio in integrum*, restitution means restoring what was taken. It is a broader and older term, wide enough to cover every kind of cost severance: environmental damage, occupational disease, a destroyed community, a lost ecosystem. The reparations tradition is the framework's primary source for backward-looking accountability. That tradition runs through Ta-Nehisi Coates, William Darity Jr. and A. Kirsten Mullen, Darrick Hamilton and Dalton Conley. Calling the instrument the Reparations Bond would narrow it to one historical application. It would also make the method's use elsewhere read as a loose analogy rather than the same discipline applied directly.

Other names fail for their own reasons. Restoration assumes the harm can be physically undone, and many of these harms cannot. Indemnification routes the obligation through insurance, a frame the framework does not use. Damages collapses the bond into the measurement and loses the advance posting that is the whole point. So the framework joins the reparations tradition rather than rebranding it.

Darity and Mullen describe what reparations require in three parts, which they call ARC: acknowledgment, redress and closure. Redress is the material part, the payment that repairs the harm. The Restitution Bond is the instrument that carries out that material part of redress. It works inside their framework and uses their language, rather than competing with it.

The forward instrument's name does similar work. A reader who hears Foreclosure thinks first of 2008, of a family barred from its home. That association is a continuity to claim, not a coincidence to apologize for. The word comes from the old *forclore*, to bar from, and it describes both events exactly. A mortgage foreclosure bars a household from its home through a legal architecture that lets the barring happen at scale. The Foreclosure Bond addresses future generations barred from what they should inherit: a habitable atmosphere, intact ecosystems, irreplaceable materials. The mechanism is the same, and only the scale differs, a household in one case and a generation in the other.

The alternatives miss what matters. "Future generations" reads as soft philanthropy. "Sustainability" has been absorbed into corporate branding. "Reclamation" is already a narrower legal term. "Conservation" and "intergenerational" both miss the specific act of barring that the word foreclosure names.

The lineage behind the instruments can now be placed. Pistor's *Code of Capital* supplies the legal half of the diagnosis. Pistor shows how law encodes and protects the severance, and the framework prices what the law severs. For the forward instrument's moral footing, Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) supplies the standing of future people. It offers a way to take obligations to people not yet born seriously, at the level of outcomes rather than particular identities. The framework leans on Parfit without claiming to have solved the non-identity problem he famously left open. That problem is named as a standing limit, not quietly defeated.

The civic-republican tradition of Philip Pettit's *Republicanism* (1997) and Quentin Skinner's *Liberty Before Liberalism* (1998) adds the idea of freedom as non-domination. A society that extracts in ways that subject others to domination breaks that commitment, whatever contract it has or has not broken. The framework does not ask readers to accept civic-republican philosophy as the only valid frame. Non-domination does its strongest work on the harms this chapter documents within a single generation. The forward instrument leans mainly on the ethics of obligation across generations: Parfit's account of standing, together with the ethics of discounting taken up in Chapter 6. The republican reading is a complement there, not the frame that carries the weight.

The environmental-economics lineage runs through John Hartwick's rule of 1977. Rents from a depleting resource should be invested in reproducible capital, so that consumption need not fall as the resource runs out. Norway's fund is the cleanest real-world application. These are tools for preventing foreclosure rather than for repairing past harm. Keeping that distinction clear keeps the two faces of the bond separate.

The backward instrument's lineage runs through the reparations tradition. In 2014 Ta-Nehisi Coates published "The Case for Reparations" in *The Atlantic*. It supplied the shape of the argument: name the mechanism, name the harm, name the debt. The framework extends that shape to communities built around extraction. The economics runs through Darity and Mullen's *From Here to Equality* (2020), the field's contemporary landmark and this framework's primary source.

It runs as well through *Umbrellas Don't Make It Rain* (2015), by Darrick Hamilton, William Darity Jr., Anne Price, Vishnu Sridharan and Rebecca Tippett. Their matched comparisons found that the structural variable is wealth, not behavior. Black households at the highest level of education hold less wealth than white households at lower levels. And the lineage reaches back to Dalton Conley's *Being Black, Living in the Red* (1999), an earlier statement that wealth is the variable that matters.

The backward instrument applies the same matched-comparison method to communities built on extraction. McDowell County's collapse in wealth can be measured against comparable Appalachian counties that did not depend on coal. Libby's health costs can be measured against similar towns that never had a Grace mine. Appalachia's opioid deaths can be measured against comparable counties where Purdue's documented marketing did not concentrate. Chapter 8 runs that third comparison in full.

The backward lineage has a second branch, and it runs forward as well, like the framework itself. A current program in climate economics already prices the atmosphere as a debt in both directions. Andrew Fanning and Jason Hickel have calculated compensation for the appropriation of the atmosphere. The Caribbean Community (CARICOM) has advanced a plan for reparatory justice. And the Loss and Damage Fund was established at the 2022 United Nations climate conference and put into operation the following year. All three treat the atmosphere as a commons overused by some and foreclosed for others. The debt runs backward to past emissions and forward to the loss of a stable climate. It is priced on the same social cost of carbon this book uses.

The framework concedes the priority. That program reached the two-way atmospheric commons first. It argues the debt at the scale such debts are usually argued: the nation, and who owes whom across a global carbon budget. What the framework adds is not the idea but the grain. It prices a single extraction event rather than a national balance. And it reserves against that event, rather than netting one nation's account against another's. The atmospheric-debt program is the macro accounting of the same commons the framework meters one ton at a time.

A boundary has to be drawn here, honestly and briefly. The same apparatus runs in reverse, and the most developed backward work in the field is Darity and Mullen's pricing of the racial wealth gap and the gap in life expectancy. Both are measurable legacy effects of historical injustice. McDowell County's own gap in life expectancy is the coal-extraction version of the same method.

One question at the edge of this work remains genuinely unresolved, and pretending otherwise would be the kind of overclaiming this book refuses. How should coerced labor itself be priced, as distinct from its downstream legacy effects? That question remains open in the economics of reparations, and it lies outside this book's empirical scope. The framework reaches coerced cases through effects that can be measured: wealth gaps, gaps in life expectancy, broken transmission of knowledge, trauma passed down through generations. It defers the direct pricing of coercion as an open question, one the field is already carrying forward. The dispossession of Indigenous land sits in the same deferred territory, along with the cultural knowledge that colonization severed. The method can reach them in principle. The computing belongs to later work, not to this book.

Where does bond-posting actually stand across American extraction today? Far below the remaining value of the commons it is meant to protect. The Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Research Service put the federal black-lung program's payouts at roughly $44 billion through 2009, the most recent authoritative total. That is a real number and a conservative floor for the program's full run. Yet its trust fund still carried $5.1 billion in debt as of September 2024.

The deeper finding is not that the instrument is missing. It exists at scale, and it is partly assigned to the wrong party. When the coal excise tax falls short, the difference is borrowed from the federal general fund. That means it comes from the public rather than from the extraction that severed the cost. Reclamation bonds posted under the 1977 law fall several billion dollars short of the reclamation they are supposed to guarantee. And no Foreclosure Bond at all stands against the obligation Social Security carries across generations.

This is the accountability gap, stated as a measurable fact rather than a metaphor. The bond posted is a small fraction of the cost severed. Chapter 6 supplies the method for measuring the gap, and Chapter 9 takes up the policy of closing it.

One discipline closes this out, because it guards against a misreading the whole argument invites. What is owed is a separate question from what any society is willing or able to collect today. The method applies whether or not anyone is ready to post the bond. Its contribution is accounting, not advocacy. What the bond would have to be, if the severance were honestly counted, has an answer in any political weather. How to move from today's near-empty bonds toward the real value of the commons is a political question. It belongs to Chapter 9, which treats it as open rather than pretending to legislate a single answer.

## Back to one body

Come back, finally, to the room where this chapter began. A pacemaker kept a heart beating through years of decline. At seventy-seven, a mother kept watch more than eighty hours a week, and no one kept a ledger of it. Nothing in that room has changed since the first page. What has changed is that you have now seen the same thing at every other scale.

The small severance in that room is the same severance that poisoned a town, fouled a coast, scarred the villages around Baotou, reset a national economy and mortgaged the atmosphere of the unborn. It is not an analogy. It is one mechanism, seen at its smallest. In the room, the person left holding the bill was a woman of seventy-seven. In the atmosphere, it is everyone who will ever be born.

The question the chapter leaves is not whether cost severance is real. Every scale settles that. The question is what it would take to post the bond before the cost is severed, rather than chasing it afterward, once the beneficiary has gone. The rest of this book is built to answer it.
