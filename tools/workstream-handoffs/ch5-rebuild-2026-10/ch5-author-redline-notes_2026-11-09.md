## What carries into the redraft

- **Opening: show, then name.** Open on a concrete scene and state the pattern plainly *after* the
  reader has something to hold (Ch 1's model: "The first thing he did in any new city was find the
  highest place in it and climb."). Give each candidate a **different** opening so the opening is
  tested too. A opens in Butler's father's room; the new B opens on its own spine's first scene.
- **One-pass readability** under the revised punctuation rule (pending the rule rewrite).
- **No repeated image within a page;** concrete verbs over neutral ones; hedges only where accuracy
  needs them.
- **"did not produce"** gets fixed once the author confirms what the "?" meant.
- **"Scatter" is retired** as a word and as a structure.
- **Mark 5 caution:** keep the verb concrete without implying bad faith (e.g. "cashed out"), so it
  doesn't cut against the chapter's no-villain reading. Author's call.

## Author's opening direction (2026-11-09, chat)

Author's proposed first sentence (verbatim, offered as "something like…"):

> "Once you have seen a pattern, you start noticing it everywhere. Especially if that pattern is
> repeated again and again throughout society & human history."

**Why it works:** leads with the condition, so no clause is interrupted; opens on the reader's own
experience instead of an abstract thesis; it's the author's voice. **Use it as the opening direction
in the redraft.** Final wording at the author's redline.

Tightenings Claude offered (not ratified): (1) "…everywhere, especially one that repeats across a
whole society and down through its history." (2) "…everywhere. Especially one that repeats across an
entire society, generation after generation." (3) "Once you see a pattern, you start noticing it
everywhere. This one really is everywhere." (Claude's lean: #3, which hands off to the first scene.)
Caution: "throughout human history" outruns the chapter's evidence (cases run 1960s–now).

## Round 3 read (author, 2026-11-09)

- **Roadmap first is out.** The opening list "landed flat": a long, seemingly disconnected catalogue
  presented with no reason to keep reading and no understanding of what the reader was looking at, so
  it didn't grab attention. **Corrected by the author the same day:** the list itself wasn't the problem.
  An opening list works when it is **framed to raise a question** and **builds toward something
  personal**; an unframed catalogue lands flat. Don't turn this into a ban on opening lists.
- Author's example of a list that pulls the reader in:
  > "Once you have noticed a pattern, you start seeing it everywhere it repeats. Imagine a pattern that
  > shows up in places seemingly unrelated such as a Montana mining town, on an oil rig in the Gulf of
  > Mexico, at a rare-earth plant in Inner Mongolia, in a retirement account, on a pay stub, in a
  > hospital room with your loved ones sitting as an unannounced and uninvited guest."

  Why it works: "seemingly unrelated" turns the list into a puzzle (what could connect these?);
  "Imagine" invites the reader in; and the last item turns from places to the reader's own family,
  with the pattern as an uninvited guest. Candidate opening material for whichever version wins.
- Author is re-reading **Out and back** and **Earned roadmap** to decide which grabs attention most.

## Baotou vs Butler (author's observation, Claude's diagnosis, 2026-11-09)

**Author:** the Baotou and Butler scenes read dramatically differently between Out and back and Earned
roadmap. In one version Baotou reads flat; in the other, Butler does.

**Claude's reading (confirmed by the author the same day):** each version's *opening* case reads well and its
*mid-chapter* case reads flat. So Baotou is flat in Out and back and Butler is flat in Earned roadmap.
The facts are the same; the order differs.

1. **First sentence.** Earned's Baotou opens on homes beside waste ponds. Out and back's Baotou opens
   "the people who pay drop out of sight entirely," then defines rare earths. Earned's Butler opens "the
   mechanism can be seen in a single family," which makes the family an exhibit.
2. **People before numbers.** Earned's Baotou paragraph ends on illness and soil that won't grow food.
   Out and back's runs phone → definition → 60 percent → a correction → chemistry, and the villagers
   arrive in the last two sentences of the third paragraph. In both versions Butler's payment figures
   ($54 / $461 / ~$12,000) come before the father's decline and the mother's 80-hour weeks, so the
   numbers answer a question the reader hasn't been given yet.
3. **Process notes in the prose.** "It should be rendered strictly within what she wrote, with nothing
   added" (Butler's second sentence, both versions); "The accurate statement, then, is not…" (corrects a
   claim the reader never made); "Honesty requires a word…" / "that should be said plainly" (announced
   hedges).
4. **What comes right before.** Earned's Butler arrives after a page of crowdfunding statistics,
   Mishel–Bivens and Hacker, under a heading that promises a hospital room.
5. **A scene buys patience.** Earned's Baotou section is as statistic-heavy as Out and back's (same 60
   percent passage, same evidence paragraph), but it follows the scene the reader has just seen.

**Carries into the pull pass:** write every case as a scene first (people → what happens to them →
system → numbers); take the process notes out of the prose; in Earned's "A hospital room," consider
opening on Butler's father and letting the crowdfunding evidence show it is not one family. Captured as
candidate lens 8 in `tools/memory/feedback_engagement_lenses.md`.

**Author's follow-up (2026-11-09):** confirmed the direction (Baotou flat in Out and back, Butler flat in
Earned roadmap). "There has to be a way to write it so they aren't so flat without making [them] so
vivid." Run with what is working; the earlier versions stay in git. A rule that every case in the book
enters as a scene is probably too broad and could read as contrived in a book that introduces a framework.
**Result:** lens 8 narrowed to "name who pays before explaining how" (scene, anchor sentence or straight
argument, chosen per passage), plus no process notes in prose. The pull pass leaves the two working
openings alone apart from process notes and fixes the two flat cases by order and presence, not color.

**Author's refinement (2026-11-09):** start with one scene, then another only "when and where it makes
sense and makes an impact… where it's earned." The Baotou sentence works because it "brings a personal
connection to a very distant cost that the reader has likely never thought of." Option: start at the
village, then make the connection to the phone in the reader's (or writer's) pocket. Lens 8 updated to
match: one opening scene, earned scenes only, and a bridge from the distant cost to the reader's life
where the connection is real.
