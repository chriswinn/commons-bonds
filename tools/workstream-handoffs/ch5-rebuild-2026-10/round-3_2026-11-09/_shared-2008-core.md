The crisis was the meeting of two transfers set in motion decades apart. The first began quietly in 1978, when section 401(k) of the tax code created the legal scaffolding for a new kind of retirement saving. Over the following years, American employers steadily replaced the defined-benefit pension with the defined-contribution account. Under a pension, the company promised a fixed income in retirement and carried the responsibility for funding it; under the new account, the worker saved into an individual account and carried the outcome. It was sold as freedom and ownership. You would control your own money, you wouldn't be tied to one employer for your pension, and you would own capital instead of depending on a promise.

What was said less loudly was whose freedom it really was. A pension placed three serious risks on the institution: market risk, the chance that investments would fall; longevity risk, the chance that retirees would live longer than planned; and behavioral risk, the chance that people would save too little or sell at the wrong moment. The new account moved all three off the corporate balance sheet and onto individuals. Unlike institutions, individuals cannot pool risk across thousands of lives or hedge a thirty-year horizon, so the freedom that mattered most in the transfer was the corporation's freedom from liability. For about thirty years the transfer produced no crisis on its own, because no shock had yet come along to reveal where the risk now sat.

The second transfer was compressed into a few years, roughly 2001 to 2008. In that window the financial industry built a machine for turning home mortgages into securities and then bundling and slicing those securities into ever more elaborate instruments. Their complexity wasn't an accident. It was structural and necessary to the business, because only an instrument too complicated to evaluate could be sold at scale to buyers who couldn't judge what they were buying. Among other places, those instruments ended up in the very retirement accounts the first transfer had filled with ordinary workers' savings. When the housing market turned and the instruments collapsed in 2008, the losses didn't stop at some abstract institution. They landed in those accounts.

Consider the scale. By the Federal Reserve's own count in its flow-of-funds accounts, American household net worth fell by roughly $11 trillion. In *House of Debt*, the economists Atif Mian and Amir Sufi reconstructed how that loss was distributed, and the distribution is the whole point, because the headline number hides the cruelty in it. The losses fell hardest, and by a wide margin, on the households with the least wealth and the most debt.

The arithmetic of leverage explains why. Picture two homeowners, one who owns a house outright and one who put down a tenth of the price and borrowed the rest. If the price of the house falls by a tenth, the first owner has lost a tenth of its value, which is a real loss but a survivable one. The second owner has lost the entire down payment, and the equity is gone. The same drop in price that dents a wealthy balance sheet wipes out a modest one. Across the country, families with the thinnest cushions and the heaviest mortgages lost a multiple of what wealthier families lost, not because they behaved worse but because leverage passes a decline in price straight through to equity. Mian and Sufi also show how differently households respond to such losses: for each dollar of housing wealth lost, the households with the least wealth cut their spending by about three times as much as those with the most. That is how a shock concentrated on the indebted poor spread into a general collapse of demand and a deeper recession for everyone.

Now look at what the system did manage to mobilize, and how fast. The Troubled Asset Relief Program committed $700 billion. When the Government Accountability Office audited the Federal Reserve's emergency lending in 2011, the cumulative total across all of its programs exceeded $16 trillion, and that cumulative figure is the one large number from 2008 that is well sourced enough to state with confidence. Within roughly eighteen months, bonuses at the rescued institutions had resumed.

On the other side of the ledger, there were millions of foreclosure filings in the United States between 2008 and 2012, and by CoreLogic's count about 4.1 million of them ended in completed foreclosures, with families put out of their homes. The system found trillions of dollars in a matter of days for the institutions at the center of the crisis. The households that had absorbed the manufactured complexity in their retirement accounts were now losing their homes, and for them the system organized nothing of comparable scale or speed. In 2011 the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission concluded that the crisis had resulted from human action and inaction, from choices rather than forces beyond anyone's control.

It would be too easy to leave the case there, with the contrast stated and the indignation invited. The case is stronger if it survives its best objections, so it is worth taking the four most serious ones in turn, each at full strength.

The first objection concerns agency. Many of the mortgages that defaulted went to borrowers who could not have repaid them under any reasonable assumption, and those borrowers signed the papers; no one held a pen to their hands. That is true, and it shouldn't be waved away. But agency depends on information, and the information surrounding these loans was built to work against the borrower. The people who originated the loans were paid according to the volume they wrote, not according to whether the loans were ever repaid, which is an engine for writing loans that shouldn't be written. The terms in the marketing often differed from the terms buried in the contract, and adjustable-rate loans were marketed to exactly the borrowers least likely to understand how the rate would reset. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's later enforcement record documents the pattern case after case. The borrower's agency was real, but the imbalance of information was the lever that turned that agency against the borrower. Saying so doesn't erase the borrower's responsibility. It puts that responsibility in proportion, beside the responsibility of the people who built the imbalance on purpose.

The second objection concerns geography: the crisis was global, the argument goes, and not a story about American policy. European banks were heavily exposed, Asian exporters were hammered by the collapse in demand, and mortgage securities were held across borders. All of that is true, and all of it strengthens the finding about the American mechanism rather than dissolving it. The instruments that spread the damage were mostly originated in the United States. The fee-on-origination model that rewarded volume over quality was an American design, and American policy permitted the conflict at the heart of the rating agencies, in which the issuer of a security paid the agency that rated it. That the poison spread worldwide doesn't make it any less American in origin. It makes the origin more consequential.

The third objection concerns repayment. TARP was largely repaid, and the Treasury's accounting shows substantial recovery and, on some measures, a modest profit. Didn't the public get its money back? TARP is simply the wrong unit of account. This chapter is not pricing the government's cash flow on one rescue program. It is pricing the welfare lost by households put out of their homes and the lifetime compounding lost by workers whose balances reset to a lower base and never caught up. It is pricing the regional economies that shrank and stayed shrunk, and the trust between citizens and institutions that an uneven rescue used up. None of that comes back when TARP is repaid, and none of it appears on the accounts that the repayment argument cites.

The fourth objection concerns reform. The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 was a serious response: it created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the Financial Stability Oversight Council, restricted banks' trading for their own accounts through the Volcker Rule, and imposed tougher capital and liquidity requirements. Wasn't the gap closed? The honest answer is that Dodd-Frank is best read as an early and partial installment of the accountability this book argues for, which is a genuine compliment rather than a dismissal. But a reform that is partial and reversible hasn't closed the gap. The Volcker Rule was later relaxed and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau was later weakened. A door pushed partway open can be pushed shut again, and that is not the same thing as a wall rebuilt. Chapter 9 returns to Dodd-Frank as one of the entering wedges, the partial but real beginnings of a fuller architecture.

One more point about 2008 matters most of all, because it answers the quiet assumption beneath all four objections, which is that the system did only what any system would have had to do. It didn't. The scope of a rescue is a choice, and history shows that a society can choose to rescue both its institutions and its households.

In the 1930s Sweden faced banking pressure much like what the United States faced from 1929 to 1933 and again in 2008 and 2009, and Sweden paired its bank rescue with substantial relief for households. In 1931 it left the gold standard and let the krona fall, which restored export competitiveness and eased the real burden of household debt. It coordinated the rescue of its banks with the modification of mortgages, so that relief reached households instead of stopping at balance sheets. Through the middle of the decade it ran public-works programs that put income directly into households, and in 1938 the Saltsjöbaden Agreement set the settlement between labor and capital that became the architecture of the Swedish welfare state. The comparison comes from the comparative literature on banking crises, in the tradition of Charles Kindleberger and Barry Eichengreen, which treats it as standing proof that rescuing banks and relieving households is not an either-or. The United States chose otherwise in 2008 and 2009, and chose similarly again in 2020. The choice was contingent then and remains contingent now, and Chapter 9 develops the fuller comparison. What matters here is that the road taken was chosen, not forced.
