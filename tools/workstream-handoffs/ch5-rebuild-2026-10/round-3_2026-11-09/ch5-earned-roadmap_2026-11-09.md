# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 5: The Accountability Gap

Near Baotou, a city in Inner Mongolia, there are villages that sit beside the waste ponds of one of the largest rare-earth processing complexes on earth. The plants there separate valuable elements from ore with strong acids, and for every ton of finished rare-earth oxide they leave behind something like ten tons of wastewater, which is the low end of the published estimates. That waste carries heavy metals and leftover acid, and because the ores are often bound up with thorium, it is mildly radioactive as well. It is piped into vast tailings ponds. The people who live nearby report raised rates of cancer, respiratory illness and birth defects, and some of the farmland around the plants has been abandoned because the soil will no longer grow food.

Hardly anyone who uses what those plants produce has ever heard of Baotou. The elements refined there end up in the magnets of electric-car motors and wind turbines and in the speakers of phones, and there is a fair chance some of them are within arm's reach of you right now.

Once you have seen a pattern, you start noticing it everywhere. Those waste ponds are one instance of a pattern that this chapter follows from the villages of Inner Mongolia toward you, and every case it visits is closer to home than the one before. After Baotou come fishing families on two American coasts and then a mining town in Montana. After that come households across the country in 2008, mine among them, then the generation paying into Social Security today, and finally a hospital room in an ordinary American family.

It works like this. A company, a generation or a government draws value from something it depends on but did not produce. That might be a mineral seam, a fishery, a river or the air, or it might be the labor of others, the trust between citizens or the future itself. Drawing the value creates a cost, and the cost is then cut loose from the people who benefited and pushed onto people with less power, less mobility and less ability to refuse.

The cost comes due later. By then the mine has closed. The company has reorganized. The executives have cashed out. The politicians who made the promises have left office. The cost stays with whoever was left standing where it landed.

The one reliable thing about this pattern is that the bill comes due. It is never cancelled, only deferred and handed to someone else, so the only open question is who ends up holding it. In the cases that follow, the people holding it come closer to you with every step.

## Baotou

The villagers beside those ponds are at the far end of a long chain, and it helps to see how long it is. China supplies on the order of 60 percent of the world's rare earths, and roughly half of China's own output comes from the Bayan Obo deposit and the processing complex at Baotou. So the accurate statement is not that this one place supplies 60 percent of the world. It is that this one place produces a large share of the output of the country that supplies most of the world.

From the villages, the severance runs in three directions. It runs across space, since the people who live with the waste are thousands of miles from the engineers who design the devices and the households that charge them. It runs across time, since the feedstock is paid for once while the damage to health and land unfolds over generations, and over something closer to geological time for the radioactive part of the waste. And it runs across a social distance that may be the hardest of the three to cross. The value flows to shareholders and to consumers in rich countries with strong institutions, while the cost settles on rural communities with little political voice and very few ways to refuse.

Honesty requires a word about how much is known. Data on health and environmental conditions around Baotou is limited, because China's information environment restricts what can be gathered and published, and it would be wrong to overstate what the record shows. The record is not empty, though. Zhang and colleagues documented the environmental costs of the rare-earth industry in 2022, and Zhou and Ge did similar work in 2021; both studies record the waste intensity and the burden on the region. What no one can honestly give is a tidy total for the cleanup. A figure of about $100 billion sometimes circulates, but it does not trace to any source that survives checking, and an unsourceable number belongs to the side. The defensible statements are narrower: the industry's annual environmental cost has been estimated in the billions, its revenue runs on the order of $10 billion to $12 billion a year, and the share of that revenue spent on remediation has historically been a small fraction of the harm.

The villagers' loss is also unusually permanent. Rare earths are the book's clearest example of a resource with almost no substitute. When oil is drawn down, its rents can at least be invested forward, the way Norway's fund invests them, so that something is built up to answer what is used up. No such bargain exists here, because no fund can rebuild a depleted deposit and nothing engineered can stand in for the elements at the scale modern technology needs them. Where substitutes run that thin, the framework's verdict sharpens: the severed cost can only be prevented or paid, never made good through reinvestment. Baotou is where the limit of substitution stops being a parameter in an appendix and becomes a field of tailings ponds.

None of this makes the person at the other end of the chain a villain, and that point is the center of the case rather than a softening of it. The consumer shares in the value the extraction produced, and the whole length of the supply chain separates the consumer from its cost, so private virtue or guilt has nothing to do with it. You could be a saint and still stand at the bright end of this severance. The distance a global supply chain creates is not an unfortunate side effect of the arrangement; it is what makes the arrangement livable for those who benefit, because it keeps the people who pay out of sight. The mechanism doesn't need anyone to be cruel. It needs only distance, and it manufactures distance for a living.

No one in this chapter is farther from you than the people living beside those ponds. Every case after this one is closer.

## Two coasts

The next people to end up holding the bill are fishing families on American coasts. That is still far from most readers, but it is no longer half a world away.

In the Gulf of Mexico, fishing families had worked the same marshes and grounds for generations. On April 20, 2010, a well being drilled for BP blew out beneath the Deepwater Horizon rig, and eleven workers were killed. Over the next eighty-seven days, before the well was capped, roughly 4.9 million barrels of oil poured into the water those families depended on.

BP had drawn enormous value out of the Gulf over the years, and that value had been banked and paid out to shareholders long before the blowout. Once the well failed, the cost went looking for a place to land, and it landed on the people and the living things that could least move out of its way. The oil itself had been destined for refineries and cities far from the water, but the poison stayed in the marshes. A quarterly earnings report looks three months ahead, while oil lingers for years in a marsh and in the tissue of fish. The toxins worked their way into the small organisms at the base of the food web and into the larger animals that depend on them, depressing reproduction for years and spreading through the whole system. None of those creatures could file a claim, so no balance sheet was built to register what they lost.

This is the case in which the polluter paid the most, and the figures deserve to be stated fairly. In 2016 BP put its own final estimate of its total cost at about $61.6 billion, and the figure passed $65 billion by 2018 as charges continued to accrue. That covers a 2015 settlement with the federal government and the Gulf states, a 2012 criminal penalty of $4 billion, and the cleanup and the litigation. No polluter in this book's cases pays more, and litigation is what forced the payment.

It still left the fishing families holding part of the bill. Beyond what BP paid, the continuing damage to the Gulf's ecosystems and fisheries is estimated at another $8 billion to $12 billion, and fifteen years after the spill those fisheries remain impaired. Other losses never had a number at all. Families had passed the knowledge of how to read the tides and the grounds from one generation to the next, and that knowledge broke where the fishery broke. Communities organized around the water lost the thing they were organized around. None of it appeared on any document BP had to reconcile, which is exactly why it could be lost for free.

Across the Gulf, families on the Mexican side were paid far less. The slick did not stop at the border, and Mexico pressed claims for damage on its side of the water. BP's settlement with Mexico came to about $25 million. Measured against comparable lengths of damaged coastline, that is smaller by orders of magnitude than what BP paid in the United States.

What separated the two sides was not the oil or the fish but the law each could reach. American claimants had the Oil Pollution Act of 1990, state tort and environmental law, and class actions that let many small injuries combine into a force a corporation must answer. Mexican claimants had a far weaker set of tools for pressing a claim across a border against a foreign corporation. The same spill produced two entirely different prices, and who could reach the law decided who got paid, a point this chapter returns to in Montana.

On the other coast, in Alaska, the herring families of Prince William Sound have been holding their share of a bill since 1989. That year the tanker Exxon Valdez ran aground on a reef in the sound and spilled millions of gallons of crude into one of the richest cold-water ecosystems on the continent. The legal fight that followed took the better part of two decades and ended with a very large award of punitive damages cut on appeal to a fraction of what the jury had set. Meanwhile the Pacific herring collapsed in the years after the spill and never came back. The families who had built their year around the herring season found there was no way to file for the loss of an entire fishery, because a fishery that simply stops returning is not the kind of harm the law knows how to compensate. For most of the decades since, the season has stayed gone. Chapter 6 gives Valdez the full accounting it deserves; for this chapter, it is enough to see who was left holding the loss.

## A town in Montana

Closer still is a small town that most Americans will never visit, though its product may well be in their homes. If your house was built or insulated while W.R. Grace was selling Zonolite, there is a real chance some of it is above your head right now, a loose, gray-brown fill lying between the joists. Millions of American attics still hold it. It came from a mine above Libby, in the far northwest corner of Montana.

W.R. Grace ran that vermiculite mine for roughly twenty-seven years, from 1963 to 1990. Heated vermiculite puffs up into a light, fire-resistant material that makes good insulation, and Grace sold it as Zonolite. For much of its life the Libby mine produced most of the vermiculite used in the United States, and it was profitable, which by the ordinary measures a company applies to itself made it a success.

The people of Libby paid for that success with their lungs. Running through the ore was a particularly lethal form of asbestos, whose fibers lodge in the lining of the lungs. Years later they produce cancers and scarring that slowly suffocate a person. Internal company documents later released in litigation show that the contamination was identified early and that workers' exposure was measured throughout the mine's life. The mining went on. Miners carried the dust home on their clothes, into their kitchens and onto their children's laps, and the company let the town use waste from the mine as fill for the high-school running track and for gardens. The dust didn't stay at the mine; it became the air of the town.

Hundreds of Libby residents have died of asbestos-related disease, and thousands more have been diagnosed with the lung damage that marks exposure. The Environmental Protection Agency eventually declared a public-health emergency in Libby, the first it had ever declared under the federal Superfund law. In a town of a few thousand people, oxygen tanks rode in grocery carts beside the bread, funerals for the relatively young were not unusual, and deaths from asbestos disease ran at many times the national rate.

Measured against what the town paid, the company's gains look small. Over its life the mine brought in revenue on the order of $100 million. Documented public spending on Libby comes to roughly $900 million to $1 billion, most of it for federal Superfund cleanup. Against the mine's revenue, that is already a ratio of about nine or ten to one. That leaves out the deaths and the disease themselves: the years of life lost, the hours spent caring for the sick and the futures that closed. No settlement ever fully priced those, and adding even a conservative value for them plausibly takes the total into the low billions. The honest statement is an order of magnitude built from disclosed parts. A company took in roughly $100 million and left behind costs at least ten times greater, and almost all of those costs fell on people who never saw any of the revenue.

The people of Libby then watched the company escape the reckoning by a route the law provided. With its liabilities mounting, Grace declared bankruptcy in 2001, reorganized and channeled its obligations into the slow machinery of a bankruptcy trust. Shareholders and executives went on with their lives, but the town could not go anywhere, because the fibers were in its soil and in its people's lungs. In 2005 the federal government indicted the company and several of its senior executives in what was then the largest environmental criminal prosecution the country had attempted. After a long trial, they were acquitted in 2009.

The acquittal does not mean the harm didn't happen; the death certificates, the cleanup budgets and the EPA's own emergency declaration document it. It means that criminal law is not built for this kind of mechanism. A prosecution looks for an identifiable act, committed knowingly, at an identifiable moment, by an identifiable person, and the harm at Libby came instead from thousands of ordinary operating choices made over twenty-seven years. Each of those choices was defensible on its own, and none of them was the single provable act the law requires. The law went looking for a moment, and there wasn't one. There was a process.

The legal scholar Katharina Pistor explains why the process wins. In *The Code of Capital* she argues that capital is not a natural substance but a legal artifact, assembled from legal modules: property, collateral, trusts, the corporate form, bankruptcy and the choice of which jurisdiction's law applies. Those modules create and protect wealth, and they decide who can be reached when something goes wrong. Read through Pistor, the bankruptcy that capped Grace's liability and the acquittal that ended the prosecution were the system working as designed, because the modules that let a corporation limit its liability and dissolve the responsible actor into a process are the same ones that make large-scale extraction financeable at all. The gap between what BP paid in the United States and what it paid in Mexico is the same point seen at a border. Pistor diagnoses the modules that encode and protect the severance, and the work of this book is to price what those modules sever.

## The neighbors, and me

Now the distance nearly closes. Every case so far has offered a comfortable reader some alibi, whether it was someone else's town, someone else's coast or a supply chain half a world long. The financial crisis of 2008 took the alibi away, because it struck the entire economy at once and reached into the retirement accounts and homes of the very people who had felt safe. The people holding the bill this time were the neighbors. I was one of them.

I was doing well at the time. I worked in sales, which gave me an unusual view of the crisis, because when the shock hit every company's finances at once, companies simply stopped spending. Every expenditure that wasn't already signed got sent back for review, and I watched demand freeze from inside the sales pipeline.

I had also done what the system told someone in my position to do. I had no pension. What I had instead was a 401(k), with a number in it that had been rising slowly, and I carried the belief that came with it: with no pensions anymore, that slowly rising number was how I would one day retire. When I opened my 401(k) in the middle of the crisis, the number had been revised to essentially zero. The crash had already wiped out the rest of my investments.

The rest arrived in a pile, the way these things actually arrive. A costly medical emergency hit my finances. My car broke down, and I replaced it by tapping my retirement savings early, which meant paying the tax penalty that punishes exactly that. The company I worked for failed, and I had to pay for my own move to another city to find work. Owning a home got postponed, and retirement got pushed back by about a decade. Together, those things reset me to starting over financially.

I'm not telling this because mine was the hard case. It wasn't; plenty of people lost far more and had far less cushion to lose it from. I'm telling it because of what it takes away. This is the case in the chapter where the alibi fails. Until now the cost has landed on someone else, somewhere else, but in 2008 it landed on millions of people who had saved. "I did everything I was told, and it reached me anyway" closes the distance more completely than "I was already struggling" ever could. The crisis didn't require anyone to gamble; it required only that they had saved, in the very instrument built for saving. So the question worth asking is not who was reckless. It is how that instrument came to be built so that a single year could erase years of ordinary prudence, and who stood on the far side of the transfer.

The crisis was the meeting of two transfers set in motion decades apart. The first began quietly in 1978, when section 401(k) of the tax code created the legal scaffolding for a new kind of retirement saving. Over the following years, American employers steadily replaced the defined-benefit pension with the defined-contribution account. Under a pension, the company promised a fixed income in retirement and carried the responsibility for funding it; under the new account, the worker saved into an individual account and carried the outcome. It was sold as freedom and ownership. You would control your own money, you wouldn't be tied to one employer for your pension, and you would own capital instead of depending on a promise.

What was said less loudly was whose freedom it really was. A pension placed three serious risks on the institution: market risk, the chance that investments would fall; longevity risk, the chance that retirees would live longer than planned; and behavioral risk, the chance that people would save too little or sell at the wrong moment. The new account moved all three off the corporate balance sheet and onto individuals. Unlike institutions, individuals cannot pool risk across thousands of lives or hedge a thirty-year horizon, so the freedom that mattered most in the transfer was the corporation's freedom from liability. For about thirty years the transfer produced no crisis on its own, because no shock had yet come along to reveal where the risk now sat.

The second transfer was compressed into a few years, roughly 2001 to 2008. In that window the financial industry built a machine for turning home mortgages into securities and then bundling and slicing those securities into ever more elaborate instruments. Their complexity wasn't an accident. It was structural and necessary to the business, because only an instrument too complicated to evaluate could be sold at scale to buyers who couldn't judge what they were buying. Among other places, those instruments ended up in the very retirement accounts the first transfer had filled with ordinary workers' savings. When the housing market turned and the instruments collapsed in 2008, the losses didn't stop at some abstract institution. They landed in those accounts.

Consider the scale. By the Federal Reserve's own count in its flow-of-funds accounts, American household net worth fell by roughly $11 trillion. In *House of Debt*, the economists Atif Mian and Amir Sufi reconstructed how that loss was distributed, and the distribution is the whole point, because the headline number hides the cruelty in it. The losses fell hardest, and by a wide margin, on the households with the least wealth and the most debt.

The arithmetic of leverage explains why. Picture two homeowners, one who owns a house outright and one who put down a tenth of the price and borrowed the rest. If the price of the house falls by a tenth, the first owner has lost a tenth of its value, which is a real loss but a survivable one. The second owner has lost the entire down payment, and the equity is gone. The same drop in price that dents a wealthy balance sheet wipes out a modest one. Across the country, families with the thinnest cushions and the heaviest mortgages lost a multiple of what wealthier families lost, not because they behaved worse but because leverage passes a decline in price straight through to equity. Mian and Sufi also show how differently households respond to such losses: for each dollar of housing wealth lost, the households with the least wealth cut their spending by about three times as much as those with the most. That is how a shock concentrated on the indebted poor spread into a general collapse of demand and a deeper recession for everyone.

Now look at what the system did manage to mobilize, and how fast. The Troubled Asset Relief Program committed $700 billion. When the Government Accountability Office audited the Federal Reserve's emergency lending in 2011, the cumulative total across all of its programs exceeded $16 trillion, and that cumulative figure is the one large number from 2008 that is well sourced enough to state with confidence. Within roughly eighteen months, bonuses at the rescued institutions had resumed.

On the other side of the ledger, there were millions of foreclosure filings in the United States between 2008 and 2012, and by CoreLogic's count about 4.1 million of them ended in completed foreclosures, with families put out of their homes. The system found trillions of dollars in a matter of days for the institutions at the center of the crisis. The households that had absorbed the manufactured complexity in their retirement accounts were now losing their homes, and for them the system organized nothing of comparable scale or speed. In 2011 the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission concluded that the crisis had resulted from human action and inaction, from choices rather than forces beyond anyone's control.

It would be too easy to leave the case there, with the contrast stated and the indignation invited. The case is stronger if it survives its best objections, so it is worth taking the four most serious ones in turn, each at full strength.

The first objection concerns agency. Many of the mortgages that defaulted went to borrowers who could not have repaid them under any reasonable assumption, and those borrowers signed the papers; no one held a pen to their hands. That is true, and it shouldn't be waved away. But agency depends on information, and the information surrounding these loans was built to work against the borrower. The people who originated the loans were paid according to the volume they wrote, not according to whether the loans were ever repaid, which is an engine for writing loans that shouldn't be written. The terms in the marketing often differed from the terms buried in the contract, and adjustable-rate loans were marketed to exactly the borrowers least likely to understand how the rate would reset. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's later enforcement record documents the pattern case after case. The borrower's agency was real, but the imbalance of information was the lever that turned that agency against the borrower. Saying so doesn't erase the borrower's responsibility. It puts that responsibility in proportion, beside the responsibility of the people who built the imbalance on purpose.

The second objection concerns geography: the crisis was global, the argument goes, and not a story about American policy. European banks were heavily exposed, Asian exporters were hammered by the collapse in demand, and mortgage securities were held across borders. All of that is true, and all of it strengthens the finding about the American mechanism rather than dissolving it. The instruments that spread the damage were mostly originated in the United States. The fee-on-origination model that rewarded volume over quality was an American design, and American policy permitted the conflict at the heart of the rating agencies, in which the issuer of a security paid the agency that rated it. That the poison spread worldwide doesn't make it any less American in origin. It makes the origin more consequential.

The third objection concerns repayment. TARP was largely repaid, and the Treasury's accounting shows substantial recovery and, on some measures, a modest profit. Didn't the public get its money back? TARP is simply the wrong unit of account. This chapter is not pricing the government's cash flow on one rescue program. It is pricing the welfare lost by households put out of their homes and the lifetime compounding lost by workers whose balances reset to a lower base and never caught up. It is pricing the regional economies that shrank and stayed shrunk, and the trust between citizens and institutions that an uneven rescue used up. None of that comes back when TARP is repaid, and none of it appears on the accounts that the repayment argument cites.

The fourth objection concerns reform. The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 was a serious response: it created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the Financial Stability Oversight Council, restricted banks' trading for their own accounts through the Volcker Rule, and imposed tougher capital and liquidity requirements. Wasn't the gap closed? The honest answer is that Dodd-Frank is best read as an early and partial installment of the accountability this book argues for, which is a genuine compliment rather than a dismissal. But a reform that is partial and reversible hasn't closed the gap. The Volcker Rule was later relaxed and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau was later weakened. A door pushed partway open can be pushed shut again, and that is not the same thing as a wall rebuilt. Chapter 9 returns to Dodd-Frank as one of the entering wedges, the partial but real beginnings of a fuller architecture.

One more point about 2008 matters most of all, because it answers the quiet assumption beneath all four objections, which is that the system did only what any system would have had to do. It didn't. The scope of a rescue is a choice, and history shows that a society can choose to rescue both its institutions and its households.

In the 1930s Sweden faced banking pressure much like what the United States faced from 1929 to 1933 and again in 2008 and 2009, and Sweden paired its bank rescue with substantial relief for households. In 1931 it left the gold standard and let the krona fall, which restored export competitiveness and eased the real burden of household debt. It coordinated the rescue of its banks with the modification of mortgages, so that relief reached households instead of stopping at balance sheets. Through the middle of the decade it ran public-works programs that put income directly into households, and in 1938 the Saltsjöbaden Agreement set the settlement between labor and capital that became the architecture of the Swedish welfare state. The comparison comes from the comparative literature on banking crises, in the tradition of Charles Kindleberger and Barry Eichengreen, which treats it as standing proof that rescuing banks and relieving households is not an either-or. The United States chose otherwise in 2008 and 2009, and chose similarly again in 2020. The choice was contingent then and remains contingent now, and Chapter 9 develops the fuller comparison. What matters here is that the road taken was chosen, not forced.

## A paycheck

The next case is closer than a neighbor's house. It comes out of your own paycheck, and in time it will come out of your children's. One of the lines on a pay stub is the Social Security tax, taken from nearly every paycheck to pay the benefits of the people who are retired today, and it sits inside the system Americans think of as the opposite of extraction.

Social Security is the promise that each working generation will care for the one before it, and it was built deliberately to express the idea that a society doesn't abandon its old. It genuinely is that. It is not a fraud and not a Ponzi scheme, and the critique that follows is aimed not at the program but at one specific feature of how its promises were funded. That distinction needs to be held firmly, because named carelessly, the mechanism this book traces is exactly the kind of thing that gets turned into a weapon against a program worth defending.

The mechanics are straightforward. Social Security was designed in 1935 as a pay-as-you-go system, in which today's workers' payroll taxes fund today's retirees' benefits. For decades the system collected more than it paid out, and the surplus was held in special Treasury bonds in a trust fund. The arrival of the baby-boom generation at retirement age was never something anyone needed to forecast. It was arithmetic: the cohort was already born and already counted, and the years when it would begin to retire could be read off a calendar decades ahead. By the early 2020s the system was paying out more than its payroll taxes brought in. Under current law and projections, the trust fund is expected to run out around 2033, after which incoming payroll taxes would cover roughly three-quarters of promised benefits, about 77 percent.

Here a number has to be handled with discipline, because it has done real damage to honest discussion of the subject. A figure circulates claiming that something like one hundred and eight trillion dollars in compounding returns was lost because the trust-fund surpluses were held in low-yielding Treasury bonds rather than in stocks, and it is sometimes paraded as the largest cost severance in human history. That figure is dishonest. Naming it as dishonest is part of the work here, because a framework that prices severed costs has no business smuggling in a number it cannot defend. The hundred-and-eight-trillion figure cannot be rebuilt from its own inputs. It sits roughly two orders of magnitude above the nearest serious estimate, it exceeds the largest size the trust fund ever reached, and it exceeds the total income the program has ever taken in. It comes from compounding money that was never actually available to compound, and it should never appear as a real measure of severed cost.

So set it aside and build the honest version from the parts. Over the life of the program, total contributions come to something on the order of $26 trillion and total benefits paid come to about the same. Those two enormous flows nearly cancel, because the system was designed for them to: money came in from workers and went out to retirees, year after year. The money actually available to invest was never that gross flow. It was only the running surplus, the reserve that accumulated when income exceeded payouts, which at its peak was on the order of $2.7 trillion. That is the honest base for an estimate of lost returns. Compound that surplus at a realistic and disclosed real return on stocks, net of the payouts the system actually had to make, and the lost return comes to a few trillion dollars. Not a hundred trillion, but a few. The method matters: which base compounds, at what assumed return, and net of which payouts. A number you cannot show your work for is a number you should not assert.

One more figure has to be kept separate, because blurring the two is its own kind of dishonesty. The program's Trustees publish a seventy-five-year actuarial shortfall of roughly $22.6 trillion, the projected gap between promised benefits and projected revenue over the long horizon. It is a real and important figure, but it measures a different quantity from the lost return, and the two must never be added together into one impressive total. The shortfall is what the program is projected to owe beyond what it will collect; the lost return is what the surplus might have earned. Keep them labeled, and keep them apart.

What makes Social Security the sharpest case in this chapter is where the severance actually lives, which is not where people instinctively look for it. The instinct says the severance is in the investment decision, the choice to hold the reserves in Treasury bonds instead of stocks. It isn't. Holding the reserves in Treasury bonds was prudent, because a public fund that gambled retirees' security on the stock market would take on exactly the risks a retirement system exists to absorb. The portfolio choice was sound. The severance lives in how the promises were made in the first place, and there is a documented moment where you can watch that happen.

In 1972 Congress passed an across-the-board increase in Social Security benefits of about 20 percent, and the same legislation created the automatic annual cost-of-living adjustment that has tied benefits to inflation ever since. The increase took effect for September 1972, which meant the larger checks reached beneficiaries in the first week of October, about a month before the November election. The payroll-tax increase meant to pay for the higher benefits didn't take effect until after the election. Put the timing beside the funding and the structure is plain: the reward of a generous promise reached voters before they voted, and its cost was scheduled to arrive after they had.

The indexing method in the 1972 law also turned out to be flawed, raising benefits by more than Congress had intended. Congress corrected it in 1977 by raising taxes and trimming benefits. By the early 1980s the program was in trouble again, with estimates showing the retirement trust fund running out of money as early as August 1983, and Congress passed another rescue that spring, once more raising taxes and cutting benefits. Each time, the bill landed on whoever happened to be working, paying in or about to retire when it came due.

It's worth being careful about what this shows, because the documented version is strong enough without embellishment. It doesn't require anyone in 1972 to have privately costed out the deficit of the 2030s and hidden it. It requires only what the record plainly holds. A benefit expansion was timed to an election, and its funding was deferred past that election. It was enacted while the ratio of workers to retirees was favorable, and the eventual decline of that ratio was a matter of public arithmetic rather than secret foresight. That is the severance. The political reward of the promise was collected while the promise was cheap to make, by people who would be out of office before the bill fully arrived, and the funding was pushed onto the workers of later decades, many of whom had not yet been born and none of whom could refuse.

There is a contrast that shows the architecture was a choice rather than a fate. The 1935 design was one option among several: pay-as-you-go, with surpluses parked in Treasury bonds. Chapter 4 described Norway's sovereign wealth fund, which channels the country's oil and gas rents into a compounding investment fund for future generations instead of spending them as they arrive. The problem is the same in both places: what one generation owes those that follow. The architectural answers differ, though, and so do the outcomes. National wealth and demography don't explain the difference; the architecture chosen at a specific moment by specific people does. Because a fund-based design existed as a live alternative, the pay-as-you-go design was selected rather than inevitable.

Two objections deserve a full hearing here as well. The first is the public-finance objection, and it should be conceded entirely. Holding the reserves in Treasury bonds is exactly what a sound fund manager does, and exposing retirees' core security to the stock market would have introduced precisely the volatility a retirement system exists to remove. The objection is correct, and it leaves the argument untouched, because the argument operates upstream of the portfolio. It isn't a claim about how the surpluses were invested but about how the promises were made, and a defense aimed at the portfolio cannot reach a claim aimed at the making of the promise. The two pass each other.

The second objection concerns tractability. This objection holds that the gap can be closed through ordinary and well-understood adjustments, such as raising the payroll-tax rate, lifting the cap on taxable wages, trimming the benefit formula at the top or raising the eligibility age as people live longer. The arithmetic is conceded: the gap can be closed, and closing it is the responsible thing to do. But notice what every item on that menu does. Each one moves the obligation onto the people living through the years of deficit: the workers who will pay a higher rate, receive a smaller benefit or work extra years. None of it reaches the generations who collected the political reward for making the promise, most of whom are now beyond the reach of any adjustment. Tractability is not the absence of severance. It is severance arriving exactly on the schedule the calendar set decades ago, and the fact that the bill can be paid does not change who is handed it.

## A hospital room

The last case is the closest of all: one family, and in the end one body. Here the mechanism works on individual people at the most vulnerable stretches of their lives, and the costs it severs are the financial ruin of particular households, often named on particular fundraising pages.

Over the 2010s, raising money for medical bills on online fundraising platforms grew from a curiosity into an institution. By 2020 more than a third of the campaigns on the largest of these platforms were medical. Between roughly 2016 and 2020 there were about 438,000 medical campaigns, which together drew more than $2 billion from some 21 million donations. The outcomes are the real story. Only about 12 percent of those campaigns met their stated goals, roughly 16 percent received nothing at all, and nearly nine in ten fell short of what they asked for.

The platform's chief executive has said that it was never designed to be a healthcare financing system and that it became the default backstop by accident, filling a hole that a working safety net would have filled. Medical crowdfunding exists in other wealthy countries too, including Canada, the United Kingdom and Germany, but the United States stands apart in how much it relies on crowdfunding as a substitute for coverage. Peer-reviewed comparative work finds the country using it far more than those comparison countries do, as the analysis by Saleh and colleagues in *JAMA Network Open* in 2020 shows.

The families who fare worst on these pages are the ones who were already worst off. Studies of medical crowdfunding find that campaigns run by people with distinctively Black or Hispanic surnames raise less money and are less likely to meet their goals, even after accounting for place and time. A 2022 analysis in the *American Journal of Public Health* by researchers including Kenworthy and Igra documented the scale of these campaigns and the structure of who succeeds. A system of mutual charity that depends on the size and wealth of a person's social network will reliably deliver the least to the people whose networks were thinnest to begin with. It sends the most help to those who need it least and the least to those who need it most, so the backstop doesn't correct the inequity. It launders it.

Behind the fundraising pages is a slower pressure on the same families. At the Economic Policy Institute, the economists Lawrence Mishel and Josh Bivens have documented how the typical worker's pay came apart from overall productivity in the early 1970s, after which the economy kept growing more productive while the median wage stalled. Over the same years healthcare costs rose at several times the rate of general inflation. The pressure that lands a family on a fundraising page is the widening gap between those two curves, with flat wages below, rising medical costs above and households caught in between. The political scientist Jacob Hacker called the broader version of this the great risk shift: the long and systematic movement of economic risk off institutions and onto families. The retirement account that collapsed in 2008 is another face of it. The pension replaced by a 401(k) and the medical bill passed to a fundraising page are the same shift in different clothes.

At the closest range of all, the mechanism can be seen in a single family. The journalist Katy Butler published an account of her father's last years, and it should be rendered strictly within what she wrote, with nothing added. As her account makes plain, the architecture of medical payment pushes relentlessly in one direction. Medicare pays a doctor very little for a long conversation about whether an intervention is wise and much more for the procedure itself. By Butler's account, Medicare would have paid her father's doctor $54 for that kind of conversation, while it paid $461 to the surgeon who implanted his pacemaker, on top of a hospital fee of roughly $12,000, nearly half of which went to the company that made the device. The liability system pushes the same way, since performing a procedure protects a clinician against the malpractice exposure that declining it could create.

So the device went in, and it kept her father's heart beating reliably through years of the cognitive decline that followed. Had the long conversation been the well-paid act, those are years the patient might have chosen to forgo. Through them the unpaid work of caregiving fell on his wife, hour after hour. At seventy-seven, Butler's mother found herself on duty more than eighty hours a week, work that appeared on no ledger, was counted by no system and was reimbursed by nothing. The cost had been severed from the institutions that profited from the procedure and fastened onto a household at its most defenseless.

The surgeon and writer Atul Gawande described the same pattern across the whole system in *Being Mortal*. The payment system rewards intervention at every decision point and underfunds the palliative care and the training that would let medicine attend well to the end of a life, and added up, that produces the over-treatment of the dying. No individual clinician has to choose extraction over compassion for this to happen. It comes from the rational responses of decent people to the incentives in front of them, and those incentives are a design choice, so changing the payment architecture changes the overall behavior without anyone's character changing at all. The same logic reaches past the dying into maternal care, mental health and the long management of chronic disease, wherever the system pays for the procedure and starves the conversation.

Butler's account describes one family's room, but rooms like it exist in every town in the country, and sooner or later most families sit in one.

## The pattern, named

Lay the cases side by side and the same skeleton shows through every one of them. Something shared was drawn on as though it cost nothing, whether it was a mineral seam, a coastline, a fishery, a supply chain, a nation's savings, a promise between generations or a dying body. Drawing on it produced a real cost, and that cost was then cut loose from the people who gained and fastened onto people who could not refuse it.

The word for this needs a moment of defense, because it carries a second meaning that can mislead. Severance is also what a company calls the check it writes when it lets a worker go, and a reader who hears that meaning first might suspect the term smuggles in the very commodified view of labor this book means to resist. It does the opposite. The two meanings share a root because both name a cut: the worker cut loose from the wage, and the cost cut loose from the party who caused it. The book keeps the word because the cut is the thing being described. A gentler word would do in language exactly what the accounting does in fact, which is to make the severing disappear.

The party that benefits escapes the reckoning under a handful of recurring covers. One is distance, which keeps the tailings at Baotou half a world away from the people charging their phones. Another is time, which let a Congress in 1972 collect the reward for a promise whose bill falls due in the 2030s. There is the gap in what each side knows, between the company that measured the asbestos and the families who breathed it, and there is the gap in who is able to leave, because capital can move and a poisoned town cannot. A cost can also become so routine that a dying fishery or a family's bankruptcy fades into background noise. And there is the look of abundance: cheap insulation, cheap electronics and a rising retirement balance all make a real cost look like no cost at all, at least for a while. Chapter 7 names the full set of these covers precisely; here they serve only to show how the escape is managed.

Call the roll of those who absorbed the cost. There is a town that could not move, fishing communities that could not relocate and villages with no way out. There are foreclosed households, and future taxpayers who cannot opt out of an obligation they will inherit. There are families whose fundraising pages fall short, and an elderly woman whose eighty-hour weeks of caregiving appear on no ledger. In every case the beneficiary departed: the company went bankrupt, the politicians left office, the generation that made the promise grew old. And in every case the cost stayed behind with whoever could not escape it.

Stated without a villain, the claim is this: these cases do not show a system failing; they show a system doing what it was built to do. No one has to intend harm for the pattern to appear, because the structures reward whoever draws the value and push the cost onto whoever is least able to resist it.

None of this is a new insight, and credit belongs where it is due. In *The Social Costs of Private Enterprise*, published in 1950, K. William Kapp argued that shifting costs onto third parties is a routine feature of private enterprise rather than an occasional failure at the edges of the market. The traditions of political ecology and environmental justice that grew up afterward documented who those third parties reliably turn out to be, and in the work of scholars such as Joan Martínez-Alier and Robert Bullard the answer is consistent: the poor, the rural and the politically voiceless. The diagnosis is theirs.

What this book adds is narrower, and it is worth stating plainly. It puts a price on the commons drawn down by a single extraction and posts that price as a reserve before the cost comes due. Existing tools come close without quite doing this. After Deepwater Horizon, regulators assessed the Gulf with a method called natural-resource damage assessment, which works only after the harm has happened. In 1990 Robert Costanza and Charles Perrings proposed posting a bond in advance, which is the right timing, but they set it against the worst damage that could possibly occur rather than against the specific cost a given extraction severs. That specific cost is the framework's target. The diagnosis is old; the contribution is the price.

## The same object, named honestly

Ask what exactly was being drawn on in each of these cases. The answer is more unified than the variety of the cases suggests, and naming it is the most important move this chapter makes.

A reef, the atmosphere, a community of fishing families, a body of human labor and a generation not yet born look like entirely different kinds of things. Define a commons by its structure rather than its substance, though, and they turn out to be one kind of thing. Structurally, a commons is anything people depend on and share that was never produced as a commodity for sale. Nobody manufactured a reef; it was inherited. Nobody built the atmosphere to a specification. A community's knowledge and trust were never made to be sold, the care that raises the next generation was never produced as a market good, and a future generation is the purest case of something inherited rather than made.

By that definition the reef, the atmosphere, the community and the unborn are the same object: a commons whose abundance hid the fact that drawing on it carried a cost. That is why one apparatus can price all of them. It prices forward, against a future that is being foreclosed, and it prices backward, against extraction from communities that has already taken place.

Putting a price on any of these things invites a serious objection, and the philosopher Michael Sandel has made it as well as anyone in *What Money Can't Buy*. Sandel warns that pricing a sacred landscape, a community or the unborn launders the priceless into a transaction. It concedes the extractor's frame, in which everything is interchangeable and everything has a number. Worse, a price can make a harm legitimate by turning a violation into a fee, something you are allowed to do as long as you pay. That worry has to be met here, because no later chapter takes it up instead.

The answer turns on what the alternative to pricing really is. The choice is not between a sacred thing left unpriced and a sacred thing reduced to a number. It is a choice between two numbers, because the thing already has a number and that number is zero. Zero is the figure the extractive accounting already assigned to the reef, the community and the future, and zero is what made the extraction profitable. A reef destroyed at a cost of zero is precisely how the extraction got financed.

The framework refuses that zero. It insists the cost was never zero and sets out to say what the cost actually is. Pricing something in order to sell it and pricing something in order to reserve it are different acts that happen to share a word. Pricing to sell turns a forest into board feet, while pricing to reserve names what the forest is worth so that its destruction can no longer be booked as free.

This refusal of the zero has a direct ancestor. In 1944 Karl Polanyi published *The Great Transformation*, a book built around what he called the fictitious commodity. Polanyi argued that the market treats labor, land and money as if they had been produced for sale, when none of them was. Labor is human life, land is nature and money is a token of social trust, and running a society as though they were ordinary goods does violence both to them and to the people and places they are.

The definition of a commons used in this book descends from that thesis, and so does the chapter's basic test, which is to imagine the commons gone and ask what cost appears. Polanyi's deepest idea was the double movement: when a society has something turned into a market good out from under it, it pushes back and binds that thing to social obligation again. For Polanyi the double movement was a historical tendency he could describe. Here it becomes an instrument.

The closest living ally for this argument is Nancy Fraser, whose work repays a debt this book took on several chapters ago. Fraser distinguishes exploitation from expropriation. Exploitation takes from workers through the wage, through a contract, however unequal that contract may be. Expropriation takes without even the form of a contract. It is capital's reliance on unpaid care work, the labor that keeps a society going. It is also capital's reliance on the resources and sinks of nature, which sit outside the wage relation altogether and are simply taken. Fraser maps this ground in her essay "Behind Marx's Hidden Abode" and in her book *Cannibal Capitalism*.

The reef taken without a contract, the community's knowledge taken without a contract and the caregiver's labor taken without a contract are all expropriation in Fraser's exact sense. So are the options of a future generation, which can never be a party to any contract at all. Chapter 2 left a thread loose in its discussion of where the word severance comes from, and this is where that thread ties off. The name it was waiting for is Fraser's.

There is a caution to state honestly here, because it answers the sharpest hostile reading of the whole project. A critic might say that pricing the commons is financialization by another route, with the commons repackaged as a new asset class for someone to trade. Polanyi and Fraser would not endorse putting a price on nature, and this book does not claim they would. Their tradition refused to treat nature as an asset, and the framework stands inside that refusal rather than against it, because accounting for a cost is a different act from creating an asset to trade.

Katharina Pistor supplies the test that shows the difference. In *The Code of Capital* she names four legal attributes that turn an ordinary asset into capital. Priority lets a claim rank ahead of others; durability lets it keep that rank over time; universality makes it binding on everyone; and convertibility lets its holder cash it back into state money. An accountability bond is built to lack the two attributes that let capital circulate. It is not convertible, because it cannot be redeemed for money or for anything except the discharge of the specific liability it records. It is not transferable, because it cannot be sold, traded or repackaged into a security. A debt that can only be paid, never traded, is the opposite of a coded asset, and that is the difference between creating an asset and accounting for a cost, shown in the code's own terms.

The most forceful living version of the financialization worry belongs to Brett Christophers, whose work traces how a financialized economy misprices whatever it turns into a tradable asset. The answer to him in principle is the one just given, and a later chapter takes up his specific argument in full.

## Who shaped the system?

So far the framework has left one question to the side, and it is worth bringing back on purpose because it complements everything above. The apparatus of this book accounts for who bore the cost; that is its ledger. A separate question sits beside it, which is who shaped the architecture that sent the cost where it went. The ledger shows where the cost landed, but it does not show who built the chute it slid down.

A rigorous toolkit for that second question already exists, and it would be a mistake of pride not to use it. James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock built the Public Choice tradition to analyze how concentrated interests shape institutions to serve themselves at a diffuse cost to everyone else. Its engine is Tullock's analysis of rent-seeking, the spending of real resources to capture a privilege rather than to create value. Mancur Olson described the imbalance underneath it in 1965: concentrated interests organize, diffuse interests do not, and the rules drift toward whoever shows up.

Where the people who extract the value and the people who bear the cost are different populations, as they are in most of this chapter's cases, the chain runs cleanly. You identify the interests that shaped the institutions, trace the lobbying and the coalitions that produced the design, and then ask whom the design serves and whom it costs. Run that chain across these cases and a roster appears.

At Libby, internal company documents released in litigation show lobbying and an effort to delay the regulation of asbestos. In the Gulf, offshore drilling was overseen by the Minerals Management Service, a textbook case of a captured regulator: its staff had accepted gifts and travel from the industry, and people moved back and forth between the agency and the companies it was supposed to oversee. After the blowout the agency was broken up and reorganized, and the Interior Department's own inspector general had reported on the capture before the disaster, not only after it.

The deregulation that made the 2008 crisis possible came in three main pieces. The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999 took down the wall between commercial and investment banking, the Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000 exempted derivatives from regulation, and a series of federal rulings pushed aside state consumer protections. Financial-industry lobbying shaped all three, as the Center for Responsive Politics and congressional hearings documented at the time.

In healthcare, the pharmaceutical industry has consistently led American industries in lobbying spending, and for decades Medicare was barred from negotiating the prices of the drugs it paid for. That ban was only recently loosened, and it is as clean a rent-seeking outcome as the literature contains. Chapter 8 adds the coal economy of McDowell County, West Virginia, where an industry spent generations shaping a state's laws and regulations to its own advantage.

The chain does not run cleanly everywhere, and it is more honest to say so than to force it. At Baotou, global consumer demand shapes the extraction as much as any lobby does, and the actors blur together. In Social Security, the people who will bear the future cost were in part the payers of an earlier era, so the dynamic across time does not reduce to a roster of interests. Where the chain blurs like this, the structural account of who bore the cost carries the weight on its own, because it does not depend on finding a lobby.

How this toolkit is being used matters, and it is easy to misread. This book draws on the rent-seeking vocabulary without endorsing Buchanan's larger political project, much of which points in directions this book does not follow. The tools belong to the toolkit, not to the opposing camp. The framework's ledger of who bore the cost is exactly what Public Choice's analysis of who shaped the architecture needs, and the two fit together without either one swallowing the other. Readers who want the architecture side developed in full should read Buchanan, Tullock and their successors directly, and they will find that the rent-seeking vocabulary survives many different readings of Buchanan's broader commitments.

This is a deliberate invitation to readers at the American Enterprise Institute, Cato, Mercatus and George Mason University, where the Public Choice tradition is most at home. The ledger this book builds is the natural complement to the rent-seeking analysis they already do, and the two halves are stronger joined. Chapter 9 develops that complement at greater length.

## Restitution and foreclosure

The cases raise a single question, and it turns the chapter from diagnosis toward prescription. If all of this is one mechanism, what would honest accounting for it require? The framework answers with a pair of instruments, named here for the first time and built out in later chapters, with Chapter 6 supplying the method and Chapter 9 the politics. The task here is only to name the two instruments and the lineage they stand on.

The accountability gap has two faces in time. One is harm already done, such as the lungs of Libby, the marshes of the Gulf, the herring of Prince William Sound and the foreclosed households of 2008. The other is harm not yet done: the cost that current extraction is foreclosing right now for people who do not yet exist, as a habitable atmosphere is spent, irreplaceable materials are drawn down and options are closed off. An architecture that addresses one face and ignores the other addresses only half the problem.

So the framework proposes two instruments. The Restitution Bond discharges harm already done; it looks backward, and it is owed to particular people and communities who exist and have been hurt. The Foreclosure Bond discharges the cost that current extraction imposes on the future; it looks forward, and it protects outcomes for people who do not exist yet. Together they make up the aggregate accountability bond the framework requires. Each is measured against its own face of the cost, so the Restitution Bond is measured against damage already done and the Foreclosure Bond against the remaining value of the commons minus whatever has already been posted to protect it.

Why call them bonds? The word was chosen over the alternatives on purpose. A tax goes into general revenue with nothing tying the amount to any specific future obligation. A fee buys permission to proceed, which makes it a toll rather than a remedy. A fund pools money without discharging any particular liability. A bond differs from all three, because it is posted before the full cost is borne, it carries forward across the long tail of that cost, and it discharges something observable: a specific posted amount set against a specific identified liability.

That structure already exists in the world, which is part of why the word is honest. Mining companies post reclamation bonds under the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977, money held against the cost of restoring the land. Oil and gas companies post decommissioning bonds against the future cost of closing a well. Norway's sovereign fund works like a bond at national scale, holding capital against future need. The economics of reparations treats a historical injustice as a documented debt to be discharged, and the civic-republican tradition's obligation to future generations finds its natural accounting form in a bond. The term gathers all of these together.

A note is owed on why the backward instrument is called Restitution when its main source is the reparations tradition. In the old legal sense of *restitutio in integrum*, restitution means restoring what was taken, and it is a broader and older term, wide enough to cover every kind of cost severance from environmental damage and occupational disease to a destroyed community or a lost ecosystem. The reparations tradition is the framework's primary source for backward-looking accountability, and it runs through Ta-Nehisi Coates, William Darity Jr. and A. Kirsten Mullen, Darrick Hamilton and Dalton Conley. Calling the instrument the Reparations Bond would narrow it to one historical application, though, and it would make the method's use in other settings read as a loose analogy rather than the same discipline applied directly.

Other names fail for their own reasons. Restoration assumes the harm can be physically undone, and many of these harms cannot. Indemnification routes the obligation through insurance, a frame the framework does not use. Damages collapses the bond into the measurement and loses the advance posting that is the whole point. So the framework joins the reparations tradition instead of rebranding it.

Darity and Mullen describe what reparations require in three parts, which they call ARC: acknowledgment, redress and closure. Redress is the material part, the payment that repairs the harm, and the Restitution Bond is the instrument that carries out that material part of redress. It works inside their framework and uses their language rather than competing with it.

The forward instrument's name does similar work. A reader who hears "foreclosure" probably thinks first of 2008 and of a family barred from its home, and that association is a continuity to claim rather than a coincidence to apologize for. The word comes from the old *forclore*, to bar from, and it describes both events exactly. A mortgage foreclosure bars a household from its home through a legal architecture that lets the barring happen at scale, and the Foreclosure Bond addresses future generations barred from what they should inherit: a habitable atmosphere, intact ecosystems and irreplaceable materials. The mechanism is the same in both, and only the scale differs, a household in one case and a generation in the other.

The alternatives miss what matters. "Future generations" reads as soft philanthropy, "sustainability" has been absorbed into corporate branding, and "reclamation" is already a narrower legal term. "Conservation" and "intergenerational" both miss the specific act of barring that the word foreclosure names.

The lineage behind the two instruments can now be placed. Pistor supplies the legal half of the diagnosis: she shows how law encodes and protects the severance, and the framework prices what the law severs. For the forward instrument's moral footing, Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) supplies the standing of future people, a way of taking seriously our obligations to people not yet born at the level of outcomes rather than particular identities. The framework leans on Parfit without claiming to have solved the non-identity problem he famously left open, and it names that problem as a standing limit rather than quietly treating it as defeated.

The civic-republican tradition of Philip Pettit's *Republicanism* (1997) and Quentin Skinner's *Liberty Before Liberalism* (1998) adds the idea of freedom as non-domination. A society that extracts in ways that subject others to domination breaks that commitment, whatever contract it has or has not broken. The framework does not ask readers to accept civic-republican philosophy as the only valid frame, and the tradition's weight falls in a particular place. Non-domination does its strongest work on the harms this chapter documents within a single generation. The forward instrument leans mainly on the ethics of obligation across generations, meaning Parfit's account of standing together with the ethics of discounting taken up in Chapter 6, and the republican reading is a complement there rather than the frame that carries the weight.

The environmental-economics lineage runs through John Hartwick's rule of 1977, which holds that the rents from a depleting resource should be invested in reproducible capital so that consumption need not fall as the resource runs out. Norway's fund is the cleanest real-world application. These are tools for preventing foreclosure rather than for repairing past harm, and keeping that distinction clear keeps the two faces of the bond apart.

The backward instrument's lineage runs through the reparations tradition. When Ta-Nehisi Coates published "The Case for Reparations" in *The Atlantic* in 2014, he supplied the shape of the argument: name the mechanism, name the harm, name the debt. The framework extends that shape to communities built around extraction. The economics runs through Darity and Mullen's *From Here to Equality* (2020), the field's contemporary landmark and this framework's primary source. It also runs through *Umbrellas Don't Make It Rain* (2015), by Darrick Hamilton, William Darity Jr., Anne Price, Vishnu Sridharan and Rebecca Tippett. Their matched comparisons showed that the structural variable is wealth rather than behavior: Black households at the highest level of education hold less wealth than white households at lower levels. And the lineage reaches back to Dalton Conley's *Being Black, Living in the Red* (1999), an earlier statement that wealth is the variable that matters.

The backward instrument applies the same matched-comparison method to communities built on extraction. McDowell County's collapse in wealth can be measured against comparable Appalachian counties that never depended on coal, Libby's health costs against similar towns that never had a Grace mine, and Appalachia's opioid deaths against comparable counties where Purdue's documented marketing did not concentrate. Chapter 8 runs that third comparison in full.

The backward lineage has a second branch, and it runs forward as well, the way the framework itself does. A current program in climate economics already prices the atmosphere as a debt in both directions. Andrew Fanning and Jason Hickel have calculated the compensation owed for the appropriation of the atmosphere, the Caribbean Community (CARICOM) has advanced a plan for reparatory justice, and the Loss and Damage Fund was established at the 2022 United Nations climate conference and put into operation the following year. All three treat the atmosphere as a commons overused by some and foreclosed for others, with the debt running backward to past emissions and forward to the loss of a stable climate, priced on the same social cost of carbon this book uses.

The framework concedes the priority. That program reached the two-way atmospheric commons first, and it argues the debt at the scale such debts are usually argued, which is the nation and the question of who owes whom across a global carbon budget. What the framework adds is not the idea but the grain. It prices a single extraction event rather than a national balance, and it reserves against that event rather than netting one nation's account against another's. The atmospheric-debt program is the macro accounting of the same commons the framework meters one ton at a time.

A boundary has to be drawn here, honestly and briefly. The same apparatus runs in reverse, and the most developed backward work in the field is Darity and Mullen's pricing of the racial wealth gap and the gap in life expectancy, both measurable legacy effects of historical injustice. McDowell County's own gap in life expectancy is the coal-extraction version of the same method.

One question at the edge of this work is genuinely unresolved, and pretending otherwise would be exactly the kind of overclaiming this book refuses. How should coerced labor itself be priced, as distinct from its downstream legacy effects? The economics of reparations has not settled that question, and it lies outside this book's empirical scope. The framework reaches coerced cases through effects that can be measured: wealth gaps, gaps in life expectancy, broken transmission of knowledge and trauma passed down through generations. It leaves the direct pricing of coercion as an open question, one the field is already carrying forward. The dispossession of Indigenous land and the cultural knowledge that colonization severed sit in the same deferred territory. The method can reach them in principle, but the computing belongs to later work rather than to this book.

Where does bond-posting actually stand across American extraction today? Far below the remaining value of the commons it is meant to protect. The Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Research Service put the federal black-lung program's payouts at roughly $44 billion through 2009, the most recent authoritative total. That is a real number and a conservative floor for the program's full run, and yet the program's trust fund still carried $5.1 billion in debt as of September 2024.

The deeper finding is not that the instrument is missing. It exists at scale, and it is partly assigned to the wrong party: when the coal excise tax falls short, the difference is borrowed from the federal general fund, which means it comes from the public rather than from the extraction that severed the cost. Reclamation bonds posted under the 1977 law fall several billion dollars short of the reclamation they are supposed to guarantee. And no Foreclosure Bond of any kind stands against the obligation Social Security carries across generations.

This is the accountability gap, stated as a measurable fact rather than a metaphor: the bond posted is a small fraction of the cost severed. Chapter 6 supplies the method for measuring the gap, and Chapter 9 takes up the policy of closing it.

One discipline closes this section, because it guards against a misreading the whole argument invites. What is owed is a separate question from what any society is willing or able to collect today. The method applies whether or not anyone is ready to post the bond, and its contribution is accounting rather than advocacy. Under an honest count, the size the bond would have to be has an answer in any political weather. How to move from today's nearly empty bonds toward the real value of the commons is a political question, and it belongs to Chapter 9, which treats it as open rather than pretending to legislate a single answer.

## The distance closes

This chapter began in villages most readers will never see, and it ends in a hospital room that could belong to any family. The mechanism never changed along the way; only the distance did. It ran from half a world away to two American coasts, then to a town whose product sits in American attics, then into the neighbors' houses, then into your own paycheck and finally to a single bed.

At the near end of that chain stands the reader. Standing there doesn't make you a villain. It means you are inside the mechanism, gaining from it in some places and paying for it in others.

One distance cannot close, though. The people who will hold the largest share of the bill haven't been born yet. They are farther away than Baotou, in time rather than space, and they cannot object to anything decided now. That is the second face of the cost, and it is why the framework needs a Foreclosure Bond as well as a Restitution Bond.

The question the chapter leaves is not whether cost severance is real, because the cases settle that. The question is what it would take to post the bond before the cost is severed, rather than chasing it afterward once the beneficiary has gone. The rest of this book is built to answer it.
