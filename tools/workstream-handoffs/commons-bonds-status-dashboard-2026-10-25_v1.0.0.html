New idea: the true cost of an AI data center

This fits the framework and your history unusually well. Virginia is the largest data-center market in the world, you live in Hampton, and you spent a career in IT, NIH and healthcare IT, in and around data centers. Most writers on this beat are reporters or activists. An insider who has crawled through server rooms and can price the cost honestly is a different voice. The topic is also live this fall. Verify each of these before using it: JLARC's December 2024 report on data centers in Virginia, Dominion's data-center-driven rate request, 2026 bills such as HB 503 on keeping data-center transmission costs off residential bills, Governor Spanberger's data-center framework and AI task force, and an October 2026 Senate report on what the AI build-out costs ratepayers.

The strategic point that answers your worry about giving away the book: use the lens on a new case and keep the book's cases out. A piece that runs the framework on something the book doesn't cover shows the method works on the biggest 2026 headline without spending any chapter. It also gives the proposal a timely line for its platform section.

Safe to use

  • The term cost severance and its one-sentence definition. Both are already public in your pitches.
  • The stance: no villain, the price is honest about everything except what it leaves out, and the benefits go on the same ledger as the costs. Your benefits-vs-costs framing is exactly the book's posture.
  • The idea of a forward-posted bond, at concept level only. Mine reclamation bonds give you a precedent outside the book. Keep it to one paragraph, because Pricing Honestly (Atlantic Ideas) relies on it.
  • Your own data-center scenes: the cable-station "rat's nest" and the mainframe walled into drywall after a flood. They're in your May session notes and appear in no chapter.
  • A rough cost count of your own from public numbers (rate filings, JLARC, county tax data), presented as illustration, not as the TA method.

Keep for the book

  • The airlock and the Commons Inversion Test. That's the proposal's opening hook and the Aeon pitch.
  • The McDowell ton walk ($4.71 → $510) and the Four Gates (Chapter 8). It's already spent in the Harper's piece and the op-ed.
  • RCV, the formulas, and the Chapter 6 counting architecture (Technical Appendix).
  • Norway as the existence proof (Chapter 4, Foreign Affairs).
  • Chapter 9's four-step program.
  • Chapter 1's workplace story (the H-1B colleague).

Before drafting: run your two-path test

  • Path 1, allocation: value goes to the operators and to AI users everywhere. Costs land on local ratepayers (transmission and generation build-out), neighbors (noise, diesel backup generators), watersheds, and whoever is left holding the grid build-out and shells when hardware turns over.
  • Path 2, shield and distance: candidate evidence includes geographic distance (users worldwide, costs in Loudoun and Prince William), time (rate-base costs recovered over decades on equipment with short service lives) and institutional distance (rate classes, nondisclosure on water use). You should judge whether both paths hold. If they hold only partly, that's an honest finding and arguably a better essay.
  • The benefit side: county tax revenue, the small number of permanent jobs, and real uses of AI. Your healthcare and nursing perspective can speak to clinical AI without overclaiming. Per the no-invented-facts rule, every figure here needs a source before it goes into prose.
  • Book decision, later: data centers aren't a case in the book yet. Keep it out of the Ch 5 rebuild. Consider one sentence in the proposal overview this winter, and decide on a short prospective case in Chapter 9 next summer, after the essay has an outcome.
