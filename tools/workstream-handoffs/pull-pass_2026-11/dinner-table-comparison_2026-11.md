## Chapter 5

**Why C.** I counted the places where I had to stop and reread. In the rings redraft (B) there were about twelve. In C there were about five. The biggest difference is the four objections about 2008. B states each one as a bare sentence ("The public got its money back." "Reform closed the gap.") and the reader cannot tell it is an objection until four paragraphs later. C says "One objection is about the borrowers," "Another objection is that the crisis was global," and so on. C also explains the two homeowners step by step, says what the three ways the oil spill's cost fell were, and replaces B's short verdict lines ("indignation is cheap," "The two pass each other," "It is not a wall rebuilt," "does that for a living") with plain statements. B is the tighter piece of writing. C is the one a reader gets through in one pass.

**C is not your voice yet either.** Your own prose in Chapter 1 runs in longer sentences joined with "and," with asides. C runs in short sentences of the same shape, one after another, and it defines terms in the middle of sentences about fifteen times. That is the work left for your redline, and it is mostly cutting.

**Neither version pulls through the last third.** From "One kind of thing" through "Restitution and foreclosure" (about 3,500 words) the chapter becomes a tour of names: Polanyi, Fraser, Christophers, Pistor, Parfit, Pettit, Skinner, Hartwick, Coates, Conley, then two paragraphs on names the bonds were not given. That is the content, and it is the same in both. It is also the part Darity knows best. A voice pass does not fix it; a decision about what stays in this chapter would.

**Lost or added.** Every figure and every quotation in C matches B exactly (checked by script). Your 2008 account is identical in both. C adds explanatory steps and no new facts, with three things for you to check:

- "In its grocery store, oxygen tanks rode in the carts beside the bread." B says "grocery carts." C's wording implies the town has one store.
- "Being angry is not the same as being right, though…" is a new sentence put in your mouth in place of "indignation is cheap."
- The explanation of Parfit ("meaning how well or badly things go for whoever ends up living") is the drafter's wording, not Parfit's.

**From the old chapter (A):** nothing was lost that should come back. Its Libby and Social Security sections say the same things at greater length, and its opening states the thesis before the reader has anyone to hold.

**The three weakest passages in C**

1. The mid-sentence definitions. Examples: "The Restitution Bond discharges, meaning pays off, harm already done." "…the median wage, which is the wage of the worker in the exact middle, stalled." "…requiring a bond, which is money set aside as a guarantee, to be posted in advance." One or two help. Fifteen read like a textbook, and to an economist they read as talking down.
2. "They are different in what they are made of. They are alike in how they stand toward the people who use them, and if a commons is defined that way, by its structure rather than its substance, they become one kind of thing." The added explanation is harder to follow than the sentence it explains.
3. "Parfit offers a way to take our obligations to people not yet born seriously. He does it at the level of outcomes, meaning how well or badly things go for whoever ends up living, rather than at the level of particular identities." This stands for the whole lineage run in "Restitution and foreclosure," where I stopped wanting the next paragraph.
