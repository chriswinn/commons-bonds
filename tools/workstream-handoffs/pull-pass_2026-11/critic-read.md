## 2. Questions for your redline (ranked)

1. **The back half is heavy after the climax.** The pocket lands at about 7,100 words. About 5,100 words of
   argument follow before the close (pattern 950, same object 1,100, who shaped 700, restitution 2,100). The
   critic's stop point is likely the middle of "Restitution and foreclosure," which has the naming defenses
   for both bonds and the lineage paragraphs. *Question:* are any of those better as a short note or moved to
   Ch 6 or Ch 9? No cut is proposed here; that's a structural call, and yours.
2. **The country ring is a third of the chapter (3,860 words).** 2008 plus its four objections plus Sweden,
   then Social Security plus its two objections. Each part pulls, but a reader reaches the world ring having
   read three long arguments in a row. *Question:* do the four 2008 objections all need to sit before Social
   Security, or could two of them move into "The pattern, named"?
3. **Does the pocket answer the opening puzzle, or just restate it?** "The same arrangement runs through every
   ring, and it runs through us" answers *where* and *who*. The *how* (the covers) arrives a section later,
   in "The pattern, named." The critic felt the answer was half-given. *Question:* is that the right split
   (sight in the pocket, the name and the mechanism right after), or should one covers sentence move up?
4. **"In your own country, the alibi fails."** It assumes an American reader. The book's horizon includes
   readers elsewhere, and Ch 7's other worlds. *Option:* "In the country I live in, the alibi fails for me,"
   which bridges through the author instead (lens 8). Your call: the current line is stronger for a US
   reader.
5. **Social Security as "the children pay for the parents."** This is your rings framing, and it reads well.
   A Public Choice reader will say pay-as-you-go is workers paying retirees, not children paying parents. The
   sentence says "across a whole country," which covers it, but flag it if Ch 9's AEI/Cato invitation matters
   more than the image.
6. **Butler details to confirm against the printout.** §3 of the Butler file says every fact in its opening
   is in the article. Four details aren't in its §1 fact table, so I couldn't cross-check them: **Connecticut,
   "upstairs, napping," eighty-five, and "a retired university professor."** Session 1 read all 11 pages, so
   they are very likely right. A 30-second check of p. 1 at redline closes it.
7. **"the term this book asks you to carry."** The handover of "severed cost" is the one place the prose
   tells the reader what to do with a word. The critic heard a faint announcement. *Option:* "It has a name: a
   severed cost." Plainer, but it loses the weight of the handover.
8. **"No one in the system was paid to say wait."** Your sentence, echoing Butler. A strict critic notes that
   the internist would have been paid $54, so someone *was* paid, just badly. "Paid to say wait" can be read
   as "paid enough"; the paragraph's own figures carry that reading. Leave it, or use Butler's exact line
   ("The system rewarded nobody for saying 'no' or even 'wait'") and drop one quotation.

- **No villain survives the read.** Butler's own "did their best," Gawande, the saint at the bright end of the
  supply chain, and "nobody has to choose that" hold the line all the way through.
- **The figures.** Every number traces to the base, the ledger or the Butler file. The two softened claims
  (the "only distance" line and BP–Mexico) no longer give a hostile reader a loose thread.
- **The relationship bridges.** Each ring lands on people: the mother, the asking family, the children's laps,
  the fishing families, the author, parents and children, the villages, the reader, our descendants.
