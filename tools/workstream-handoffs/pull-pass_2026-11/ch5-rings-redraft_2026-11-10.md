# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 5: The Accountability Gap

## The family

One October afternoon in 2007, the journalist Katy Butler was visiting her parents in Connecticut when her mother asked her for something. "Please help me get Jeff's pacemaker turned off," she said. Butler's father was upstairs, napping. He was eighty-five, a retired university professor with dementia, and the device in his chest had kept his heart beating for nearly five years. Its battery was expected to last five more.

His decline had begun with a stroke in 2001 that left him unable to finish a sentence. Butler's mother became his caregiver, and by the next year, at seventy-seven, she was on duty more than eighty hours a week. That winter a cardiologist would not clear him for hernia surgery unless he had a pacemaker. Before the stroke he had turned the same device down on the advice of their trusted primary-care doctor, who thought it was overtreatment. Now he could no longer weigh the choice, so it fell to Butler's mother. Exhausted from caring for him, she said yes, and the decision took minutes. The pacemaker kept his heart going while the rest of him failed. In 2006 she wrote in her journal: "My life is in ruins. This is horrible, and I have lasted for five years."

Nothing in the system had pushed the other way. Their doctor still opposed the device, and no one consulted him. A meeting with him could have covered what the choice meant: that the battery would last ten years, whether Butler's father wanted to live to eighty-nine "in his nearly mute and dependent state," and the option of a temporary external pacemaker that could have seen him safely through the surgery. Medicare would have paid him its standard office-visit rate of $54 for what would certainly have been a long meeting, and nothing for the phone calls needed to work out a plan with the other doctors. For the permanent device, it paid the surgeon $461 and the hospital a flat fee of about $12,000, of which an estimated $7,500 went to the company that made it. Butler believed her father's doctors did their best. But as she writes, the system "rewarded nobody for saying 'no' or even 'wait.'"

The surgeon and writer Atul Gawande has shown how a decision like that gets made. In *Being Mortal* he describes a payment system that rewards intervention at every turn and underfunds palliative care and the training that would let medicine attend well to the end of a life. No clinician has to choose extraction over compassion for the result to follow. Decent people respond sensibly to the incentives in front of them, and the incentives do the rest. Because those incentives were designed, they can be redesigned, and the behavior would change without anyone's character changing at all.

The device maker, the hospital and the surgeon were paid. The rest of the cost was paid too, in years of decline and in a caregiver's exhausted weeks, and it was paid by the one person in the house least able to refuse it. No one in that room chose to send it to her. So how did it get there? And is medicine the only place it lands?

## The community

In Butler's family the bill came due in her mother's hours. In many families it comes due in money. A family that cannot pay a medical bill does what families have always done and asks the people who know it, and over the 2010s the asking moved online. By 2020 more than a third of the campaigns on the largest fundraising platform were medical. Between roughly 2016 and 2020 people started about 438,000 of them, and some 21 million donations brought in more than $2 billion.

Then look at what the asking returned. Only about 12 percent of those campaigns met their stated goals, which means nearly nine in ten fell short of what they asked for. Roughly 16 percent received nothing at all.

The platform's chief executive has said it was never designed to finance healthcare. It became the backstop by accident, filling the hole a working safety net would have filled. Families in Canada, the United Kingdom and Germany crowdfund medical costs too, but peer-reviewed comparisons find Americans leaning on it far more heavily as a substitute for coverage, and the analysis by Saleh and colleagues in *JAMA Network Open* in 2020 is one of them.

The asking also works worst for the people who started with the least. Studies find that campaigns run by people with distinctively Black or Hispanic surnames raise less money and are less likely to reach their goals, even after accounting for place and time. Kenworthy and Igra mapped the scale of these campaigns and who succeeds in a 2022 analysis in the *American Journal of Public Health*. Charity that runs on the size and wealth of a person's community sends the least help to the people whose communities had the least to give. The backstop doesn't correct the inequity. It launders it.

In some communities the cost reaches everyone at once. In Libby, a small town in the far northwest corner of Montana, miners came home from work with dust on their clothes. It went into their kitchens and onto their children's laps. The company that owned the mine let the town use its waste as fill for the high-school running track and for gardens, so the dust spread until it was simply the air the town breathed.

The mine sat above the town. W.R. Grace ran it for roughly twenty-seven years, from 1963 to 1990, digging vermiculite, a mineral that puffs up when heated into a light insulation that resists fire. Grace sold it as Zonolite. For much of its life the mine produced most of the vermiculite used in the United States. It made money, and by the measures a company applies to itself it was a success.

That insulation did not stay in Montana. If the house you are sitting in was built or insulated in the decades Grace was selling, there is a real chance Zonolite is above your head right now, a loose gray-brown fill lying quietly between the joists. Millions of attics still hold it. This case is not at arm's length. It is the distance between your chair and your ceiling.

The ore also carried a particularly lethal form of asbestos. Its fibers lodge in the lining of the lungs, and years later they produce cancers and a scarring that slowly suffocates a person. Internal company documents later released in litigation show that the contamination was identified early and that workers' exposure was measured throughout the mine's life. The mining went on.

Hundreds of Libby residents have died of asbestos-related disease, and thousands more have been diagnosed with the lung damage that marks exposure. In a town of a few thousand people, oxygen tanks rode in grocery carts beside the bread. Funerals for the relatively young were not unusual, and deaths from asbestos disease ran at many times the national rate. The Environmental Protection Agency eventually declared a public-health emergency in Libby, the first it had ever declared under the federal Superfund law.

Over its life the mine brought in revenue on the order of $100 million, an industry estimate that no primary source has confirmed. Federal cleanup of the town has cost more than $600 million, and in 2008 Grace agreed to pay back $250 million of it. Cleanup alone comes to about six times what the mine took in, and it leaves out the deaths and the disease themselves: the years of life lost, the hours spent caring for the sick, the futures that closed. No settlement ever fully priced those. Built from disclosed parts, the order of magnitude is plain. A company took in roughly $100 million and left behind costs many times greater, and most of them fell on people who never saw the revenue.

By then Grace had found its way out. With its liabilities mounting, it declared bankruptcy in 2001, reorganized and moved its obligations into the slow machinery of a bankruptcy trust. In 2005 the federal government indicted the company and several of its senior executives in what was then the largest environmental criminal prosecution the country had attempted. After a long trial they were acquitted in 2009.

The acquittal doesn't mean the harm didn't happen. The death certificates, the cleanup budgets and the EPA's own emergency declaration all record it. What failed was the fit between the harm and the law. A prosecution looks for an identifiable act, committed knowingly, at an identifiable moment, by an identifiable person. Libby's harm came from thousands of ordinary operating choices over twenty-seven years, each defensible on its own and none of them the single provable act the law requires. The law went looking for a moment, and there wasn't one. There was a process.

The legal scholar Katharina Pistor explains why a process wins. In *The Code of Capital* she argues that capital is not a natural substance but a legal artifact. It is assembled from legal modules: property, collateral, trusts, the corporate form, bankruptcy and the choice of which jurisdiction's law applies. Those modules create and protect wealth, and they also decide who can be reached when something goes wrong. Read through Pistor, the bankruptcy that capped Grace's liability and the acquittal that ended the prosecution were the system working as designed. The modules that make large-scale extraction financeable also let a corporation limit what it owes and dissolve the responsible actor into a process. Pistor shows how the law protects the cut, and the work of this book is to price what the law cuts loose.

Grace's shareholders and executives went on with their lives. The town stayed where it was, with the fibers in its soil and in its people's lungs.

Other communities are held together by the work they share. Along the Gulf of Mexico, fishing families had worked the same grounds for generations, and the knowledge of how to read the tides and the water passed from parents to children. On April 20, 2010, a well being drilled for BP in the deep water of the Gulf blew out beneath the Deepwater Horizon rig. Eleven workers were killed. For eighty-seven days, until the well was finally capped, oil poured into the Gulf, roughly 4.9 million barrels of it.

BP had drawn enormous value from the Gulf over the years, and that value was real. It was banked and paid out to shareholders. When the well blew, the cost went looking for somewhere to land, and it landed three ways at once. It landed across space, because the oil was refined and burned in cities far from the water while the poison settled in the marshes those families worked. It landed across time, because a quarterly earnings report looks three months ahead and oil lingers for years in a marsh and in the tissue of fish. And it landed on the living system itself. Toxins entered the small organisms at the base of the food web and the larger animals that eat them, depressing reproduction for years. No balance sheet was built to register what those creatures lost, because they cannot file a claim.

Here, unusually, the polluter paid, and paid more than in any other case in this book, because litigation forced it to. In 2016 BP put its own final estimate of its total cost at about $61.6 billion, and the figure passed $65 billion by 2018 as charges kept accruing. That total covers a 2015 settlement with the federal government and the Gulf states, a 2012 criminal penalty of $4 billion, the cleanup and the litigation. It still fell short. The continuing damage to the Gulf's ecosystems and fisheries is estimated at another $8 billion to $12 billion beyond what BP paid, and fifteen years after the spill those fisheries remain impaired.

Some of what the coast lost never had a price at all. The knowledge those families handed down broke where the fishery broke. Communities organized for generations around the water lost the thing they were organized around. None of that appeared on any document BP had to reconcile, which is exactly why it could be lost for free.

The oil did not stop at the border. Mexico's government sued BP over damage on its side of the Gulf, and in February 2018, after five years, it settled for $25.5 million. Of that, $15.3 million reimbursed the government's own monitoring and other costs, and $10.2 million went to programs to protect and restore the Gulf. Nothing was set aside for the fishing communities, and the deal released BP from responsibility for any damage in Mexican waters. The government had funded twenty-two scientific studies of the damage. It kept them out of its lawsuit and out of public view, and it published twenty of them only after the settlement, under freedom-of-information requests. The two from its own petroleum institute were still classified when the deal came to light.

So the families went to court on their own. In 2015 some 25,000 Mexican fishermen sued BP in the United States for compensatory and punitive damages, the first class action over the spill brought by people who were not American citizens. On the American side of the water, claimants could reach for the Oil Pollution Act of 1990, for state tort and environmental law, and for class actions that let many small injuries combine into a force a corporation must answer. Claimants in Mexico had far weaker tools for pressing a claim across a border against a foreign corporation. How much damage reached Mexican waters was never settled, and the studies that might have answered it were held back until the case was closed. The same oil, the same chemistry and the same fish met two legal systems. Pistor's modules are not abstract here. They decided whether a cost was counted at all.

Twenty years earlier the same story played out on another coast, and Chapter 6 gives it the full accounting. In 1989 the tanker Exxon Valdez ran aground on a reef in Prince William Sound, in Alaska, and spilled millions of gallons of crude into one of the richest cold-water ecosystems on the continent. The legal fight took the better part of two decades. It ended with a very large award of punitive damages, which an appeal cut to a fraction of what the jury had set. In the years after the spill the Pacific herring collapsed and never recovered, though it had anchored both the sound's ecosystem and the fishing economy built on it. The families who depended on it found no way to file for the loss of an entire fishery, because the law isn't built to compensate a fishery that simply stops returning. For most of the decades since, the season has stayed gone.

## The country

In your own country, the alibi fails. Every ring so far has left a comfortable reader somewhere to stand apart: someone else's family, someone else's town, someone else's coast. The financial crisis of 2008 took that place away. It struck the whole economy at once and reached into the retirement accounts and homes of people who had felt safe. I was one of them.

I was doing well at the time. I worked in sales, which gave me an unusual view of the crisis, because when the shock hit every company's finances at once, companies simply stopped spending. Every expenditure that wasn't already signed got sent back for review, and I watched demand freeze from inside the sales pipeline.

I had also done what the system told someone in my position to do. I had no pension. What I had instead was a 401(k), with a number in it that had been rising slowly, and I carried the belief that came with it: with no pensions anymore, that slowly rising number was how I would one day retire. When I opened my 401(k) in the middle of the crisis, the number had been revised to essentially zero. The crash had already wiped out the rest of my investments.

The rest arrived in a pile, the way these things actually arrive. A costly medical emergency hit my finances. My car broke down, and I replaced it by tapping my retirement savings early, which meant paying the tax penalty that punishes exactly that. The company I worked for failed, and I had to pay for my own move to another city to find work. Owning a home got postponed, and retirement got pushed back by about a decade. Together, those things reset me to starting over financially.

I'm not telling this because mine was the hard case. It wasn't; plenty of people lost far more and had far less cushion to lose it from. I'm telling it because of what it takes away. This is the case in the chapter where the alibi fails. Until now the cost has landed on someone else, somewhere else, but in 2008 it landed on millions of people who had saved. "I did everything I was told, and it reached me anyway" closes the distance more completely than "I was already struggling" ever could. The crisis didn't require anyone to gamble; it required only that they had saved, in the very instrument built for saving. So the question worth asking is not who was reckless. It is how that instrument came to be built so that a single year could erase years of ordinary prudence, and who stood on the far side of the transfer.

The 401(k) was one piece of a longer shift. The typical worker's pay came apart from overall productivity in the early 1970s, as the economists Lawrence Mishel and Josh Bivens have documented at the Economic Policy Institute. The economy kept growing more productive while the median wage stalled, and over the same years healthcare costs rose at several times the rate of general inflation. A family on a fundraising page lives in the widening gap between those two curves. The political scientist Jacob Hacker named the larger movement in *The Great Risk Shift*: the long, systematic transfer of economic risk off institutions and onto families. The 401(k) is one of its instruments.

It began quietly in 1978, when section 401(k) of the tax code created the legal scaffolding for a new kind of retirement saving. Over the following years American employers steadily replaced the pension with the individual account. Under a pension the company promised a fixed income in retirement and carried the job of funding it. Under the new account the worker saved and carried the outcome. It was sold as freedom and ownership. You would control your own money, you could change jobs without losing a pension, and you would own capital instead of depending on a promise.

What was said less loudly was whose freedom it was. A pension left three serious risks with the institution: that markets would fall, that retirees would live longer than planned, and that people would save too little or sell at the wrong moment. The new account moved all three off the corporate balance sheet and onto individuals, who cannot pool risk across thousands of lives or hedge a thirty-year horizon. The freedom that mattered most was the corporation's freedom from liability. For about thirty years the shift caused no crisis, because no shock had come along to show where the risk now sat.

The shock was built in a few years, roughly 2001 to 2008. The financial industry turned home mortgages into securities, then bundled and sliced those securities into ever more elaborate instruments. The complexity was no accident. Only an instrument too complicated to evaluate could be sold at scale to buyers who couldn't judge what they were buying. Some of those instruments ended up in the very retirement accounts the first shift had filled with ordinary workers' savings. So when the housing market turned in 2008 and the instruments collapsed, the losses landed there.

American household net worth fell by roughly $11 trillion, by the Federal Reserve's own count in its flow-of-funds accounts. The headline hides who lost it. In *House of Debt*, the economists Atif Mian and Amir Sufi reconstructed how the loss was spread, and by a wide margin it fell hardest on the households with the least wealth and the most debt.

Leverage explains why. Picture two homeowners. One owns a house outright, and the other put down a tenth of the price and borrowed the rest. If the price falls by a tenth, the first owner has lost a tenth of the house's value, which hurts but can be survived. The second has lost the whole down payment, and the equity is gone. The same drop that dents a wealthy balance sheet wipes out a modest one. Across the country, families with the thinnest cushions and the heaviest mortgages lost a multiple of what wealthier families lost. They didn't behave worse. Leverage passed the fall in price straight through to their equity. Mian and Sufi also show how differently the two groups responded: for each dollar of housing wealth lost, the households with the least wealth cut their spending by about three times as much as those with the most. That is how a shock concentrated on the indebted poor became a general collapse of demand and a deeper recession for everyone.

Now look at what the system could mobilize, and how fast. The Troubled Asset Relief Program committed $700 billion. When the Government Accountability Office audited the Federal Reserve's emergency lending in 2011, the cumulative total across its programs exceeded $16 trillion, and that cumulative figure is the best-sourced large number from 2008. Within roughly eighteen months, bonuses at the rescued institutions had resumed.

On the other side there were millions of foreclosure filings between 2008 and 2012, and by CoreLogic's count about 4.1 million of them ended in completed foreclosures, with families put out of their homes. The system found trillions of dollars in days for the institutions at the center of the crisis. For the households losing their homes it organized nothing of comparable scale or speed. In 2011 the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission concluded that the crisis had come from human action and inaction, from choices rather than forces beyond anyone's control.

That contrast invites indignation, and indignation is cheap. The case is stronger if it survives its four best objections, so take each at full strength.

The first is agency. Many of the borrowers who defaulted could never have repaid their loans under any reasonable assumption, and they signed the papers. Nobody held a pen to their hands. That's true. But agency runs on information, and the information around these loans was built to work against the borrower. The people who wrote the loans were paid by volume, not by whether the loans were repaid, which is an engine for writing loans that shouldn't be written. The terms in the marketing often differed from the terms in the contract, and adjustable-rate loans were sold to exactly the borrowers least likely to understand how the rate would reset. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's later enforcement record documents the pattern case after case. The borrower's choice was real, and the imbalance of information turned it against the borrower. Saying so doesn't erase the borrower's responsibility. It sets that responsibility beside the larger one carried by the people who built the imbalance on purpose.

The second says the crisis was global, not a story about American policy. European banks were heavily exposed, Asian exporters were hammered when demand collapsed, and mortgage securities were held across borders. All of that is true, and all of it strengthens the finding. The instruments that spread the damage were mostly originated in the United States. The fee-on-origination model that rewarded volume over quality was an American design. American policy permitted the conflict at the heart of the rating agencies, where the issuer of a security paid the agency that rated it. A poison that spreads worldwide is no less American in origin. Its origin only matters more.

The third says the public got its money back. TARP was largely repaid, and on some of the Treasury's measures it turned a modest profit. But TARP is the wrong unit of account. The question here is not the government's cash flow on one rescue program. It is the welfare lost by households put out of their homes, and the lifetime compounding lost by workers whose balances reset to a lower base and never caught up. It is the regional economies that shrank and stayed shrunk, and the trust between citizens and institutions that an uneven rescue used up. None of that comes back when TARP is repaid, and none of it shows up in the accounts the repayment argument cites.

The fourth says reform closed the gap. The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 was a serious response. It created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the Financial Stability Oversight Council, restricted banks' trading for their own accounts through the Volcker Rule, and imposed tougher capital and liquidity requirements. It is fair to read Dodd-Frank as an early and partial installment of the accountability this book argues for, and Chapter 9 returns to it as one of the entering wedges of a fuller architecture. But partial and reversible is not closed. The Volcker Rule was later relaxed and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau was later weakened. A door pushed partway open can be pushed shut again. It is not a wall rebuilt.

Underneath all four objections sits a quieter assumption: that the system did only what any system would have done. It didn't. The scope of a rescue is a choice, and a society can choose to rescue its households along with its banks.

Sweden did, in the 1930s. It faced banking pressure much like what the United States faced from 1929 to 1933 and again in 2008 and 2009. In 1931 it left the gold standard and let the krona fall, which restored export competitiveness and eased the real burden of household debt. It coordinated the rescue of its banks with the modification of mortgages, so relief reached households instead of stopping at balance sheets. Through the middle of the decade it ran public-works programs that put income directly into households, and in 1938 the Saltsjöbaden Agreement set the settlement between labor and capital that became the architecture of the Swedish welfare state. The comparative literature on banking crises, in the tradition of Charles Kindleberger and Barry Eichengreen, treats Sweden as standing proof that rescuing banks and relieving households is not an either-or. Chapter 9 develops the comparison. The United States chose otherwise in 2008 and 2009, and chose much the same again in 2020. The road it took was chosen, not forced.

The country holds one more compact, and it runs between parents and children. Social Security is the promise that each working generation will care for the one before it, so that across a whole country the children pay for the parents. It was built on purpose to say that a society doesn't abandon its old, and it still says so. It is not a fraud and not a Ponzi scheme. What follows takes aim at one feature of how its promises were funded, not at the program. The distinction matters, because a careless version of this argument is exactly the kind of thing that gets turned into a weapon against a program worth defending.

Social Security was designed in 1935 as a pay-as-you-go system, in which today's workers' payroll taxes fund today's retirees' benefits. For decades it took in more than it paid out, and the surplus was held in special Treasury bonds in a trust fund. Nobody needed to forecast the baby boom's retirement. The cohort was already born and counted, and the years it would start to retire could be read off a calendar decades ahead. By the early 2020s the system was paying out more than its payroll taxes brought in. Under current law and projections the trust fund runs out around 2033, after which incoming payroll taxes would cover roughly three-quarters of promised benefits, about 77 percent.

One figure does measure the bill honestly. The program's Trustees publish a seventy-five-year actuarial shortfall of roughly $22.6 trillion: the projected gap between the benefits promised and the revenue expected to pay them.

So where was the cost cut loose? Instinct points at the investment decision, the choice to hold the reserves in Treasury bonds instead of stocks. Instinct is wrong. Holding the reserves in Treasury bonds was prudent, because a public fund that gambled retirees' security on the stock market would take on exactly the risks a retirement system exists to absorb. The cut lives in how the promises were made, and there is a documented moment where you can watch it happen.

In 1972 Congress passed an across-the-board increase in Social Security benefits of about 20 percent. The same law created the automatic annual cost-of-living adjustment that has tied benefits to inflation ever since. The increase took effect for September 1972, so the larger checks reached beneficiaries in the first week of October, about a month before the November election. The payroll-tax increase meant to pay for them didn't take effect until after the election. The reward reached voters before they voted. The cost was scheduled to arrive after they had.

The 1972 law's indexing method also turned out to be flawed, and it raised benefits by more than Congress had intended. Congress corrected it in 1977 by raising taxes and trimming benefits. By the early 1980s the program was in trouble again, with estimates showing the retirement trust fund running out as early as August 1983, and that spring Congress passed another rescue that raised taxes and cut benefits once more. Each time, the bill landed on whoever happened to be working, paying in or about to retire when it came due.

The record is strong enough without embellishment. It doesn't require anyone in 1972 to have privately costed out the deficit of the 2030s and hidden it. It requires only what the record plainly holds. A benefit expansion was timed to an election, and its funding was pushed past that election. It was enacted while there were many workers for each retiree, and the eventual fall in that ratio was public arithmetic, not secret foresight. The people who made the promise collected its political reward while it was cheap to make, and they were out of office before the bill fully arrived. The funding fell to the workers of later decades, many of whom had not yet been born and none of whom could refuse.

It didn't have to be built this way. The 1935 design was one option among several. Chapter 4 described Norway's sovereign wealth fund, which puts the country's oil and gas rents into a compounding fund for future generations instead of spending them as they arrive. Both countries faced the same question of what one generation owes the next. They answered it with different architectures and got different outcomes, and national wealth and demography don't explain the gap. A choice made at a specific moment by specific people does. Because a funded design was a live alternative, pay-as-you-go was selected, not fated.

Two objections deserve a hearing. The first comes from public finance, and it should be conceded entirely. Holding reserves in Treasury bonds is what a sound fund manager does, and exposing retirees' core security to the stock market would have added exactly the volatility a retirement system exists to remove. The objection is correct and leaves the argument untouched, because the argument isn't about how the surpluses were invested. It is about how the promises were made. A defense aimed at the portfolio cannot reach a claim aimed at the promise. The two pass each other.

The second says the gap is tractable. Raise the payroll-tax rate, lift the cap on taxable wages, trim benefits at the top or raise the eligibility age as people live longer. The arithmetic is conceded. The gap can be closed, and closing it is the responsible thing to do. But look at what every item on that menu does. Each one moves the obligation onto the people living through the years of deficit: the workers who will pay a higher rate, receive a smaller benefit or work extra years. None of it reaches the generation that collected the political reward for making the promise, and most of that generation is now beyond the reach of any adjustment. A gap that can be closed is still a cost cut loose. It is arriving on the schedule the calendar set decades ago, and the fact that the bill can be paid doesn't change who is handed it.

## The world

In the villages near Baotou, in Inner Mongolia, people report raised rates of cancer, respiratory disease and birth defects. Farmland around the processing plants has gone out of use, because the soil can no longer grow food.

The plants refine rare earths, the elements that make the strong magnets in electric motors and wind turbines. Their ore comes from the Bayan Obo deposit nearby, and this one place produces roughly half of China's output. China supplies on the order of 60 percent of the world's.

Refining them is filthy in a way the finished products are not. Separating the valuable elements from the ore takes large volumes of strong acid. It leaves enormous amounts of toxic waste laced with heavy metals and leftover acid, and because the ores are often bound up with thorium and other radioactive elements, the waste is mildly radioactive too. A conservative accounting puts the wastewater at roughly ten tons for every ton of finished rare-earth oxide, the low end of the published estimates. The waste flows into vast tailings ponds.

Here the cost is cut loose in every direction. The feedstock is paid for today, while the damage to health and land unfolds over generations, and over something closer to geological time for the radioactive waste. The value flows to shareholders and to consumers in rich countries with strong institutions. The cost settles on rural communities with little political voice and few ways to refuse.

The evidence from this far away is thinner, because China's information environment restricts what can be gathered and published about health and the environment around Baotou. Peer-reviewed work exists all the same. Zhang and colleagues documented the environmental costs of the rare-earth industry in 2022, and Zhou and Ge did similar work in 2021, both recording how much waste the industry makes and the burden it puts on the region. Nobody can give a tidy total for the cleanup. A figure of about $100 billion sometimes circulates, but it doesn't trace to any source that survives checking, so it stays out. What can be said is narrower. The industry's annual environmental cost has been estimated in the billions, its revenue runs on the order of $10 billion to $12 billion a year, and the share of that revenue spent on remediation has historically been a small fraction of the harm.

Rare earths also mark a limit. They are the book's clearest example of a resource with almost no substitute. When oil is drawn down, its rents can at least be invested forward, the way Norway's fund invests them, so that something is built up to answer what is used up. No such bargain exists here. No fund can rebuild a depleted deposit, and nothing engineered can stand in for these elements at the scale modern technology needs them. Where substitutes run that thin, the cost can only be prevented or paid. It can never be made good by reinvestment. At Baotou the limit of substitution stops being a parameter in an appendix and becomes a field of tailings ponds.

## In your pocket, and in mine

Now think of the phone in your pocket, and the one in mine. Rare earths go into their speakers and screens, as they go into the motor of an electric car and the generator of a wind turbine. Wealthy economies treat the devices built from them as ordinary, and more and more as clean. The tailings ponds sit in Inner Mongolia, out of sight of the engineer who designs the phone and the household that charges it.

That doesn't make you or me a villain, and that is the center of the case rather than a softening of it. We share in the value the extraction produced, and the whole length of the supply chain stands between us and its cost, so private virtue or guilt has nothing to do with it. A saint could stand at the bright end of this arrangement. The distance a global supply chain creates is not an unfortunate side effect. It is what makes the arrangement livable for those who benefit, because it keeps the people who pay where the people who gain will never see them. The mechanism doesn't need anyone to be cruel. Here it needs only to keep the cost out of sight, and a supply chain that circles the planet does that for a living.

With the phone in your hand, look back through the rings. In Butler's family the person who paid was in the same house. In Libby the people who paid were in the kitchens where the dust came home. In the Gulf they were out on the water, on both sides of the border. In 2008 I was one of them. In Social Security many of them are not yet old enough to work, and at Baotou they live in villages most of us will never see. In every ring so far the reader has stood near the people who pay, and sometimes among them. The phone shows the other side. In some ring of every life we are also among the people who gain, and nobody has to choose that for it to happen. That is how a cost finds the person least able to refuse it when no one in the room chose to send it there. The same arrangement runs through every ring, and it runs through us.

## The pattern, named

Lay the cases side by side and one skeleton shows through all of them. Something shared was drawn on as though it cost nothing: a mineral seam, a coastline, a fishery, a supply chain, a nation's savings, a promise between generations, a dying body.

It works like this. A company, a generation or a government draws value from something it depends on but did not produce. That might be a mineral seam, a fishery, a river or the air, or it might be the labor of others, the trust between citizens or the future itself. Drawing the value creates a cost, and the cost is then cut loose from the people who benefited and pushed onto people with less power, less mobility and less ability to refuse.

The cost comes due later. By then the mine has closed. The company has reorganized. The executives have cashed out. The politicians who made the promises have left office. The cost stays with whoever was left standing where it landed.

The one reliable thing about this pattern is that the bill comes due. It is never cancelled, only deferred and handed to someone else, so the only open question is who ends up holding it.

What you have been watching in every ring has a name. It is a severed cost: a cost cut loose from the party that drew the value and left with whoever could not refuse it. The weeks Butler's mother spent on duty were a severed cost. So were the lungs of Libby, the Gulf's lost seasons, the savings erased in 2008 and the farmland around Baotou. Once you can name it, you start to see it everywhere.

The word has a second meaning that could mislead. Severance is also the check a company writes when it lets a worker go, and a reader who hears that meaning first might suspect the term carries the commodified view of labor this book resists. It does the opposite. Both meanings name a cut: the worker cut loose from the wage, and the cost cut loose from the party that caused it. The book keeps the word because the cut is the thing being described. A gentler word would do in language what the accounting does in fact, which is make the cut disappear.

How does the party that gains slip the bill? Chapter 7 names the full set of covers, and a few are enough here. Distance keeps the tailings at Baotou half a world from the people charging their phones. Time let a Congress in 1972 collect the reward for a promise whose bill falls due in the 2030s. A gap in knowledge separated the company that measured the asbestos from the families who breathed it. A gap in who can leave means capital moves and a poisoned town cannot. Routine lets a dying fishery or a family's bankruptcy fade into background noise. And abundance makes a real cost look like none at all, at least for a while: cheap insulation, cheap electronics, a rising retirement balance.

Then call the roll of those who absorbed the cost. A town that could not move. Fishing communities that could not relocate. Villages with no way out. Foreclosed households, and future taxpayers who cannot opt out of what they will inherit. Families whose fundraising pages fall short, and a woman whose eighty-hour weeks of caregiving appear on no ledger. In every case the party that gained departed: the company went bankrupt, the politicians left office, the generation that made the promise grew old. In every case the cost stayed with whoever could not escape it.

Stated without a villain, the claim is this. These cases don't show a system failing. They show a system doing what it was built to do. No one has to intend harm for the pattern to appear, because the structures reward whoever draws the value and push the cost onto whoever is least able to resist.

None of this is a new insight. In *The Social Costs of Private Enterprise*, published in 1950, K. William Kapp argued that shifting costs onto third parties is a routine feature of private enterprise, not an occasional failure at the edges of the market. The traditions of political ecology and environmental justice that grew up afterward documented who those third parties reliably are. In the work of scholars such as Joan Martínez-Alier and Robert Bullard the answer is consistent: the poor, the rural and the politically voiceless. The diagnosis is theirs.

Existing tools come close to what this book adds without quite reaching it. After Deepwater Horizon, regulators assessed the Gulf with a method called natural-resource damage assessment, which works only after the harm is done. In 1990 Robert Costanza and Charles Perrings proposed posting a bond in advance. That is the right timing, but they set the bond against the worst damage that could possibly occur, not against the specific cost a given extraction severs. That specific cost, priced for a single extraction, is this framework's target.

Kapp and those who followed him showed who is left holding the bill. What this book adds is the amount written on it, posted as a reserve before the bill comes due.

## One kind of thing

Ask what each of these cases drew on. A reef, the atmosphere, a community of fishing families, a body of human labor and a generation not yet born look like entirely different kinds of things. Define a commons by its structure rather than its substance, though, and they become one kind of thing. A commons is anything people depend on and share that was never produced as a commodity for sale. Nobody manufactured a reef. It was inherited. Nobody built the atmosphere to a specification. A community's knowledge and trust were never made to be sold, the care that raises the next generation was never produced as a market good, and a future generation is the purest case of something inherited rather than made.

By that definition the reef, the atmosphere, the community and the unborn are the same object: a commons whose abundance hid the fact that drawing on it carried a cost. That is why one apparatus can price all of them. It prices forward, against a future that is being foreclosed, and backward, against extraction from communities that has already happened.

Pricing any of these invites a serious objection, and the philosopher Michael Sandel has made it as well as anyone in *What Money Can't Buy*. Sandel warns that pricing a sacred landscape, a community or the unborn launders the priceless into a transaction. It concedes the extractor's frame, in which everything is interchangeable and everything has a number. Worse, a price can make a harm legitimate by turning a violation into a fee, something you may do as long as you pay. That worry has to be met.

The answer turns on what the alternative to pricing really is. The choice is not between a sacred thing left unpriced and a sacred thing reduced to a number. These things already have a number, and the number is zero. Zero is what the extractive accounting assigned to the reef, the community and the future, and zero is what made the extraction profitable. A reef destroyed at a cost of zero is precisely how the extraction got financed.

The framework refuses that zero. It says the cost was never zero and sets out to say what it is. Pricing something to sell it and pricing something to reserve it are different acts that happen to share a word. Pricing to sell turns a forest into board feet. Pricing to reserve names what the forest is worth, so that its destruction can no longer be booked as free.

That refusal has an ancestor. In 1944 Karl Polanyi published *The Great Transformation*, a book built around what he called the fictitious commodity. Polanyi argued that the market treats labor, land and money as if they had been produced for sale, when none of them was. Labor is human life, land is nature and money is a token of social trust. Running a society as though they were ordinary goods does violence to them and to the people and places they are.

This book's definition of a commons descends from that thesis, and so does the chapter's basic test: imagine the commons gone and ask what cost appears. Polanyi's deepest idea was the double movement. When a society has something turned into a market good out from under it, it pushes back and binds that thing to social obligation again. For Polanyi the double movement was a tendency he could describe in history. Here it becomes an instrument.

The closest living ally for this argument is Nancy Fraser, and her work repays a debt this book took on several chapters ago. Fraser distinguishes exploitation from expropriation. Exploitation takes from workers through the wage, through a contract, however unequal. Expropriation takes without even the form of a contract. It is capital's reliance on the unpaid care work that keeps a society going, and on the resources and sinks of nature, which sit outside the wage relation and are simply taken. Fraser maps this ground in her essay "Behind Marx's Hidden Abode" and in her book *Cannibal Capitalism*.

The reef, the community's knowledge and the caregiver's labor were all taken without a contract. They are expropriation in Fraser's exact sense. So are the options of a future generation, which can never be party to any contract at all. Chapter 2 left a thread loose where it traced the word severance, and this is where it ties off. The name that thread was waiting for is Fraser's.

One caution answers the sharpest hostile reading of the whole project. A critic might say that pricing the commons is financialization by another route, with the commons repackaged as an asset class for someone to trade. Polanyi and Fraser would not endorse putting a price on nature, and this book doesn't claim they would. Their tradition refused to treat nature as an asset. The framework stands inside that refusal, because accounting for a cost is a different act from creating an asset to trade.

The most forceful living version of this worry belongs to Brett Christophers, whose work traces how a financialized economy misprices whatever it turns into a tradable asset. A later chapter takes up his argument in full. The answer in principle comes from a test Katharina Pistor supplies.

In *The Code of Capital* Pistor names four legal attributes that turn an ordinary asset into capital. Priority lets a claim rank ahead of others. Durability lets it keep that rank over time. Universality makes it binding on everyone, and convertibility lets its holder cash it back into state money. An accountability bond is built to lack the two attributes that let capital circulate. It is not convertible: it can't be redeemed for money or for anything except the discharge of the specific liability it records. It is not transferable: it can't be sold, traded or repackaged into a security. A debt that can only be paid and never traded is the opposite of a coded asset. That is the difference between creating an asset and accounting for a cost, shown in the code's own terms.

## Who shaped the system?

The ledger shows where the cost landed, but it doesn't show who built the chute it slid down. This book's apparatus accounts for who bore the cost. Beside it sits a separate question: who shaped the architecture that sent the cost there?

A rigorous toolkit for that question already exists, and it would be pride not to use it. James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock built the Public Choice tradition to analyze how concentrated interests shape institutions to serve themselves at a diffuse cost to everyone else. Its engine is Tullock's analysis of rent-seeking, the spending of real resources to capture a privilege rather than to create value. Mancur Olson described the imbalance underneath it in 1965. Concentrated interests organize, diffuse interests don't, and the rules drift toward whoever shows up.

Where the people who extract the value and the people who bear the cost are different populations, as they are in most of these cases, the chain runs cleanly. Identify the interests that shaped the institutions. Trace the lobbying and the coalitions behind the design. Then ask whom the design serves and whom it costs. Run that chain across this chapter and a roster appears.

At Libby, internal company documents released in litigation show lobbying and an effort to delay the regulation of asbestos. In the Gulf, offshore drilling was overseen by the Minerals Management Service, a textbook case of a captured regulator. Its staff had accepted gifts and travel from the industry, and people moved back and forth between the agency and the companies it was supposed to oversee. The Interior Department's own inspector general had reported on the capture before the disaster, and after the blowout the agency was broken up and reorganized.

The deregulation that made 2008 possible came in three main pieces. The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999 took down the wall between commercial and investment banking. The Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000 exempted derivatives from regulation. A series of federal rulings pushed aside state consumer protections. Financial-industry lobbying shaped all three, as the Center for Responsive Politics and congressional hearings documented at the time.

In healthcare, the pharmaceutical industry has consistently led American industries in lobbying spending, and for decades Medicare was barred from negotiating the prices of the drugs it paid for. That ban was loosened only recently. It is as clean a rent-seeking outcome as the literature contains. Chapter 8 adds the coal economy of McDowell County, West Virginia, where an industry spent generations shaping a state's laws and regulations to its own advantage.

The chain doesn't run cleanly everywhere. At Baotou, global consumer demand shapes the extraction as much as any lobby does, and the actors blur together. In Social Security, the people who will bear the future cost were partly the payers of an earlier era, so the dynamic across time doesn't reduce to a roster of interests. Where the chain blurs, the account of who bore the cost carries the weight alone, because it doesn't depend on finding a lobby.

The use of this toolkit is easy to misread. This book draws on the vocabulary of rent-seeking without endorsing Buchanan's larger political project, much of which points where this book doesn't go. The tools belong to the toolkit, not to a camp. The framework's ledger of who bore the cost is exactly what Public Choice's analysis of who shaped the architecture needs, and neither swallows the other. Readers who want the architecture side in full should read Buchanan, Tullock and their successors directly. They will find that the vocabulary of rent-seeking survives many readings of Buchanan's broader commitments.

Chapter 9 develops this pairing at length. It is a deliberate invitation to readers at the American Enterprise Institute, Cato, Mercatus and George Mason University, where the Public Choice tradition is most at home. The ledger this book builds is the natural complement to the rent-seeking analysis they already do, and the two halves are stronger joined.

## Restitution and foreclosure

If all of this is one mechanism, what would honest accounting for it require? The framework's answer is a pair of instruments. Chapter 6 supplies their method and Chapter 9 their politics, and this section names them and the lineage they stand on.

The gap has two faces in time. One is harm already done: the lungs of Libby, the marshes of the Gulf, the herring of Prince William Sound, the foreclosed households of 2008. The other is harm not yet done. It is the cost current extraction is foreclosing right now for people who don't yet exist, as a habitable atmosphere is spent, irreplaceable materials are drawn down and options close. An architecture that answers one face and ignores the other answers half the problem.

So the framework proposes two instruments. The Restitution Bond discharges harm already done. It looks backward, and it is owed to particular people and communities who exist and have been hurt. The Foreclosure Bond discharges the cost current extraction imposes on the future. It looks forward, and it protects outcomes for people who don't exist yet. Together they make up the aggregate accountability bond the framework requires. Each is measured against its own face of the cost: the Restitution Bond against damage already done, and the Foreclosure Bond against the remaining value of the commons minus whatever has already been posted to protect it.

Why bonds? A tax goes into general revenue, with nothing tying the amount to a specific future obligation. A fee buys permission to proceed, which makes it a toll and not a remedy. A fund pools money without discharging any particular liability. A bond is posted before the full cost is borne, it carries forward across the long tail of that cost, and it discharges something you can observe: a specific posted amount against a specific identified liability.

That structure already exists, which is part of why the word is honest. Mining companies post reclamation bonds under the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977 against the cost of restoring the land. Oil and gas companies post decommissioning bonds against the future cost of closing a well. Norway's sovereign fund works like a bond at national scale, holding capital against future need. The economics of reparations treats a historical injustice as a documented debt to be discharged, and the civic-republican obligation to future generations finds its natural accounting form in a bond. The word gathers all of these together.

The backward instrument is called Restitution, and the name places it inside a tradition, not beside it. William Darity Jr. and A. Kirsten Mullen define reparations as "a program of acknowledgment, redress, and closure for a grievous injustice." In a 2024 essay with Thomas Craemer, Daina Ramey Berry and Dania V. Francis, Darity spells out the middle term: "Redress is the act of restitution, the specific steps taken by the culpable party or their successors to provide compensation for damages to the victims or their heirs." The Restitution Bond is built to carry out that act. It is not a rival to their framework or a new name for it. It is an instrument their framework already has room for.

Other names fail for their own reasons. Restoration assumes the harm can be physically undone, and many of these harms can't be. Indemnification routes the obligation through insurance, a frame the framework doesn't use. Damages collapses the bond into the measurement and loses the advance posting that is the whole point.

The forward instrument's name does similar work. A reader who hears "foreclosure" probably thinks first of 2008 and of a family barred from its home. That association is a continuity to claim, not a coincidence to apologize for. The word comes from the old *forclore*, to bar from, and it describes both events exactly. A mortgage foreclosure bars a household from its home through a legal architecture that lets the barring happen at scale. The Foreclosure Bond addresses future generations barred from what they should inherit: a habitable atmosphere, intact ecosystems, irreplaceable materials. The mechanism is the same in both, and only the scale differs, a household in one case and a generation in the other.

The alternatives miss what matters. "Future generations" reads as soft philanthropy. "Sustainability" has been absorbed into corporate branding. "Reclamation" is already a narrower legal term, and "conservation" and "intergenerational" both miss the specific act of barring that "foreclosure" names.

Now the lineage behind the two instruments can be placed. Pistor supplies the legal half of the diagnosis: she shows how law encodes and protects the severance, and the framework prices what the law severs. For the forward instrument's moral footing, Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) supplies the standing of future people. It offers a way to take our obligations to people not yet born seriously at the level of outcomes rather than particular identities. The framework leans on Parfit without claiming to have solved the non-identity problem he famously left open, and it names that problem as a standing limit rather than treating it as defeated.

Two more traditions stand behind the instruments. The civic republicanism of Philip Pettit's *Republicanism* (1997) and Quentin Skinner's *Liberty Before Liberalism* (1998) treats freedom as non-domination, and it does its strongest work on harms within a single generation, like the ones this chapter documents. John Hartwick's rule of 1977, that the rents from a depleting resource should be reinvested so that later generations are no worse off, is the forward instrument's ancestor, and Norway's fund is its cleanest application.

The backward instrument's lineage runs through the reparations tradition. When Ta-Nehisi Coates published "The Case for Reparations" in *The Atlantic* in 2014, he supplied the shape of the argument: name the mechanism, name the harm, name the debt. The framework extends that shape to communities built around extraction. The economics runs through Darity and Mullen's *From Here to Equality* (2020), the field's contemporary landmark and this framework's primary source. It also runs through *Umbrellas Don't Make It Rain* (2015), by Darrick Hamilton, William Darity Jr., Anne Price, Vishnu Sridharan and Rebecca Tippett. Their matched comparisons showed that the structural variable is wealth, not behavior: Black households at the highest level of education hold less wealth than white households at lower levels. The lineage reaches back to Dalton Conley's *Being Black, Living in the Red* (1999), an earlier statement that wealth is the variable that matters.

The method belongs to them as well. For the debt owed to the descendants of people enslaved in the United States, the pricing is Darity and Mullen's work, and this book defers to it. I should say plainly why. I present as a white man, and it would not be right for me to put a dollar figure on what this country owes for slavery. That accounting belongs to the scholars who have spent their careers building it. Their measures of the racial wealth gap and the gap in life expectancy price the legacy of a historical injustice, and they are the most developed backward accounting in the field. McDowell County's own gap in life expectancy borrows their method for a different harm. What this framework offers is a fit, not a replacement. It prices harm in both directions, and running it backward is what their method already does. The same apparatus that can meter an asteroid-mining colony's draw on a commons no one has touched yet can carry their accounting of a debt already incurred, on their terms.

The backward instrument applies the same matched-comparison method to communities built on extraction. McDowell County's collapse in wealth can be measured against comparable Appalachian counties that never depended on coal. Libby's health costs can be measured against similar towns that never had a Grace mine. Appalachia's opioid deaths can be measured against comparable counties where Purdue's documented marketing did not concentrate, and Chapter 8 runs that third comparison in full.

A current program in climate economics reached the two-way debt first. Andrew Fanning and Jason Hickel have calculated the compensation owed for the appropriation of the atmosphere, the Caribbean Community (CARICOM) has advanced a plan for reparatory justice, and the Loss and Damage Fund, established at the 2022 United Nations climate conference, began operating the following year. Each treats the atmosphere as a debt running back to past emissions and forward to the loss of a stable climate, priced on the same social cost of carbon this book uses, and argued at the scale of nations. The framework concedes the priority and adds only the grain: it prices a single extraction event and reserves against it, rather than netting one nation's account against another's.

One question remains open in the field itself, and this book does not presume to close it. How should coerced labor be priced, as distinct from its legacy? Darity and Mullen price what can be measured of that legacy, and the framework follows them: wealth gaps, gaps in life expectancy, broken transmission of knowledge, trauma passed down through generations. The direct pricing of coercion belongs to the people already carrying that question forward. The dispossession of Indigenous land and the cultural knowledge that colonization severed sit in the same territory. The method can reach them in principle, but the computing belongs to later work.

Where does bond-posting actually stand across American extraction today? Far below the remaining value of the commons it is meant to protect. The Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Research Service put the federal black-lung program's payouts at roughly $44 billion through 2009, the most recent authoritative total. That is a real number and a conservative floor for the program's full run, and yet the program's trust fund still carried $5.1 billion in debt as of September 2024.

The deeper finding is not that the instrument is missing. It exists at scale, and it is partly assigned to the wrong party. When the coal excise tax falls short, the difference is borrowed from the federal general fund, so it comes from the public and not from the extraction that severed the cost. Reclamation bonds posted under the 1977 law fall several billion dollars short of the reclamation they are supposed to guarantee. And no Foreclosure Bond of any kind stands against the obligation Social Security carries across generations.

This is the accountability gap, stated as a measurable fact rather than a metaphor: the bond posted is a small fraction of the cost severed. Chapter 6 supplies the method for measuring the gap, and Chapter 9 takes up the policy of closing it.

What is owed is a separate question from what any society is willing or able to collect today, and keeping the two apart guards against a misreading the whole argument invites. How to move from today's nearly empty bonds toward the real value of the commons is a political question, and Chapter 9 treats it as open rather than pretending to legislate a single answer. The method applies whether or not anyone is ready to post the bond. Its contribution is accounting, not advocacy, and under an honest count the size the bond would have to be has an answer in any political weather.

## The family, again

Come back, finally, to the room where this chapter began. A pacemaker kept a heart beating through years of decline, and at seventy-seven a woman kept watch more than eighty hours a week while no one kept a ledger of it. Butler's father died in the spring of 2008 with her mother at his side, and the family's request had never been granted. "Inside his chest, his pacemaker was still quietly pulsing."

Nothing in that room has changed since the first page. What has changed is that you have now seen the same thing in every ring around it. It isn't an analogy. It is one mechanism, seen at its smallest, and in the room the person left holding the bill was her mother.

The rings end where they began, with family. The people who will hold the largest share of the bill are our own descendants, and they haven't been born yet. They are farther away than Baotou, in time rather than space, and they cannot object to anything decided now. The cost of what is extracted today can be pushed onto them as a habitable atmosphere is spent, irreplaceable materials are drawn down and options are closed off. It is the same severed cost as all the others. The people who pay have simply been moved into another time, the one place from which no claim can ever be filed back. In the atmosphere, the person left holding the bill is everyone who will ever be born.

So the question this chapter leaves is not whether severed costs are real, because every ring settles that. It is the question a family would ask on behalf of its own children. What would it take to post the bond before the cost is severed, rather than chasing it after the beneficiary has gone?
