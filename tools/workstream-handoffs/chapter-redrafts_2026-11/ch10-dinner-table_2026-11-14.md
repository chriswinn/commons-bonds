# Chapter 10. Common Bonds

It is a long time before sunrise. The first thing that reaches me out here is not light. It is the sound of men going to work.

A diesel engine coughs somewhere down the line of slips, then catches, then settles into a steady idle. A rope that has been slapping against a mast all night goes quiet. Sound travels a long way over still water, so I hear all of this as if the water were carrying it to me, and in a way it is. The Fort is dark behind the slips. The Bay is gray, and it is turning very slowly into a slightly lighter gray. The channel marker blinks red on its own schedule, whether or not anyone is watching it. North of Newport News a tugboat is working, and I can hear it long before I will be able to see it.

I am sitting on the deck of a sailboat. The boat is in a slip on the harbor side of Old Point Comfort. I am awake because the work boats are starting up, and nobody sleeps through that. I wouldn't want to sleep through it.

This is the last chapter of the book, and I came out here to write it. For years I have come out here to do the things that matter most to me. The chapter is what I have to say. The morning is what I came here to listen to.

## The sentence

There are nine chapters before this one, and all nine of them say one thing.

Value leaves the place it comes from, and the costs of its leaving stay behind.

That one sentence is the framework. The nine chapters open the sentence up and show what is inside it. I tested it against the coal of West Virginia. I tested it against the oysters of the Chesapeake, half a mile from where I am sitting. I tested it against the rare earths of Inner Mongolia, the oil of the North Sea and the Niger Delta, the water of the Ogallala, and the water ice of Mars. Those are different resources, in different centuries, on different oceans. The same sentence turned out to be true of every one of them.

What I wanted was for the sentence to count for something. For that, it had to be built well enough that people could put weight on it. Everything else in the book is there to serve that purpose. So why did I need to write it at all?

## Why I came here to write it

I didn't set out to write a book. I was in middle age, and there were a few things I wanted to understand because they would not leave me alone.

The first was the ledgers. I spent years in rooms where the numbers were honest about everything except the one thing that mattered. When a cost landed on someone who was out of sight, it was simply not written on the page. The page still balanced, and everyone in the room agreed that it balanced. After enough of those meetings I stopped trusting a page just because it balanced.

The second was my parents. They live in Hampton, and they were getting older the way parents do, which is all at once and then steadily. Caring for them is work. No ledger I know of records that work, and it is real labor all the same.

The last was the way the whole thing was built. At some point I understood that the dishonest ledgers and the care nobody counted were not two separate troubles. They were one trouble, wearing different clothes. I had to turn around and look straight at it, and this book is what I saw when I looked. Once I was looking, I began to notice who else was carrying the same trouble.

## The room

If I let myself, I can picture all of them in one room.

There is a miner in southern West Virginia, pulling on his boots in the dark. There is a waterman from the lower Bay who has been crabbing since he was twelve. He runs a mechanic shop in the afternoons, because the water alone will no longer support a family. A woman in Baotou is there, and her kitchen window looks out on a tailings pond. So is a fisherman from the Niger Delta whose son will not fish the creek. A farmer in the Texas Panhandle is watching the irrigation pivots come off his field for the last time. And there are three more: a rural nurse, a warehouse worker on the third shift, and a long-haul driver who is thirty nights away from his own bed.

These people don't know each other. If they met, most of them would have nothing to talk about. The miner has never heard of Baotou. The waterman has no quarrel with the farmer.

But the same thing happened to every one of them. Each of them helped produce something valuable, and the value went somewhere else. The cost of producing it stayed where they live. Nobody has ever told them that they have this in common. They are kin in that one way and none of them knows it, and I wrote this book to say so out loud.

I add one more person to the room, because I spent a whole chapter on Mars and I am not going to be coy about it now. She is the administrator of a habitat on Mars, and ten thousand people depend on her. She has been asked to take water out of the ground faster so that the settlement can keep growing. Cost severance is not impossible on Mars. It is impossible to hide. In a sealed dome there is no away. There is no downwind. There is no later generation in another country who will absorb the loss out of sight. Everyone who would bear the cost is in the room, breathing the same recycled air. Everyone breathes the same air; everyone drinks the same drawn-down water; the cost-bearer and the value-capturer are in the same room by physical necessity.

The same mechanism runs on Earth. The only differences are that Earth's room is much larger and the lighting in it is poor. So what is it, exactly, that keeps the people in the room from seeing one another?

## What keeps it out of sight

The answer is distance. The people who gain and the people who pay are kept apart, and the cases in this book showed four kinds of distance doing it.

The first kind is geographic. The value goes to one place and the cost stays in another. The two places are far enough apart that nobody ever sees the gain and the cost side by side. That is how McDowell County's coal left the county.

The second kind is distance in time. The people who make the decision are alive now. The people who absorb what it costs are born later, and by then nobody can reach the ones who decided.

The third kind is social. Here the person who gains and the person who pays may be close together on a map. They can even work in the same building, as my colleague in Chapter 1 and his employers did. One of them still has no seat in the room where the decision is made.

The fourth kind is informational, which means that one side knew what was happening and the other side did not find out in time. The company measured the asbestos, and the family breathed the dust. The collapse of 2008 depended on this kind of distance more than on any other. Informational distance can last a very long time, and two things help it last. One is routine. A cost becomes normal so slowly that people stop counting it as a cost, and they start calling it the cost of doing business. The other is apparent abundance, which is the working belief that a resource will never run out. That belief holds until the math turns over. People believed it about the atmosphere while the atmosphere absorbed two centuries of carbon, and that makes it the longest-running illusion of them all.

Power works differently from all four kinds of distance. Power doesn't hide a cost. It decides who gets the cost. Capital can move away, and a poisoned town can't. So the cost goes to whoever is unable to refuse it. The pyramids were built that way.

The instruments we have each answer one kind of distance, and only part of it. A border adjustment closes part of the geographic distance. A sovereign wealth fund closes part of the distance in time. Disclosure requirements close part of the informational distance. A price on carbon does too, because it ends part of the pretense that the atmosphere is endless. Every one of these instruments is real. No one of them reaches the whole problem.

So the strategy is simple to state. Bring the person who pays and the person who gains closer to standing in the same room, because the closer they stand, the harder it is to keep the cost separated from the gain. That strategy needs an instrument that measures the distance truthfully. I built one, and a reader has every right to ask why it should be trusted.

## The model maker

My grandfather was the man my family called Pooh. He worked at NASA Langley, which is twelve miles up the road from this slip, and his job was making models for the wind tunnels.

He built scale aircraft and spacecraft out of wood, metal and resin. Engineers put his models in the tunnel to learn the truth about a full-size design that did not exist yet. His patented work extended the discipline past the tunnel itself. He also did small repairs in the workshop behind his house. I spent time in that workshop as a boy, and I watched him make a small true thing that could stand in for a large one.

His whole craft depends on one demand. The model has to be built so faithfully that reality will not lie when it is run against your work. A model that flatters the person who designed it is worse than having no model, because it fails in the air. In the air, reality does not negotiate. And the people who pay for the error are people who can't recover from it.

The framework in this book is a model of the same kind. I am a model maker's grandson. I work in a different shop, with different materials, at a different scale. What I inherited from him is what I used to build the framework, and I built it to meet his demand. Meeting that demand also means saying what the model can't do.

## What the model can't do, and what it can

It can't settle the discount rate. The discount rate answers a question: how much should a cost that falls on someone not yet born count against a benefit that arrives today? Economists have argued about that for decades, and they have not agreed. Nordhaus discounts the future at a higher rate and gets a smaller number. Stern discounts it at a lower rate and gets a larger one.

The model doesn't have to settle that argument. I left this point out for too long, and it matters. The framework reaches two results. One is that the severance is real and greater than zero. The other is that a fraction of a hard-to-substitute resource is worth reserving. Both results hold everywhere in the range between Nordhaus and Stern. A reader can discount the future at the steepest rate anyone defends, and the conclusion still stands. Weitzman proposed that, for the longest horizons, the rate should decline over time. The framework adopts his approach directly, and doing so makes its own estimates lower. So the model uses the most conservative discounting it can defend, and it still finds the cost.

It can't price things that are not measured in any currency. The ocean under the ice of Europa is one of those things, and a sacred landscape is another. The model prices what can be priced. It says plainly when something can't be. It never makes up a number in order to look complete.

It can't predict when a substitute will arrive. It can tell you that a resource is hard to substitute, and that losing it would therefore be costly. It can't tell you the year a laboratory will solve the problem.

And it can't settle the conflict between a need today and a need in the future. A community that needs the income now has a real claim. A future that needs the resource left intact has a real claim too. The model can show what each claim costs. It can't say which claim is right. That is a moral choice, and it stays a moral choice.

Here is what the model can do. It can make the second transaction visible. Every extraction is really two transactions. The first is on the page, and it is the price paid for the coal or the fish or the ore. The second is off the page. It is the cost that was severed onto whoever could not refuse it. The model names that second transaction and puts a number on it, in words a person can use.

Once you can see the second transaction, the arithmetic of preventing it is not frightening. Suppose a country reserves a fraction of a hard-to-substitute resource. The cost is only the revenue it gives up on that fraction. Suppose it captures the rents in a sovereign fund. The cost is the discipline to maintain the fund, and Norway has shown that a country can do that. A border adjustment costs administrative complexity. None of those costs is large when you set it beside the loss it prevents. The insurance is cheap compared with having no insurance, and that is most of the argument.

The framework's job is to make the work something people can imagine doing. The rest belongs to whoever picks up the instrument. And the instrument does not only point forward.

## Backward, and the account it can't reach

The model that prices what will be extracted in 2027 can also price what was extracted from a community in 1860, or 1960, or 1995. The accounting works the same in either direction. It asks one thing, which is whether the cost was severed.

What should follow from pricing a past harm? It could be compensation, or restitution, or structural reform, or some other form of acknowledgment. The framework does not answer that question. It insists on one thing only, which is that the accounting be honest, because an answer built on a dishonest accounting can't be honest either. The framework names the column and does not fill it. There are harms I have no standing to price, and I leave the figure to those who do.

There is also one account that the framework does not reach at all.

In 1868 the Treaty of Fort Laramie guaranteed the Black Hills to the Lakota Sioux. The Lakota name for the Hills is Ȟe Sápa. The Hills were named inside the boundary of the Great Sioux Reservation. Six years later the Custer expedition entered the Hills and found gold. Three years after that, in February of 1877, an Act of Congress took the Hills away. That Act was the formal severance of a sacred landscape.

In 1980 the Supreme Court ruled on that taking, in a case called *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians*. The Court held that the 1877 taking had been unconstitutional. It awarded roughly $17.5 million, which was the value of the land in 1877, plus interest since 1877 at five percent. By 1980 the award had grown to something like $106 million. The Lakota refused the money. It has sat in escrow ever since, drawing interest, and the trust account now holds more than $1.5 billion. They have refused it for more than four decades.

The framework can't reach this account. Money does not substitute for that land. It doesn't at any amount, and it doesn't for any purpose. The Black Hills belong to the category of things the model can't price. The framework assigns no number to that taking, and I do not, and I do not say who else may.

The framework operates inside a settlement that began with a wound. It can't heal that wound, and it does not pretend to. The miner pulling on his boots and the waterman in his shop are not in the same position as the descendants of the people who held Ȟe Sápa under treaty before the gold was found. I am not ranking the harms when I say that. I am describing them accurately.

If you want to understand what the framework can't reach, read the people whose question it is. They include Robin Wall Kimmerer, of the Citizen Potawatomi; Leanne Betasamosake Simpson, of the Michi Saagiig Nishnaabeg; Glen Coulthard, of the Yellowknives Dene; Roxanne Dunbar-Ortiz; and Vine Deloria Jr., of the Standing Rock Sioux. Their question has a different shape from the one this book asks, and it goes deeper.

A reader could fairly object to what I have just done. I have set Indigenous dispossession beside the collapse of a fishery and the tailings of a mine, and I have called them cases of one mechanism. That risks flattening something that should not be flattened, and I take the risk seriously. The framework is making a claim about structure. It is not claiming that the harms are morally equal. What was done to Indigenous peoples in North America was not an aberration of the American system. It was that system operating as it was designed to operate, visible at its most severe. Saying that the mechanism is general does not make the particular wound smaller. It makes clear what kind of wound it is. The Black Hills account is the one that holds the framework honest, and it is not the only test the framework has to meet.

## Three objections

Out here before light, there is no one to perform for, and I can hear the objections to this book more clearly than I can at a desk. Three of them are real attacks on what I have built.

The first comes from my allies, and that is why it is sharp. A reader on the left sees what I see in these cases, and that reader will tell me the book does not go far enough. Honest pricing, strategic reserves and accountability bonds will not save us, the argument goes. The system's own logic drives it to extract until nothing is left. Such a system will absorb the reform and keep extracting, a little more slowly and with a cleaner conscience. The framework is exactly the kind of thing a carbon-neutral multinational adopts so that it can justify carrying on. What is needed is a different relationship to the resource, and a better price is not a different relationship.

The people who say this are right about what they see. The framework can be captured, which means the people it was meant to hold to account can take it over and use it for themselves. It can be softened into an ESG line in a quarterly report. That has happened to carbon pricing. It has happened to sovereign wealth funds, and to reclamation bonds in almost every place they exist. Think of the conflict-free certificate that delivers a premium to the retailer and nothing to the miner. That is the pattern, and I have watched it run.

But this book is not an argument for capitalism, and it is not an argument against it. The model measures what extraction costs. A state-owned mine that severs costs is still severing costs. So is a worker cooperative that does it. Feudal lords severed costs. The pharaohs severed costs to raise the pyramids, and the Qin emperors severed them too. So the mechanism does not belong to any one form of ownership, and it is older than the form my critic wants to blame. Capitalism is the vehicle that delivers the mechanism now, and it is the most efficient one yet built. It is not the cause. If we treat it as the cause, we do the same damage as when we treat it as the cure. Either way the conversation moves into political vocabulary, and the mechanism keeps running underneath the argument.

The second objection goes deeper than capture, and I have thought about it more than any other. It says the instrument is the disease. On this view, when I price the commons and write the obligation down as a bond, I am carrying the market into the last things it had not reached. A reader with Polanyi in hand will say I have manufactured one more fictitious commodity. A reader with Pistor in hand will point out that a bond is one of the legal modules capital is assembled from. To that reader, coding the commons as a bond is coding it as capital. I met Pistor earlier in the book as a friend, a diagnostician on my side. The honest thing is to admit that the same diagnosis can be turned around and aimed at me. Christophers would add that a bonded commons hands the rentier one more asset to capture. And there is Michael Sandel, who is my ally everywhere else in this book. He warns that putting a number on the priceless already concedes the extractor's frame.

Chapter 5 answered Polanyi's readers and Sandel in full, and Chapter 9 answered Christophers. Here I give only the center of the answer. Accounting for a cost and coding an asset are opposite acts, and they happen to share the word "price." The bond does not create anything a person can own or trade. It records a debt, and it keeps that debt visible until the debt is paid. As for Sandel, the choice was never between a sacred thing left unpriced and a sacred thing reduced to a number. It is a choice between two numbers, because the thing already has a number, and that number is zero. Zero is what made the extraction profitable. Some things can't be given a number at all, and the ocean under Europa's ice and the Black Hills are two of them. For those the model says so and invents no number. I also concede here what I conceded to the first reader. An institution that wanted to could rebrand a commons bond into something tradable, the way carbon credits were. The design guards against that, and so does the tradition the design comes from. But keeping it from happening is political work.

The third objection comes from the other direction. It comes from my parents' world, from the conservative friends they have in Hampton. I respect it because they are real people and not a strawman. This reader says the framework is a Trojan horse for the state. Add up the accountability bonds, the strategic reserves, the sovereign wealth funds and the border adjustments, and you get a vast new apparatus of government control over private extraction. It is a central planner's wish list dressed as accounting. Price every externality and you have written a license for unlimited government reach. "Honest accounting" is the thin edge of a wedge, and the wedge ends with the state owning the commons it claimed only to be measuring. And the agencies will be captured, as the book itself documents.

My answer starts where my answer to the first reader ended. The framework legislates nothing. It measures what extraction costs. A society can do many things with that measurement. It can build a tax. It can require a bond posted with a private trustee. It can set up a market in tradable reserve credits, or leave the matter to a contract between private parties, or create a sovereign fund, or do nothing at all. Choosing among those is a political question, and the framework does not decide it. A conservative can take the figure and argue for a market instrument. A progressive can take the same figure and argue for a public fund.

Honest accounting is also a conservative value before it is anyone else's. A sound balance sheet requires every cost to be priced honestly. A hidden liability is a fraud whether a government hides it or a company does. So what the framework hands the center-right reader is a demand for honest books. Conservatives already ask that discipline of the state and of companies alike.

On capture, I concede the point in both directions. The regulatory state can capture the apparatus, just as a company can soften it into an ESG slogan. The left's example is the conflict-free certificate. The right's example is the minerals agency owned by the industry it was built to watch. My answer to both is the same. Every instrument runs the risk of capture. The first guard is the design, which accounts for costs and does not code assets. After the design, the guard is politics.

So I have friends on the left who will find me too modest about institutional reform. My parents have friends who will find me too generous to it. Both groups are asking the framework to take a political side, and it was not built to take one. The model measures. Taking sides is the reader's work, and that work has a deadline.

## The hinge

If you step back far enough, something becomes clear about the moment that you and I are alive in.

We are the first generation to know, with the numbers in hand, that the resources are finite. We are the first to have the technology for substitutes, even though the substitutes are partial. We are the first with institutions capable of doing something, scattered and incomplete as those institutions are. And we are very likely the last generation with meaningful leverage. After us, the costs harden into facts that no instrument can soften.

Carbon is the clock that is easiest to read, and it is not the only clock. The aquifer drawdown is a clock. The fishery collapse is one, and so is the loss of soil. So is the depletion of the hard-to-substitute resources that the renewable transition itself depends on. The decisions do not wait in the next century. They are in this century, and several of them are in this decade.

That is what I mean by the hinge. I am describing where the leverage is, and how briefly it will last.

## Two readings

You can read the title of this book in two ways.

*Commons Bonds.* Read this way, the title means the obligations a participant owes to the commons. The bond is the account that keeps the debt visible until it is paid. It is the instrument this book has built.

*Common bonds.* Read this way, the title means the bonds among the people the value was taken from. The miner, the waterman, the woman in Baotou and the farmer watching the pivots come off his field are kin, and none of them was told. They did not choose the position the mechanism put them in. But the bonds of a position you did not choose are bonds all the same.

The two readings are one reading. There is an obligation owed to the commons, and there is a kinship among the people who paid. Those are the same fact. The first reading names it from the side of what is owed, and the second names it from the side of who was made to pay.

## The morning

The first gray is in the sky now. The channel marker is still blinking, and the dawn has caught up to it.

Below me, the dog stirs at the foot of the companionway. She lifts her head, decides it is not time yet, and puts it back down.

There is a kettle to put on. After that there is a day, and the day is made of small things. There is nursing coursework, which I took up late in a working life, and I will sit with it at the table. There are hours at the hospital, where I am learning the bedside work that the framework has nothing to teach me about. There is desk work on the book. There is the call to my parents, who pick up on the first ring or the second. There is the tutoring office. The first-generation college students who come through it remind me what a window opening looks like from the inside. And if the day allows, there is an hour on the foredeck with a book.

Over the years, the line between my work and my life has grown shorter. I have tried to keep it short enough that my attention stays attached to the people it was meant for. That is most of what I have learned about how to live, and the framework taught me none of it.

## The compass

Camus closed *Le Mythe de Sisyphe*, in 1942, with a sentence I have carried for a long time. *Il faut imaginer Sisyphe heureux.* One must imagine Sisyphus happy.

Sisyphus is not happy because the work is finished. The work is never finished, and the stone always rolls back down. He is happy because the work is his, and he has chosen it. He walks back down the hill, seeing the stones and knowing the weight, and he puts his shoulder to the boulder again.

The framework is not the boulder, and it is not the hill. It does not make the slope flatter or the load lighter, and it does not grant rest. What it does is tell the walker where north is. A compass is not the journey. It lets a person who has to keep walking know whether the direction is right. The framework's honesty does not lift any of the work. It tells the walker that the work, when it is done, will have been done in the right direction.

The men out in the slips already hold compasses. I don't mean the framework, which they have never needed and never read. They hold something older. They can tell honest weather from weather that lies. They carry that knowledge in their hands when they need it and in their bodies when they don't. They did not need the framework before it existed, and they will not need it after it is gone. The framework is for the rest of us. It offers us the chance to see what the men on the dock have seen all along, and the chance to do something with it.

## Let us begin

Naming a thing is where the work begins.

The work that comes after the naming is long. It is institutional, and it will take generations. Most of it will be done by people I will never meet. They will use instruments I will never see, and those instruments will have replaced the ones I have described. This book does not finish the work. It insists that the accounting be honest, so that whatever follows can be honest too.

The light is full enough now to see the far shore. The work boats are out past the marker. Somewhere down the line a man I will never meet is hauling pots in water my grandfather might have known.

The kettle is on. The dog is up. The day is made of small attentions, and they are waiting.

Let us begin.
