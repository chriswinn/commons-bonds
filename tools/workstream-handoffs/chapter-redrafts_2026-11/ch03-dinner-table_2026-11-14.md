# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 3: The Waterman

---

The harbor at Old Point Comfort wakes up long before the city does. The first work boat eases out of its slip while it is still dark, in the last dark before the sky turns gray. A diesel engine coughs and then settles. On the next pier a man says something to a man on the boat. Neither of them raises his voice, because at that hour sound travels a long way over the water, farther than either man means it to. The Fort sits dark behind them. In front of them is the mouth of the Bay, gray water under a gray sky, with the channel marker blinking red. Somewhere on the far side of the channel a tug is moving supplies west toward Newport News. It is one more load for the expansion of the Hampton Roads Bridge-Tunnel, a project that has dragged on for years.

I live aboard a boat now, but I did not grow up on the water the way a waterman's son would have. When I went out as a kid, it was for a day of fun, and maybe the fish bit and maybe they didn't. For a waterman it is work. He is deep into it before dawn, and he stays out until there is enough aboard to feed a family and put money in a pocket. He comes back in the afternoon, tired. Some men come back with a hold full of crabs and some come back with nothing. The men who come home empty are quieter than the others, though neither group says much. They tie up, hose down the boat and walk to a truck. At four the next morning each of them will be at the slip again, or he will not. The Bay will not notice either way. The slip will notice, and so will the dock, and so will the man two slips over who has been doing this since before any of them were born. That is how the working waterfront keeps its books.

So what are those books not allowed to record?

---

I am writing from Hampton, Virginia, and I grew up on this bay. Hampton once held one of the largest oyster-packing operations in the United States. The firm was J.S. Darling and Son, and it took that name in 1884, when Frank Darling joined his father's oyster business. At its peak, 160 shuckers handled 200,000 bushels of Chesapeake oysters every year. The meat went out in iced tubs to dinner tables and restaurants in Baltimore, New York and other cities. The empty shells stayed in Hampton. Conveyor belts carried them out of the plant and onto piles in the back, and those piles rose more than four stories high. They were mountains that the town could see and smell from the waterfront. The Darlings also ran a factory at Factory Point in Fox Hill, where thousands of tons of menhaden a year were rendered into fish meal for fertilizer and oil for industry. Up and down the coast, people called Hampton Crabtown. In the 1870s a man named James McMenamin had invented and patented a canning process there. It put cooked Chesapeake crabmeat in airtight tins, and the tins were shipped to tables far from the Bay.

The meat left town, and the shells had to go somewhere. Most of them were sold and crushed into roads and driveways across the lower Bay, because shell was cheaper than rock for keeping a dirt lane out of the mud. My father's family's own driveway was paved with them. Some of the shells were hauled out to Back River and dumped there, and new oysters anchored on them and grew. That means people at the time could see what shell did when it went back in the water. Most of it went into roads anyway, because the other choice was to pay more for gravel. My father worked every summer to pay his way through college, and he spent one of those summers pouring concrete for the boardwalk at Buckroe Beach. The boardwalk was concrete and rock with no shell in it, and it still stands. The driveway is the part I keep returning to: the material a reef is rebuilt from, spread under a family's cars.

In the summer of 2020, Navy personnel and volunteers went to work at the Navy's own installation on the Hampton Roads shoreline. They stacked concrete blocks called oyster castles into the water. Young oysters need something hard to cling to, and the blocks give them that. So the towns of the lower Bay paved their roads with the thing that builds reefs, and a generation later the Navy is casting concrete to do the job the shell would have done for free.

The company shut down in 1979, and I was born in 1980. I never saw the shell mountains. Not enough oysters come out of the water now to pile them that high. Seafood is still pulled from the Chesapeake today, but it is nothing like what came across these docks then. Nobody has ever had to explain to me how a resource that renews itself can turn into one that doesn't. I have watched some of it happen here.

---

The neighborhood of Hampton I am closest to is called Fox Hill. It is, or was, an island fishing community. For most of the twentieth century almost every house there had at least one person on the water. My grandfather was an exception. He worked just up the road at NASA Langley, building pieces of a different kind of America in a different kind of building. The neighbors on either side of him were on the water, and the boys my father grew up with were the sons of watermen. My father did not become a waterman himself. But watermen taught him how to handle a small boat, where the fish were running, and what the smell of the channel meant in late August. My family is one generation removed from the working life, and it has never been removed from the community.

Most days, at most hours, the Chesapeake looks like a postcard: flat water, soft light, a bay you would put a child on. Ask any waterman about the chop and you will hear about the other days. On the ocean a five-foot wave is barely worth a comment. The tops of ocean waves run thirty feet apart or more, so a five-foot wave lifts a boat slowly and sets it down slowly, and the rocking can put a man to sleep. Waves on the Bay are a different shape. On the Bay a five-foot wave can come at you with only about five feet between it and the next one. That is a wave very nearly square, and a square wave will turn the hardiest seaman green. The boat built for this water is called the deadrise. It is the waterman's boat and nothing fancy, and the men who run them will tell you it rides the chop more steadily than almost anything else out here. There are no superyachts on this water. Those are somewhere else in the world, which says something about where the water's value went. The men who have worked the Bay for forty years know which days you do not go out. They know which days you go out but stay close to home. They know which days the sky is lying about what the water will do in three hours, and they know all of it without consulting anything outside themselves. The water gives them that knowledge on one condition, which is that they never mistake which day they are looking at.

It takes forty years of going out every day to know what the watermen know, and none of it is learned on weekends. It is learned on the sweltering summer days when the heat takes the air. It is learned on the winter mornings when the gale comes off the cape and blows through whatever you are wearing and shakes your very soul. The men go out anyway, because the houses are not paid for and the families do not eat when nothing comes home. Their rain slicks keep the water out but hold no warmth. So under the slicks they wear thick base layers and sweaters against the brutal cold, and every man dressed that way knows what the clothes weigh. He knows that if he goes into the freezing water he will not get them off in time to reach the surface. He knows it, and he goes.

A community that grows up around work like that takes a particular shape. When the watermen of a place like Fox Hill say they look out for each other, they are not describing a feeling. They are describing how the work is built. Take a waterman whose nets need rebuilding before the next season. He cannot rebuild them alone in the time he has, and there is no industry of net-rebuilders he can hire. So a morning is set. At three or four, when every man there would normally be on the water or on his way to it, the boats stay tied up. The pickups come to the place of whoever needs the nets. The men bring their own coffee, and they work until the nets are done. Nobody there thinks of the morning as a favor. Each of them knows, as surely as the sun rises and sets, that a morning is coming when the man who needs the help will be him, or his son, or his father. A community that fails to come on the day you need it failed long before that day. That is the bargain, and it holds for as long as the community holds.

The hardest part to convey, to anyone who has not been around it, is what happens when one of the men who said he would be there does not show up. Everyone stops, and the nets go down. Somebody picks up a phone and calls somebody else. Within ten minutes the whole morning has turned into a search for the missing man. Everyone there has done this work long enough to know that a waterman is always where he said he would be, unless something serious has happened, so serious that he cannot tell anyone about it. It might be his truck, or his health. Or he went out on the water at four-thirty and did not come back, which is why the water is the first place the other men go to check. The older men carry that possibility in their bodies the way a cook carries the possibility of burns.

This is the community Fox Hill was for most of my father's life. In my own life I have watched it slowly thin.

---

To understand what is happening on the Chesapeake now, it helps to start with what the Chesapeake was within living memory. I have lived near this water long enough to have known three rough generations of the men who work it. The grandfathers fished one Bay. The fathers fished a thinner one. The sons would have fished a thinner one still, if there had been a Bay left for them to fish. Across those three generations the resource has been visibly thinning, everywhere, in every season.

The oyster is the easiest place to begin, because the historical record is clearest for the oyster. In the late 1880s the Chesapeake produced something on the order of fifteen million bushels of oysters a year. By the 1990s the number had fallen to under two hundred thousand. That is a 99 percent decline in a century. Not many fisheries in the world have lost that much and still been recognizable as the same fishery, and there is a real argument that this one wasn't. In 1990 the boats looked much as they had in 1890. So did the tongs, and so did the dock at the end of the day. But the men were going out for a different thing from what their grandfathers had gone out for.

A 99 percent decline is not caused by luck or by weather. Its causes are well documented now, and they were largely understood at the time. The first cause was overharvesting. Dredges scraped the reefs flat, season after season, and they did it faster than the reefs could rebuild. The second cause was disease. A parasite called MSX arrived in the late 1950s and another called Dermo followed, and the oyster populations were already too weak and too thin to withstand them. The third cause was sediment. Runoff from farms smothered the young oysters in the places where they tried to set. The nutrients in that runoff also fed blooms of algae, and the algae used up the dissolved oxygen that everything else in the water depended on. Each of the three made the next one worse. There had once been reefs that showed above the water at low tide, reefs that ships in the colonial period had to chart a course around. Those reefs were ground down to flat bottom and buried in silt. An oyster needs something hard to set on. By the late twentieth century, much of the bottom the oysters had built over centuries had been ground into the same mud the runoff was laying over it.

The last chapter ended on the difference between two kinds of drawdown, and the reef is where that difference matters. A coal seam is finite from the day it forms. You can dig it out faster or slower, but you cannot dig out more than was laid down. An oyster reef is another kind of thing entirely. It produces more oysters every year, and it will keep doing so for as long as the reef itself is not damaged. Take some, leave the rest to spawn, and come back next year. If the harvest is done that way, it does not shrink from one generation to the next. You can do it, and your son after you, and his son after him, because the fishery makes more of itself.

What happened in the Chesapeake is what happens when people take from a fishery faster than the fishery can replace what they take. For a long while nobody can see that it is happening. The harvest is still good and the price still feels right. The boats come back fairly full most days, and there are good seasons and bad seasons the way there have always been. Then a season comes that nobody can explain away. By the time that season comes, the damage is already permanent, and the reason is the reef. All along, the harvest had been taking the reef itself along with the oysters, and the loss of the reef was written off as a cost of doing business. But the reef the oysters built was the bottom the next generation of oysters would have set on. When the reef is ground down, more is gone than this year's oysters. The bottom has lost its ability to make any oysters at all. From that point on, leaving the system alone is no longer an answer. Either somebody puts something back, or what was lost stays lost.

Elinor Ostrom spent her career documenting commons that did not collapse. She studied fisheries, forests and irrigation systems that communities had governed for generations without taking them past the point of renewal. In the commons she studied, the people who took from the resource also lived with what happened to it. On this reef, the people who took the value had stopped sharing the reef's fate.

Nobody marks the day a reef crosses that line. Decline on this water moves the way a sunset moves: you would swear it is taking forever, and then you blink. The men who go out the next morning go out the same way they went out the morning before. But something has been done that no decision of theirs can undo.

One of the oystermen my father knew, growing up on Fox Hill, was a man called Biggie. Biggie had the build of an oak. He was not tall, in the way some big men are not tall, but he was solid through the shoulders and the back. It was the build of a man who had done one specific kind of work for most of his adult life. He worked oysters, and he had worked them since he was old enough to be useful in a boat with whoever brought him into one. He had worked the same bars his entire life, and his boat was suited to the work. He went out before the day began. He came back when the bushels were what the bushels were going to be. You could watch him shift among his beds with one oar in the water, making the subtlest corrections with no wasted motion, and see at once that his family had been doing this for generations.

Every morning he went out, Biggie carried aboard a six-pack of Coca-Cola in a wire carrier. He brought no ice, no cooler and no lunch. The first time my father asked him about the lunch, my father was a small boy, still young enough to be honestly puzzled. Biggie smiled and told him his lunch was waiting for him in the water, as fresh as it could be. Once the sun was high and the work had found its rhythm, Biggie ate oysters as he worked. He kept a spot on the boat, near his body, where he set the oyster he was about to shuck, so that he never had to reach or lift his arm. Over a working life he had reduced the job to its smallest possible movement: a quarter turn of the wrist, the shell parting, the meat coming up clean. My father watched him more than once and tried to count how many oysters Biggie put away in a single day on the water. He told me, when I was old enough to ask, that he never managed to keep count. It was not that there were too many to count, though there may have been. It was that the eating was so woven into the work that there was never a moment when an oyster started or stopped being eaten. It was simply something Biggie was doing alongside everything else he was doing, the way breathing is. My father has told that story so many times that I finish it for him now whenever he tells it again.

Biggie has been dead for more than thirty years. He died before 1994, the year Virginia stopped its commercial shad fishery. The men still on the water sometimes use that date to mark when things were still working. Biggie went out with what an earlier generation of men had gone out with, and he came back with what they had come back with. The bars sounded under his tongs the way they had sounded under the tongs of the men before him.

The bars do not sound that way anymore. Biggie's body had worn the work down to a quarter turn of the wrist. Nobody is showing that motion to any younger man at Fox Hill, because no younger man at Fox Hill is going out to bars that would let him learn it.

---

The men who lived through this do not use words like renewable or regeneration rate. Suppose you sat with them on a dock, late in a season that had not gone the way a season is supposed to go. They would tell you what they remember. A grandfather went out of a place that does not exist as a working dock anymore. Their fathers ran boats named for wives and daughters, and some of those names are still on boats and some are not. When these men were small, they could stand in the bow on a calm summer day off the mouth of the Poquoson River and see the bottom. They could actually see it, the grass and the crabs walking through the grass. A child cannot do that anymore in most of the places on the Bay where it could once be done. The water is greener than it used to be, and the smell at the dock in late August has changed slightly. Their fathers came home with a kind of catch that does not come home anymore.

Think of a man in his sixties who has worked the Lower Bay since he was twelve. He does not need a stock assessment to tell him something has changed. He has been making the comparison himself, every morning, for fifty years.

None of this is nostalgia. The way these men talk is closer to the way you might describe a relative's slow illness, in small and exact observations, because they have been paying attention for a long time. What you feel around them is less anger than a patient, unsentimental clarity. They are not asking to be pitied, and most of them would refuse the offer. They are telling you what they have seen.

What they have seen is the margins disappearing. A good season used to carry a family through a year, and now it carries them through only part of one. When one species ran short, another used to fill in for it, and that does not happen the same way now. The crabs are smaller, on average, than the crabs they used to bring in. Their fathers could time the runs of striped bass to the calendar, and those runs do not keep to the calendar anymore. The water itself seems to have a different temper. These men have been making observations like these for forty and fifty years. That makes theirs one of the longest continuous records that any working person in the United States keeps, and they keep it in their bodies and their memories.

J.C. Hudgins runs the Virginia Watermen's Association and works the Piankatank River in the Middle Bay, and he has done the math out loud. *You're lucky if you can put $150 in your pocket per day,* he told *Maryland Matters*. *And it ain't no easy job. You're freezing your butt off. It's cold.* He has done the math for the buyer, too: *People aren't going to spend $20 for a pint of oysters. It's pretty expensive eating. I think people are feeding their families in other ways.*

So the men on the water knew years ago. What did the people who manage the Bay do once they knew it too?

---

The striped bass, which people on the Bay call rockfish, is the species that showed Maryland and Virginia what stepping in can do. That was in the 1980s, and I am old enough to remember it. The fish I loved to eat as a boy were striped bass, red snapper and grouper. What those years taught me first was how quietly a fish can disappear. You notice you are catching one of them less often. Then it crosses your mind that you have not seen it on a menu in a couple of years, and you put the difference down to a bad season or two. I understand now that the bad season or two was the last of the seasons when the fish were there in any real number. I also understand that those of us who fished the Bay went on taking as much as we could carry to shore. As the moratorium years came on, the watermen I knew as a boy were talking about something they had not had to talk about before. I knew them from the marinas where my family kept boats. I did not understand the politics of it then. What I understood, the way a child understands without being told, was that the men whose work depended on this fish had agreed to stop the work for a long time so that the fish could come back, and that they had agreed reluctantly.

By the late 1970s the striped bass population had collapsed. It was bad enough that Maryland and Virginia, working with the federal government, agreed to a moratorium, which is a complete stop to fishing for a set time. On the Maryland side it began in 1985 and lasted five years. During those years no one fished for striped bass at all: not the watermen, not the charter fleet, not the recreational anglers. It was a hard thing to ask of the people whose income depended on the fishery. Many of them lost a livelihood for those years, and some never came back to the work. But the moratorium did what it was designed to do. The spawning population recovered, and by 1995 the fishery had been declared restored. The men who had given up five years saw a fishery come back from a place very few fisheries come back from.

Those men also disproved a famous argument. In an essay called "The Tragedy of the Commons," Garrett Hardin held that people who share a resource cannot restrain themselves. Each of them, he argued, takes until the resource is gone, and the only cures are private ownership or rule imposed from above. The watermen did restrain themselves. They tied up their boats for five years, by agreement and at real cost to themselves, and the fish came back.

That recovery is part of why the present is so hard for the men who remember it. What is happening to the rockfish now is what was happening to the rockfish before the moratorium. Every year Maryland counts the young fish born that year, and year after year the count has come in below the long-term average. In 2024 Maryland's young-of-year index was 2.0 fish per sample. The long-term average is 11.0. It was the sixth such season in a row, and six bad seasons in a row cannot be put down to weather.

Allison Colden is the Chesapeake Bay Foundation's Maryland executive director, and she has watched that signal build across the years. *Maryland's striped bass surveys have made it clear,* she has said in public statements; *we're facing a looming disaster. Without action now, the Chesapeake Bay won't have a striped bass fishery in the future.* In a different interview she was blunter: *there are very few levers left to pull.* Many people in Maryland, she has said elsewhere, *rely on striped bass for their food, their businesses, and their futures. Without action, that will all be at risk.* Summer water temperatures are rising and the low-oxygen periods of summer are getting worse, both because of warming. Together they are *pummeling* the species, in her word, in the months when it can least afford it.

Chris Moore, the foundation's Virginia executive director, said the harder thing in response to the 2024 stock assessment. *Despite recent management actions, striped bass continue to struggle. It is obvious that conditions have changed and striped bass will not have the same recovery they had in the 1980s. Warmer water from climate change, habitat loss, and invasive species are all making it very hard for striped bass to bounce back.*

The men on the water did not need to be told. They felt it before the survey numbers came in, the way miners know what the dust is doing to them years before the X-rays come back. The surveys matter for a different reason. They force the institutions to admit what the watermen already knew. That admission always comes late. It comes later than the data would have allowed, and later than the watermen warned. In 1985 it did not come late, because in 1985 there was a political will to act, and that will has thinned in the decades since. The agencies that once could ask a fishery to take a generation off have lost some of their ability to ask.

So when the watermen watch the rockfish, they are really watching something else. They are watching whether the system that was built to manage the Bay can act on what its own science is telling it. So far, in this round, the answer has been mostly no. Catch limits get tightened, but in smaller steps than the science has called for. Emergency actions get debated for longer than the species can spare. The committees meet and substitute motions are offered. One side argues for erring on the side of conservation, and the other side answers that nobody is certain enough yet to act. When the proposal was to raise the harvest, the committees acted with confidence. Now that the proposal is to cut it, they have become cautious.

Bill Goldsborough was the fisheries director at the Chesapeake Bay Foundation through the rockfish moratorium years. He told *Smithsonian Magazine* how differently time runs for the scientists and for the watermen. *Our objective is to try to restore the health of bay and aquatic life so that it can support viable fisheries,* he has said. *We see that task taking place over a longer term [...]. But the watermen don't have the luxury of the long term. They are concerned about making their next boat payment.*

Some of the men still on the water lived through all of it. They gave up five years so that a fishery could come back. They watched it come back. Now they are watching it begin to go again, and no second moratorium has been called.

The rockfish moratorium was not the only one. In January 1994, a few years after it ended, Virginia closed its commercial shad fishery. The shad run came every spring, and through most of the twentieth century it had been one of the Bay's reliable fisheries. Shad live most of their lives in the open Atlantic. Each spring they come into the Bay to spawn, running up the tributary rivers to lay their eggs in fresh water. The Bay is the spawning ground for the shad of an entire region, so when the Bay's rivers fail the shad, the shad fail in the ocean too. The men whose fathers had timed the shad run to the calendar were still on the water when the runs stopped coming. They could tell you the spring when the nets in late March began to come up empty. The closure was meant to give the shad a generation off, as the moratorium had given the rockfish. But the shad runs did not come back after 1994. Scientists have not agreed on a single explanation for that. The one thing they do agree on is that there has been no recovery. In any working sense there are no shad in the Bay anymore. The measure that saved the rockfish was tried for the shad, and it came too late.

The blue crab is in a different kind of trouble. A crab does not build a reef, and it lives only a year and a half or sometimes two. In principle, that means the population can rebound quickly from a bad year if the next year is good. For a long time that made crabbing the most reliable seasonal income a working waterman on the Chesapeake had. The crabs came back every spring. The watermen tonged them, trotlined them and potted them through the warm months, and sold them to picking houses up and down the Bay. In the picking houses, mostly women picked the meat by hand. The meat ended up in restaurants in cities where most of the people eating it had never seen a crab pot in the water. Then the numbers turned. The 2025 winter dredge survey put the Bay-wide population at around 238 million crabs. That was the second-lowest level since the survey began in 1990, and it was the sixth consecutive year of below-average numbers. *The red flags are flying for blue crabs,* Colden has said, and she has named the cause: *with more than five years of below average crab numbers, it is clear that changing conditions in the Bay are undermining the current management of this important species.* She is saying that the rules are not so much wrong as written for a Bay that no longer exists. The committees that set the limits still meet on the schedule the earlier Bay kept, and the crabs are no longer on that schedule. The men on the water say it more simply. The crabs aren't where they used to be. They aren't running when they used to run. The bigger ones are gone.

---

There is a fish you have probably never eaten. It is small, oily and silver. It runs in schools so dense that when a school moves, it can flatten the surface of the water the way a passing wind does. By weight it is one of the most important fish in the Atlantic. It is the menhaden. On the New Jersey side they call it bunker, and in New England they call it pogy. On the Bay it is just menhaden.

You do not eat it because it is not pleasant to eat. Almost everything else eats it instead. It is what biologists call a forage fish, which means it sits at the base of the Bay's food web. The striped bass, the bluefish, the ospreys, the loons and the dolphins all come for it. It is also the catch of the largest single fishery on the East Coast. An industrial operation out of Reedville, Virginia, takes menhaden at scale and grinds them down into fish meal, fish oil, omega-3 supplements, pet food and feed for fish farms. The catch leaves the Bay in tank trucks, in railcars and on ships, and none of it goes to anyone's dinner table as fish.

That makes it an unusual case, because the menhaden is the food web. If enough of it is taken out, the rockfish do not have enough to eat in summer. Summer is when the rockfish are stressed by heat and low oxygen and are trying to put on weight. The ospreys are affected too. In some of the Bay's tributaries they have begun to fail to fledge their young. The people who watch ospreys suspect that the parent birds are not bringing in enough small oily fish to feed a growing brood. Some of them call what they are seeing across the Bay *a hollowing*. They mean that the bigger species look fine until you ask whether they are reproducing and whether their young are surviving. Once you ask that, the picture changes.

How much menhaden to take out of the Bay is the longest-running fight over fisheries policy in the region. There is a cap for the Bay of 51,000 metric tons. The Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission set it in 2017, and it was a cut of roughly forty percent from the limit before it. Conservation groups regard the cap as a floor rather than a ceiling. The industry regards it as a hard-fought concession. In recent years the commission's scientific committee has recommended reductions along the whole coast, along with precautions for the Bay. The management board has adopted reductions, but smaller ones than the committee recommended, and on slower schedules than the committee proposed. *Just as we were confident in increasing the total allowable catch when the science says we should,* Colden has said, *we need to be as willing to take reductions when the science indicates that that's warranted.*

Meanwhile nobody knows how many menhaden are in the Bay. Genny Nesslage, an associate research professor at the University of Maryland Center for Environmental Science, said it plainly to WHRO: *We don't know the abundance of menhaden in the bay at any given time relative to the coast.* Rob Latour, who studies menhaden at the Virginia Institute of Marine Science, said it more bluntly to the same outlet: *We have no data to really do this in a meaningful way, from a biological perspective.* In 2023 the institute drafted a $3 million proposal for a study of menhaden in the Bay itself. The Virginia General Assembly declined to fund it.

So an increase in the catch is adopted as fast as people want it. A reduction is adopted only as fast as the resistance to it allows. The watermen know that, and the ospreys are living with it.

More than any other fight over managing the Bay, the menhaden cap shows who the system is built to serve. Reedville sits at the mouth of the Great Wicomico River in Northumberland County. The plant there has employed several hundred people, in shifts that follow the seasons the menhaden run. The fishery does not feed people directly. It supplies ingredients to other industries. The customers at the end of those chains have no idea that the small oily fish at the base of the Chesapeake food web passed through a Reedville plant on the way to them. One of those customers is a person taking an omega-3 capsule in Indianapolis. Another is the owner of a salmon farm in Chile. Another is a shopper buying a forty-pound bag of dog food at a grocery in Phoenix. The value spreads out across a continent and across industries. The cost stays here. It falls on the rockfish, on the ospreys, and on the watermen who depend on a Bay that depends on the menhaden.

On the last day of 2025 a Virginia resident named Tonya O'Connor filed a 349-page petition with the Virginia Marine Resources Commission. The Reedville operation is called a reduction fishery, because it reduces the fish to meal and oil. The petition asked the agency to declare a temporary moratorium on that fishery in the Bay, to last until the agency produced science specific to the Bay. The petition's central finding was that the commission *did not have*, and *still does not have*, *a Bay-wide estimate of menhaden biomass, age structure, spatial distribution, recruitment trends, or localized depletion risk.* Its legal argument rested on Virginia's public-trust doctrine, and it cited three cases: *Commonwealth v. City of Newport News*, 158 Va. 521 (1932); *Taylor v. Commonwealth*, 102 Va. 759 (1904); and *Bradford v. Nature Conservancy*, 224 Va. 181 (1982). The commission held a hearing on April 21, 2026, and received 1,266 public comments. It voted six to two against the petition.

---

The fishing is only part of what reaches the watermen. The rest comes down the rivers. The Chesapeake watershed covers some 64,000 square miles across six states and the District of Columbia. The water that makes the Bay starts as rain. It falls on a corn field in central Pennsylvania, a soybean field on the Eastern Shore, a parking lot in Northern Virginia, a chicken house on the Delmarva Peninsula, a wastewater plant in Baltimore, a suburban lawn in Loudoun County. By the time that water reaches the Bay it is carrying everything those places have given it. It carries nitrogen from fertilizer, phosphorus from manure, sediment from disturbed ground, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, and whatever else the economy upstream has not contained.

The science of this has been understood for forty years. The Chesapeake Bay Program was established in 1983. Ever since, it has been measuring the loads of nutrients coming into the Bay, modeling them, dividing them among the states, and asking the parties upstream to reduce them. It has had partial successes and significant failures. Pollution from wastewater plants and factory pipes has come down in many places. But the Program's own science advisory committee recently reviewed the forty-year effort, and it said, as plainly as such reports are allowed to, that the cleanup is not on track to meet its goals. Agriculture has not been required to do what the science said agriculture needed to do. The political will to require it has not formed.

The science was right. The targets were modeled, the schedule was set, and the states agreed to it. Even so, in the places where the loads were largest, they did not come down at the rate the agreement called for. The reason is political weight. The farm economy upstream carried more of it than the fishing economy downstream, and year by year the decisions went the way of the heavier weight.

From the deck of a sailboat in summer, in the years I have been on it, I have watched the green of the water change in a way that does not feel like life. The grass beds I could see through the water in the early 2000s are not visible the same way anymore. The watermen have been watching for decades longer than I have. They know that the runoff reaching the grass beds in Mobjack Bay started as fertilizer on a field they have never seen. The field is owned by a farmer they will never meet, in a state none of their children have lived in. They know about the dead zones too. Those are the volumes of low-oxygen water that form in the deeper parts of the Bay every summer. A dead zone suffocates the bottom-dwelling species that cannot move away from it, and it stresses the swimming species that can. The watermen know the dead zones come from farming and building upstream, and they know they have never had any voice in either.

The watermen live at the bottom of a watershed, and the watershed's accounting does not include them. Everything anyone does upstream has to come past their boats. Nothing they do reaches anyone upstream at all.

There is a man on Fox Hill I am leaving unnamed here. He is alive and still on the water, and the question of how he wants to appear in a book is not yet settled between us. He is a large man, not tall, and he has been on the Bay since he was a boy. He comes from the same Fox Hill world Biggie came from, and his working life picks up roughly where Biggie's left off.

He is a crabber and a fisherman, and he has been one all his working life. The water near Fox Hill and Poquoson is the water he has always known. Across his decades on it he has watched the math change for a man like him. The catches have come back thinner. The costs of fuel, gear and the slip have gone up. Not all of the species he came up working are still running. None of this is particular to him, because every man still working this water has had to absorb the same conditions in his own way. What is particular to him is how he has absorbed them.

He still gets up at two or three in the morning. He still goes out and pulls his crab pots, and he runs them the way he ran them when he was younger. But the math at the end of the day is not what it was then. So he comes back to the dock by mid-morning, hoses down the boat, and goes to the mechanic shop he owns on the Peninsula. His name is on the sign. His crab pots are stacked behind the locked fence in the back lot, behind the cars he is working on for customers. He works the shop until five or six in the evening. What he calls this arrangement is between him and the people who know him. What it does is keep the family's math balancing, the boat in the slip, the license current and the man on the water.

He did not quit the water, because he could not. What he did instead took decades. On his own time and his own dime, he built a second economy that lets him stay. The older men lived by an accounting in which the crab and the boat and the long days were enough. That accounting does not work for him. He has filled the gap with the only thing he had left to fill it with, which is more of himself.

I have not asked him what he would say about all this, and I do not have his answer to put on this page. The accounting in this book is not necessarily the one he would apply to himself. This book counts what the surrounding economy has been accepting from him, and from many men like him up and down this Bay. It accepts those things in exchange for a continued supply of the seafood it has not stopped wanting.

---

He has a second trade to fall back on. Whole communities on this Bay do not.

Tangier Island sits in the middle of the Lower Bay. It is a bit over a square mile of low marsh and sand, and people have lived on it continuously since the 1770s. By some accounts the accent spoken there is closer to a colonial-era English than to anything else still spoken in North America. The island's economy has always been the water, crabs in the warm months and oysters in the cold ones. Its rhythms and its vocabulary have been carried through eight generations of the island's families. The island is also losing its shoreline to erosion, at nearly two feet a year on the long-term average. The projections suggest most of the island will be uninhabitable within the lifetime of the children now in its school. The mayor and the men on the docks have asked, repeatedly, for the engineering that would buy them another few decades. So far, most of what they have asked for has not come at the scale that would matter.

James "Ooker" Eskridge is the mayor of Tangier, and he has been a working waterman for more than fifty years. He told the Thomson Reuters Foundation what was at stake: *We're talking about saving a culture, the people, (a) way of life.* Cook Cannon is a former Tangier waterman who has spent the years since at the Chesapeake Bay Foundation. He talked to *Smithsonian Magazine* in his island home on a cold January night. *I could cry to think about what's going to happen to Tangier.*

Smith Island is the Maryland counterpart, twelve miles to the north, and it is in roughly the same shape. For generations the men on the boats brought in soft-shell crabs and mostly older women picked the meat. The picking houses have been thinning, and the young people are leaving for the mainland. There is a saying on Smith that you get mud between your toes when you spend time there, and that the mud means the island never leaves you. The women who say it are mostly past the age where leaving was an option. The children who hear it have been on the mainland for a decade.

Up the Bay on Tilghman Island in Maryland, the same work continues. Pat Whewell was photographed culling oysters aboard Lonnie Gowe's boat in Broad Creek in 2023, and Chumley Fisher and Chet Schwartz were photographed offloading oysters on Tilghman three years later. The pictures ran in *Maryland Matters*. The picking houses on Tilghman have been thinning like the ones on Smith.

Hampton, where I sit, is not Tangier or Smith. It is a bigger place with more kinds of work, so when its working waterfront shrinks slowly, the larger economy of the city can absorb the loss. The boats are still here and the slips are still rented. The dockmasters still know who is going out tomorrow and who is not. But I have kept my own boat at my marina for years, and in those years I have watched it tip slowly. It used to be a place where the working operators had most of the slips. It is becoming a place where the recreational owners do. Over a long enough time the direction is the same as on the islands. The number of working watermen on the Lower Bay has been falling for a generation. The picking houses are closing, and the boatbuilders who used to build wooden deadrises are mostly gone. A working waterfront depends on welders, engine shops, ice plants, buyers and haulers, and it loses them one at a time. In some places the community that used to come at three in the morning to help with somebody's nets no longer has the bodies to come. Eventually a young person who might have stayed cannot find the work that would have made staying possible. So that young person leaves, and the next one faces the same choice with less to stay for.

The broader economy has no name for what is being asked of these communities. In effect they are being asked to disperse. They are asked to absorb the cost of their own transition and to move their families on their own dime. They are asked to retrain their young for industries that do not exist locally. They are asked to abandon places their ancestors have lived in for two and three centuries, and to give up languages, skills and seasonal rhythms that took those generations to develop. And they are asked to do all of it without compensation, without acknowledgment, and largely without notice from the economy upstream. That economy's runoff, its warming of the climate and its harvest pressure have together made staying impossible. The people in these places are not asking for sympathy. Mostly they are asking to be allowed to keep doing what they know how to do, in the place where they know how to do it, for as long as the water, the regulation and the market allow.

What are these men telling their children? I have heard the answer in pieces, from men along this waterfront. They say the work is hard, the money is uncertain and the regulations are unpredictable. They say nobody is encouraging a son into the work. Some sons go anyway, because the work is what they know and what their grandfathers did, and the choice is the son's. A daughter in nursing school in Norfolk has more options than her father had at her age. He is glad of it, and there is also something in the gain that he does not, in his bones, fully celebrate. When a family leaves the water over the generations, it is a kind of liberation, and it is also a kind of loss.

Add that up across the Lower Bay, the Eastern Shore and the small islands. A whole generation of fathers is holding back the people who would have continued the fishery. Their own fathers gave them years of patient teaching, and they are not giving those years to their sons. They will not teach a son into a trade when they cannot promise him it will support him. It took a community five generations to build the knowledge of where the crabs run in late October. That community is not building a sixth generation that will know it the same way. The knowledge is dying with the men who hold it.

This is its own kind of cost, and it shows up on no stock assessment. An inheritance took centuries to accumulate. It is draining away now, because the generation that should receive it has been quietly told it cannot be passed on.

The children, for their part, are mostly going. They go to Norfolk, to Richmond, to the Hampton Roads tech corridor, to the medical centers and the military bases, to wherever a paycheck is more predictable than a season on the water. Some of them come back on weekends and ride out on the boat one more time to remember how it felt. Some of them never come back. The slip stays empty after the father retires, and the waterfront is one boat thinner. The next father and the next son hear the news and run their own arithmetic.

Cameron Evans, a Tangier native then studying at Virginia Wesleyan, put the question to the Thomson Reuters Foundation in his own words. *If you can't save an island a mile and a half long, how are you going to save the East Coast...* Andy Harris, who represents Maryland's Eastern Shore in Congress, has put the same arithmetic from the other side of the aisle. *Our watermen are pretty resilient,* he has told *Maryland Matters*. *They're like our farmers. They have good years and bad years. But if you have a couple bad seasons in a row, then you're in trouble.*

---

And yet the oysters are coming back.

Maryland has an oyster restoration program, and by the measures the program set for itself it has succeeded. It has built five sanctuaries, each the size of a whole tributary. It has rebuilt hundreds of acres of reef, and it adds tens of millions of oysters a year to the rebuilt structures. The oyster population in Maryland has roughly tripled since 2005. *Large-scale oyster restoration is working,* Colden has said. *Our effort to bring oysters back from the brink in Chesapeake Bay is working,* she has said elsewhere, adding that *continued recovery is not guaranteed.*

Mitch Tarnowski, who leads the oyster reef surveys for Maryland's Department of Natural Resources, summed up the other half in twelve words. *There are plenty of oysters out there,* he told *Maryland Matters*. *They're just not being harvested.*

He was talking about the wild bars outside the restoration sanctuaries. Those bars are not being worked. The watermen with the licenses are not on the water, and the boats that used to take the oysters off those bars are mostly tied up.

The program was being celebrated as one of the largest successful ecosystem restorations in North America. In that same year the State of Maryland filed for a federal disaster declaration for its oyster fishery. The harvest had collapsed in volume and in value, and the watermen who depend on the wild harvest were in financial crisis. There were two reasons. Farm-raised oysters from the Gulf Coast had hollowed out the market for Chesapeake oysters, and seasons of extreme weather had disrupted the windows when the harvest is allowed. *Despite a growing oyster population,* Colden said in support of the disaster request, *Maryland's watermen are feeling the pinch of extreme weather and market competition.*

Jason Ruth owns Harris Seafood Co., an oyster processor in Grasonville, Maryland, and he told the same paper how the longer arc looks from inside an oyster house. *While the demand for boutique aquaculture oysters has grown over the last decade,* he has said, *the shucked oyster sector,* which is the larger part of the industry, *has seen a 60-year nationwide decline.*

So the oyster population is recovering while the watermen are in a declared disaster. Both are true, and the same facts explain both. The recovery is happening in sanctuaries, and the watermen are not allowed to harvest in the sanctuaries. Outside them, on the wild bars, the watermen's harvest is being undercut in the market. It is undercut by aquaculture operations that the watermen mostly do not own, and by Gulf Coast operations that never bore the costs the Bay's recovery is now carrying. The ecosystem is being asked to come back, and the watermen are being asked to absorb the cost of its coming back. The people eating the recovered oysters in restaurants are paying for an oyster. They are not paying for what the watermen absorbed to put it on the half-shell in front of them. They could not pay for it if they wanted to, because the price was never built to carry it.

*One of the most important ways people connect with the Chesapeake Bay,* Colden has said, *is through eating seafood and fishing.* Without watermen, *there's certainly a lot fewer eyes on the water and a lot fewer people caring passionately about the Bay.* For centuries the watermen's attention has held the Bay to account. Now an accounting is beginning to recover what was lost, species by species, and that same accounting is squeezing the watermen out.

A recovery that never asks who paid leaves the people who paid still paying. The oysters can come back without the watermen coming back. The reef can be rebuilt while the working waterfront is not. So who did pay for the reef, and who should have?

The last chapter added up what was left unpaid for the miner. The same can be done for the oyster. The instrument that does the adding is one I am going to name here and build properly in a later chapter. I call it the Restitution Bond. It looks backward at a commons that was drawn down. It prices the physical cost of making that commons whole, and it names who should hold the bill. It has a partner that looks forward, and that one can wait. Here I only want to run the backward one, once, on this reef.

I am going to price one thing only. I am not pricing the lost fishery, or the menhaden, or the livelihoods of the watermen, or all the oysters the bars would have grown if they had been left whole. Every one of those is a real cost. But the reef bottom itself is physical and documented. It is the hard foundation that was stripped out of the water and never put back. What would it cost to rebuild it?

Darling's shell mountains are the picture of what was taken, but they cannot give the price. Raw shell is cheap. And no operation produces its peak year every year for a century, so a peak year cannot simply be multiplied by a hundred. The price has to be built from acres.

The Hampton bars belong to the James River system, and scientists who study the James have measured how much high-quality oyster reef bottom the river lost in the twentieth century. Between 1910 and 1981 the James lost about 2,700 acres of it. Careful historical acreage exists for the James and does not exist for Hampton Bar alone, so the James stands in here for the Hampton bars. The next number is the cost of rebuilding an acre of reef. On lower-Bay bottom where oysters still set by themselves, the restoration record puts that cost between about $7,300 and $13,500. Multiply the acres by that cost and the result is roughly $20 million to $37 million. That is the floor. It is the smallest documented loss, priced at the cheapest documented rebuilding cost, and it covers this one river. There is also a higher figure. Completed reef restoration across the whole Bay has cost about $77,000 an acre on average. At that cost the same acres come to roughly $211 million, which is the center of the estimate.

Now compare that with what the reef was worth while it stood. The watermen who brought the oysters up were paid by the bushel at the dock. Today's dockside rate is around $35 a bushel. At that rate a Darling peak year of two hundred thousand bushels comes to about $7 million. That is one peak year at today's prices, and it should not be multiplied across the decades any more than the shells should. But the dockside price was never what the oyster was worth. A bushel holds roughly two hundred oysters, and an oyster sells for a few dollars on the plate. So by the time that same harvest reached the tables it was sold to, it was worth on the order of $120 million a year. That value was realized in the dining rooms of Baltimore, New York and beyond. The watermen got the dockside price. The cities got the oyster, the restaurants, the jobs and the tax base. A whole economy was built on the Bay's abundance, and it was built everywhere except the Bay.

Put the three numbers side by side. The reef produced around $120 million a year in value, and a century ago that value went up and out to other regions' tables. The labor earned around $7 million in a peak year, and that money stayed at the dock. The cost of rebuilding the lost reef is $20 million to $37 million for one river at the floor, and that cost stayed home and went unpaid. Two writers have described what these numbers show. Mariana Mazzucato writes about the difference between creating value and capturing it. David Harvey gave taking by foreclosure its name, accumulation by dispossession. This reef is one small instance of both, with a price on it.

The reef is already being rebuilt, and the public is paying for all of it. In Maryland alone, more than $92 million in state and federal money has gone into rebuilding roughly 1,300 acres of oyster reef in the sanctuaries. Across the whole Bay the public total comes to roughly $115 million. That figure is from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's own itemized accounting of the completed restoration of ten tributaries. It puts Maryland's share at $93.52 million and Virginia's at $21.77 million. The money came from NOAA, the Army Corps of Engineers, and the Maryland and Virginia resource agencies. These two sets of numbers measure different things. The damage figure is for one river, and the public spending is for the whole Bay, so one cannot be subtracted from the other. What they show together is who has paid. The Navy's oyster castles are part of the same picture. At the Hampton Roads installation they went into the water on the Navy's account and the public agencies', and the Chesapeake Bay Program supplied the young oysters. No dollar figure for that one project has been published. The Navy paid, and the taxpayers paid. One party is missing from that list. The oyster industry took the value of one of the country's largest oyster operations out of this water and shipped it to other people's cities, and it has paid nothing toward the reef's return. The industry's line in this column is zero.

McDowell County had the same defendant. There, the value of the coal spread across a continent, and the black lung and the orange creeks stayed in the hollows. When the bill came due, a public trust fund and public reclamation money paid it, because the companies that had collected the value were gone or shielded. Here the value of the oyster went to the cities and the cost of the lost reef stayed in the water. Public money and Navy labor are paying the bill. In both places the party that collected the value is not in the room. The reason is the same in both places too. No instrument existed to put the cost into the price at the moment of the sale. So the cost came loose from the value, and decades later it landed on whoever was nearest. Usually that is the public.

The Restitution Bond does not invent a new bill. The bill already exists, and the public is already paying it. What the bond changes is who holds it. It names the parties who should have been holding the bill all along, and it moves the cost back onto the value. It does not pay any waterman a dollar. The moment checks go to individuals, the argument becomes which individuals and how much, and that argument swallows the idea. The bond pays into the reef. It rebuilds the commons that was drawn down, and it asks the parties who drew it down, and their successors, to fund the rebuilding in place of the public.

I did not invent this move. Looking back at a commons that was foreclosed, pricing what it would cost to make it whole, and naming who owes the bill is what the economics of reparations has been doing for a different and a graver commons. William Darity and Kirsten Mullen built the most developed version. Ta-Nehisi Coates made its architecture plain, which is to name the mechanism, the harm and the debt. I am borrowing their logic for a reef, and I am keeping the distance between a reef and what they price exactly as wide as it is.

The real figure for this reef is higher than the floor. The full cost to the people of the Bay is higher still. It would take in the fishery, the menhaden, the watermen's lost living and a hundred years of wealth sent elsewhere. I have left all of that out of the column on purpose.

---

What is happening on this Bay is happening in the Gulf of Maine, in Bristol Bay, on the coast of West Africa and in the Mekong Delta. It happens wherever a fishery has been pushed past the rate at which it could replace itself.

The waterman is paid for the crab and not for the Bay. He is paid for the oyster that comes up in the tongs. He is not paid for the reef the tongs ground down across a century, or for the grass beds the runoff smothered. He is not paid for the menhaden the reduction fleet is taking out of the food web his life depends on, or for the Tangier shoreline going under, or for the daughter who has gone to nursing school in Norfolk because there was no boat for her to come back to. What does not come up in the tongs is left in the water, and something has to carry it. The Bay carries it while the Bay can. After that the watermen carry it, and the working waterfront, and the families. The children carry it too, the ones who never had the chance to learn the late-October crab runs. By the time they were old enough to learn, the crabs were no longer running on a schedule anyone could teach.

Put the miner and the waterman in a room together and they would not recognize each other right away. They live in different states and watch different news. They vote, when they vote, for different people. They have been told different stories about who is to blame for what has happened to them. The waterman has been told that the people who would clean up the runoff are the same people who would shut down his harvest. The miner has been told that the people who would clean up the air are the same people who would close his mine. So each of them has been handed an enemy, and the enemy is the other man. Both men are paid for what they produce, and neither is paid for what producing it costs. That arrangement lasts, in part, because the two of them are kept in separate rooms with separate stories.

What the Bay and the hollow share is not a resource. It is a position. Both sit at the bottom of a watershed of value that flows somewhere else.

One country has handled this question differently. Norway has fewer people than the state of Virginia. In the late 1960s it found a finite resource under its part of the North Sea. Its leaders had seen what happened to other countries that were rich in resources. The money scattered, the costs piled up at home, and the next generation was left with the bill. So they built an instrument. It is a set of rules that holds the value coming out of the seabed in trust for the people of Norway and for generations not yet born, in a fund with limits on what can be drawn from it. There has been more pressure to spend that fund than is usually acknowledged, and they have kept it intact for more than thirty years. The next chapter asks whether what Norway built for its oil could be built for the Bay. If it could, the value that has been leaving this watershed for two centuries might begin to stay.

For now, the boats are out at the mouth. The light has crossed the channel and reached the top of the Fort. A man two slips over has come in early, empty, and is hosing down. A truck door closes somewhere on the parking lot. The day is beginning. Within my father's lifetime, on this same water, a man stepped aboard at this hour with six bottles of Coca-Cola in a wire carrier and nothing else, because his lunch was waiting for him in the water, as fresh as it could be. It is the day a waterman has, on the Bay he has, with the accounting the country has agreed to do. It is not yet the day he could have had.
