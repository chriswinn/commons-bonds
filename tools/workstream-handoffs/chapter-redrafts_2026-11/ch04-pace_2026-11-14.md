# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 4: The Existence Proof

---

Three days before Christmas in 1969, a drilling crew in the North Sea found oil.

They were working a patch of Norwegian seabed marked on the maps as Block 2/4. The North Sea in late December gives a crew almost nothing. The light comes up late and gray, and it is gone by the middle of the afternoon. The water under the platform is cold Atlantic water, running in long ridges that have had the whole width of the sea to build. Above it the work is steel and noise: a drill string turning down through the seabed one section at a time, and pipe handled in the wind by men dressed for weather that does not care that it is almost Christmas. Crews had been probing the Norwegian shelf for years by then. Three days before the holiday, the well on Block 2/4 came back with the news. The field was named Ekofisk.

Ekofisk turned out to be one of the largest petroleum reservoirs the industrialized world had ever found. The country that owned the seabed above it had fewer than 4 million people in 1969, and Statistics Norway counts only about 5.6 million today. They lived strung along a cold Atlantic coast, and much of their economy was built on what could be pulled from that water. Not one of them had ever produced a barrel of oil.

Every place that has sat on a find of that size has met the same fork, usually within ten years. One thing is settled on the day the discovery is confirmed: value is going to come up out of the ground. Two things are not settled. Who gets the value? And who carries the costs of bringing it up? Nothing in the rock answers either question. Institutions answer them, and people build the institutions, or fail to.

---

What usually happens next has happened so many times that it can be written down in advance. The windfall gets spent. Some places spend it fast and badly, on imports and patronage and prestige projects that rust. Other places spend it slowly and carefully, on schools and roads and clinics, and they still spend it, so that one generation uses up what the seabed took a hundred million years to store.

Even the careful places tend to end there. The Canadian province of Alberta built a Heritage Savings Trust Fund and put a slice of its oil royalties into it. Then the price of oil fell and the province drew on the fund, and it did the same in downturn after downturn, so the fund never grew to anything like Norway's scale. A fund that gets raided in every bad year is the usual best case. The usual case is a windfall that simply disperses.

Norway chose not to spend most of its windfall, and almost no other place has made that choice. The choice was argued in public and written down. Then it was enforced for five decades by rules built for one purpose above the others, which was to take away from every future government the power to undo it. Norway is where the engineering can be inspected.

---

A child born in Norway this morning holds, from the first breath, a share of a fund worth more than $2 trillion. Divide the fund's own reported value (NBIM Annual Report 2025) by Statistics Norway's count of the population, and the share comes to roughly $390,000 for every Norwegian alive, the newborns included. The newborn did nothing to earn it except be born downstream of a decision.

The machine that produced that share was built in pieces, and in public. In 1990 the Storting, Norway's parliament, established what was then called the Government Petroleum Fund. The first deposit went in six years later, in 1996. In 2006 the fund was renamed the Government Pension Fund Global, which is the name on the door today.

Money reaches the fund from three directions. The first is tax on the private companies that operate on the shelf, at a marginal rate near 78 percent by the accounting of the Norwegian Petroleum Directorate and the Finance Ministry. The second is the state's own production licenses. The third is the state's ownership stake in Equinor, the national oil company, which was called Statoil until 2018. Add the three together and the Norwegian state keeps roughly 70 to 80 cents of every dollar of net petroleum wealth that comes up from its seabed.

The fund invests every krone of it outside Norway, in stocks, bonds and real estate spread across the world, so the oil wealth grows with the output of everyone else's economy and does not swell and distort the small economy at home. The capital is never drawn down. What may be spent is set by a fiscal rule adopted in 2001, which capped each year's withdrawal at the fund's expected real return, put then at 4 percent. In 2017 the rule was revised, and the revision went down, to 3 percent. Rules like this one almost never move in that direction. As the fund grew, the temptation to spend it grew too, and Norway answered by tightening the rule.

The numbers underneath are large in every direction. Through 2024 the Norwegian shelf has produced roughly 55 billion barrels of oil equivalent, which the Norwegian Offshore Directorate estimates at about 57 percent of all it will ever give up. And in recent years the fund's investment returns have been larger than the state's revenue from the oil and gas themselves (NBIM). In those years Norway earned more from owning pieces of the world's economy than from the oil that bought the pieces.

A rule on paper is easy to break, so the fund is surrounded by machinery whose only job is to make breaking it hard. That machinery is easy to skip past, and it is most of the point. Norges Bank Investment Management, which runs the fund, sits at arm's length from political direction. A public ethics council reviews what the fund holds and forces decisions to sell into the open. Four times a year the managers publish an accounting of everything, a public document any citizen or journalist or opposition politician can hold up, so a breach of the rules would be visible within the quarter. And changing the structure itself takes a supermajority of the Storting. A government that wanted to raid the fund would have to do it in daylight, slowly, against its own parliament.

---

Since the first deposit in 1996 the arrangement has come through oil-price crashes, changes of government, populist pressure to spend and industry pressure to pump faster, and it has held. Every administration since, of every party, has treated the fund as something close to untouchable. The agreement runs across the center-left and the center-right, which in most countries agree on very little. Each generation of Norwegian politicians has now done the same unusual thing: it has inherited the largest fortune in its country's history, with the chance to spend it never more than one election away, and it has handed the fortune to the next generation larger than it found it. Why?

It was not an accident of temperament. In the early 1970s, with the find still new, Norwegian policymakers studied the countries that had struck oil before them, and they saw two ways it went wrong. In the first, the windfall is consumed within a generation: a boom buys imports and inflation, and nothing is left standing when the price turns. In the second, the wealth is captured by domestic elites or by foreign operators, and the people who live over the resource are left holding the costs. In 1971 the Storting set down a list of principles that Norwegians have called the ten oil commandments ever since. The principles put the resource under national management, committed the country to developing it at a deliberate pace, and aimed its benefits at the whole society and the generations after it. The fund came two decades later, and it was the instrument those principles had been waiting for. It was designed to shut out both failures at once, and so far it has.

---

Follow one barrel of Norwegian crude out into the world and it ends in places that are easy to picture. Some of it becomes the clear tubing and molded fittings on a surgical tray, the medical-grade plastics no hospital runs without. Some of it becomes the kerosene in the wing tank of an airliner. Some becomes asphalt under traffic, lubricants in gearboxes, and the specialty chemicals that sit far upstream of carbon fiber and a thousand industrial processes.

Three questions follow that barrel. What did Norway keep? What would an honest price have counted? And how much of the difference is still unpaid?

Start with what Norway kept, because no one else has kept as much. Spread across every barrel the shelf has produced, the fund holds about $36 a barrel. All the rent those barrels earned, meaning the income above what it cost to produce them, comes to roughly $32 to $36 a barrel. So once its investment returns are counted, the fund holds about as much as the oil's whole rent, and it holds it in permanent trust for the people the seabed belongs to and for the Norwegians who come after them. Value set aside that way, against the drawing down of a commons, is what this book calls an accountability bond. No extraction community on earth has posted one this size.

An economist had described half of this before Norway built it. In 1977 John Hartwick showed that a society which invests the rents from a resource in lasting capital can hold its living standard steady even as the resource runs out (*American Economic Review* 67, 1977). The fund is Hartwick's rule carried out at the scale of a nation. His result rests on an assumption, though, which is that capital can stand in for the resource. Where it can't, no investment policy fully makes up for what is gone. The fund gives Norway the means to pay for repair, to fund substitutes, and to compensate whoever the damage finally lands on. It does not make a burned barrel come back.

So what would an honest price have counted? It would have counted the full value of what was drawn down, to everyone the oil would ever have served, which is more than any one generation receives. That value has three parts, and they can be put back on the objects the barrel became. The surgical tray and the wing tank are the first part, the access that future people lose. Medical-grade plastics and jet kerosene have no substitute yet that can be made at industrial volume, so a future that needs them and cannot have them is a future paying for this barrel, and the harder a use is to replace, the more this part weighs. The asphalt, the lubricants and the specialty chemicals leave the second part, which is the damage that keeps running after the platform is gone. The third part is the molded fitting nobody has designed yet: the use this chemistry might have served that no one alive has imagined, gone for good the moment the barrel burns. The Technical Appendix builds this account formally and calls the total residual commons value. Subtract the bond from it, and what is left is cost severance: the full value drawn down, minus what was actually set aside, equals what was quietly passed to people downstream in place and in time.

Only one piece of that honest price can be counted with confidence today, and it is the carbon. Burning a barrel of oil puts about 0.37 tons of carbon dioxide into the air. The United States Environmental Protection Agency puts the damage done by one ton at $190, which makes the carbon cost about $70 a barrel. Against that, Norway holds about $36. So at least about $34 a barrel is unpaid, and across 55 billion barrels that comes to about $1.9 trillion, a sum about as large as the fund itself. It is a floor. It counts the carbon alone, and it leaves out the tray, the wing tank and the fitting nobody has designed.

The fund does not pay that bill, and it was never built to. The fund is rent saved for later generations of Norwegians. The carbon from half a century of the shelf's oil and gas was burned all over the world, and it now sits in the one atmosphere everyone shares. Most of its cost falls on people outside Norway, and no handling of the rent would have paid them. Closer in, the platforms have disturbed the North Sea's ecosystems where they stand. And the barrel is gone. Substitutes do mature, and confident forecasts of permanent scarcity have a long record of embarrassment. But a fund compounds money. It does not rebuild a physical resource, and a future that needs what only that chemistry could provide does not get it back because the proceeds were invested well.

Norway, then, is not proof that extraction has been priced correctly anywhere. Its unpaid cost is large, and most of it lands on people who had no say. What Norway proves is narrower, and nobody had shown it before: the rent from a commons can be kept whole for the people it belongs to, including the ones not yet born, by rules a country writes for itself and then keeps. Oil is the non-renewable case, where every barrel taken is a barrel gone. A renewable commons, like the oyster on my home water, severs a cost only when the harvest outruns what can grow back.

---

McDowell County, West Virginia, sits about as far from the Ekofisk field as circumstances allow, and the objection that starts there is a fair one. Norway was a special case. It had strong institutions before the oil came, low corruption, a public administration its citizens trusted, a politics that runs on consensus, and one of the highest levels of social trust ever measured. McDowell County had none of that. The Niger Delta had none of that. Most places where something is dug or pumped out of the ground have none of that.

Every word of the objection is true, and it is the argument. Cost severance is a mechanism: value is taken out, and the costs are passed to communities with little power to refuse them. A mechanism like that runs most easily exactly where Norway's conditions are missing, where institutions are weak, where workers have nowhere else to go, and where a community's ability to insist on an accounting was never built or was taken apart. It finds weakness. So the one place with strong defenses can hardly be faulted for failing to show the disease.

Think of the miner in Chapter 2. The people of McDowell County were not less deserving than Norwegians, and the companies that mined their coal were not unusually malicious. They were ordinary firms operating where the usual machinery of accountability was absent, and the absence did the damage. Every condition for severance was present: the value went one way, the costs stayed, the harm showed itself years later, and a county with a single industry had no way to refuse. McDowell was also missing the fund. The proceeds of its coal were spent as they arrived, so no reserve stands anywhere that could pay for repair, or for substitutes, or for compensation.

The communities most exposed to the mechanism are the ones with the weakest defenses, and waiting for such places to grow Norway's advantages on their own means waiting forever. But Norway's defenses did not grow on their own either. Identifiable people built them, in identifiable years, against real pressure. So what does the same oil look like in a place where nobody built them?

---

On 10 November 1995, the Nigerian state hanged Ken Saro-Wiwa and eight other leaders of the Ogoni people, whose homeland in the Niger Delta sits above the oil. Nine men, one day. The day after, 11 November, Nigeria was suspended from the Commonwealth. The suspension lasted four years, and in 1999 Nigeria was reinstated. On the day of the hangings the oil kept moving. It kept moving on the day of the suspension, and through the four years that followed, and in every year since. The arrangement the Ogoni Nine died inside outlived them unchanged. Production continued, and the communities went on carrying what production left behind. The hangings cost Nigeria four years of Commonwealth membership; they cost the arrangement nothing.

Nigeria has been producing oil since the 1950s. Its first commercial find came at Oloibiri in 1958, eleven years before the strike on Block 2/4. Over roughly six decades the reserves under the Niger Delta have brought the government hundreds of billions of dollars in petroleum revenue. NEITI, Nigeria's extractive-industries transparency body, puts the total near $583 billion since 1960, and that is the government's take alone, not the full value of the oil. The resource was comparable to Norway's, the span of years was comparable, and the same world market was buying. The architecture was the opposite, and so is everything that followed.

Where Norway built a fund, in Nigeria the value left. It moved out through international oil companies into financial systems far from Nigeria. Some of it left through transfer pricing, which works like this: one arm of a company sells to another arm of the same company at a price chosen so that the profit appears wherever taxes are lowest, and taxes were never lowest in the Delta. Some of it settled in secrecy jurisdictions, places whose laws are written so that money can arrive and no outsider can establish whose it is. Economists have measured both. Gabriel Zucman's *The Hidden Wealth of Nations* (2015) counts the wealth held in tax havens beyond the reach of the countries it came from, and Léonce Ndikumana and James Boyce, in *Africa's Odious Debts* (2011), measure the capital that has fled African countries, Nigeria among them. <!-- Insertion M, cut to two sentences (map; style rule 24). The terms of the extraction were older than the country. They came down from concessions written before independence, when Nigeria did not yet have the standing to negotiate them, and they were inherited largely intact. And of the share that did reach the Nigerian state, much was captured by elites, a history recorded in the corruption prosecutions of the decades since. None of this is a story of Nigerian failure alone. It is a story about an international architecture in which Nigeria's weak institutions were one part, and the other parts were designed, operated and defended abroad.

The people who live in the Delta carry what is left, and they carry it in the land itself. Decades of spills have moved through the mangrove creeks and into the farmland. Crude has worked into the sediment where the fish begin and into the soil people farm. The people there live with chronic illness from the spilled crude, from the gas flared off at the wellheads, and from heavy metals. Fishing and farming had carried these communities for generations, and both collapsed into dependence on compensation payments that were often unpaid, or captured before they arrived. Around the whole arrangement runs political violence tied to the oil.

The most careful outside accounting of any part of this is the United Nations Environment Programme's 2011 environmental assessment of Ogoniland, one region of the Delta. UNEP found contamination severe enough to recommend an initial restoration fund on the order of $1 billion, and it projected that full restoration would take 25 to 30 years of sustained work. The years since have not kept that schedule, and monitoring has found the cleanup running well behind it. Ogoniland is also the studied case, the one place where an assessment that careful was done at all. The contamination of the wider Delta, spread across hundreds of square kilometers of creeks and farmland, has never been measured even that closely.

Now run for the Delta the same account Norway got. The value drawn down is enormous: six decades of one of the world's great oil provinces. What reached the communities that bore the costs rounds to zero. No bond was posted, and nothing was held in reserve anywhere that could pay for repair. The severed cost approaches the whole of the value.

Two countries found comparable oil and sold it into the same market. In one, the rent stayed with the people the seabed belongs to. In the other, the rent left and the damage stayed. Geology did not decide that, and neither did the market or any law of economics. Cost severance is a policy choice. The architecture that stands between a resource and the people who live over it is engineered, and anything engineered once can be engineered somewhere else.

An accounting that treated all extraction as a disaster, always and everywhere, would be useless in front of these two cases. It would describe Norway and Nigeria in the same words, prescribe the same thing for both, and be wrong about each in opposite directions. The accounting in this book tells them apart. Norway comes back with its rent kept and a carbon bill unpaid. Nigeria comes back with almost nothing kept and almost everything unpaid. The geology and the commodity are the same, so the difference comes from the one thing a society can actually change, which is the architecture of accountability between the resource and the people. That is what makes the accounting a tool for policy and not only a moral complaint.

---

One objection is left, and it is the strongest. Norway is far away. It is small, rich, homogeneous and high-trust, and it has a state that works. Whatever it proves about Norwegians, the objection goes, it proves nothing about anywhere else. It could never work here, wherever here happens to be.

In 1976 the voters of Alaska, a US state not famous for its warmth toward collective ownership, ratified an amendment to their constitution by roughly three votes to one. Governor Jay Hammond championed it, and it is now Article IX, Section 15 of the Alaska Constitution. It directs at least 25 percent of the state's oil and mineral royalties into a permanent fund, and the constitution itself protects the fund's principal from being spent. Since 1982 the fund has paid a dividend to every Alaska resident, every year, through more than 40 years of booms, busts and administrations of both parties. The 2024 dividend was $1,702, and the fund stands near $89 to 90 billion in 2026 (Alaska Permanent Fund Corporation). That is resource rent captured for everyone, written into a constitution and ratified by voters, inside the United States.

The crews who strung fiber-optic line across Chattanooga, Tennessee, worked for the city's own electric utility, EPB, which the city has owned since 1935. By about 2010 the utility had gigabit internet service available across its whole footprint, ahead of Google Fiber, which lit its first city, Kansas City, in 2012. The build was paid for with roughly $111 million in 2009 federal stimulus money and with municipal bonds. The network serves on the order of 125,000 customers, has run at a profit since about 2015, and sells service today at speeds up to 10 gigabits (EPB; FCC 2015 preemption record). The incumbent companies did not welcome any of it. Comcast sued in 2008, before the launch, and the fight over whether EPB could expand ran all the way to the Federal Communications Commission's preemption proceeding in 2015. The network got built anyway, and it turned a profit while the litigation aged.

In 1956, in the Basque town of Mondragón, a priest named José María Arizmendiarrieta helped found a workshop that grew into the largest federation of worker cooperatives in the world. The federation's own reporting counts roughly 80,000 worker-owners across some 95 cooperatives, and they compete in ordinary industrial markets. Pay inside is capped by rule. Historically the cap was around 3 to 1 between the highest and the lowest paid, and it is roughly 6 to 9 to 1 even in the large industrial cooperatives, against ratios that run past 200 to 1 at large American firms. When Fagor, a flagship cooperative, went bankrupt in 2013, the federation took most of its displaced workers into the other cooperatives and did not turn them out. Anyone who believes worker ownership survives only in boutiques has seven decades at competitive scale to account for.

In Vienna the city began building municipal apartment blocks, the Gemeindebauten, in 1919, in the years remembered as Red Vienna, and their courtyards have held tenants for a century. Today about 62 percent of Vienna's roughly 1.9 million residents live in municipal or non-profit cooperative housing. That is well over 400,000 apartments, some 220,000 of them owned by the city itself (City of Vienna). The system is paid for in part by a dedicated housing contribution of about 1 percent of payroll, alongside progressive taxation and subsidized federal loans, and it sustains itself. It has held through every change of government, through Austria's accession to the European Union in 1995, and through the crisis years of 2008 to 2013. Nothing about it reads as poverty housing. It is simply where much of working Vienna lives, by choice, and has lived for a hundred years.

These cases sit at four scales: a nation, a US state, a municipality, a firm. They cover four kinds of commons: a resource, an infrastructure, labor, housing. Whatever explains Norway now has to explain Alaska and Chattanooga and Mondragón and Vienna too, and by then the explanation is no longer Scandinavian exceptionalism. It is something plainer: architecture, built on purpose and defended over time, in places that look nothing like each other.

---

The United States built one of these architectures itself, and the year it was built explains nearly everything about its shape. Social Security was designed in 1935, in the middle of the Depression, for an elderly generation that was destitute then, that winter, and not at some distant date on an actuary's chart. The design that could reach them was pay-as-you-go: payroll taxes collected from people working now go, almost at once, to people retired now. When the system later ran surpluses, the surpluses were lent to the Treasury in return for special bonds that cannot be sold on any market. So the trust fund is a record of promises the government holds against itself, and nothing in it grows with any economy outside the one that must eventually make the promises good.

Set that beside the Norwegian design. Norway takes a finite stream of oil rents and compounds it in capital invested outside its own economy. The United States takes an ongoing stream of payroll taxes and passes it across the generations as it arrives. Neither is dishonorable, and the difference comes from circumstance and not from virtue. Norway designed in the 1970s and 80s, with a windfall to invest and a mature global financial system to invest it in. The United States designed in 1935, when there was no windfall and the move within reach was a transfer program that could put checks in old hands without delay. A funded design was not unimaginable. Bismarck's German old-age and disability insurance of 1889 was partly funded, and it stayed that way until the hyperinflation of the early 1920s burned the fund away, so funded designs carry risks of their own. None of this says the United States should adopt Norway's design. The claim is narrower. The architecture is a choice. No arrangement of value and cost across generations arrived by itself. Someone engineered each one, under the constraints of its date, and what was engineered at one date can be re-engineered at another. A choice is easiest to see as a choice when a second architecture is held up beside it, and that is most of what Norway is for.

Chapter 3 ended on a dock in Hampton, Virginia, with a question pointed at Oslo: could the instrument built for the oil be built, in some adapted form, for the oyster? My father grew up at Fox Hill among the sons of watermen; the men who taught him boats and weather and the meaning of the channel's smell in August were men whose working lives were the water that question is about. The family is one generation off the boats and still inside the community that worked them. That standing is the reason the question gets asked here with the oil's seriousness, and not as an analogy passing through. <!-- PROTECTED: the three sentences on the author's father and family are carried word for word from the current chapter. Part of the answer has a number now. Chapter 3 put a price on what was taken from the Chesapeake reef: a floor of $20 to 37 million to restore it, with a central estimate near $211 million. It is the one case in this book where a cost already imposed has been fully computed. What was destroyed has a stated price. The question Norway adds points forward. The oyster is the renewable case. Its severance came when the harvest outran what the reefs could rebuild, which the Bay's record shows it did for a century, and inside what the reefs can rebuild nothing is severed. So could the rents of a living commons, one that makes more of itself as long as the taking stays within what grows back, be captured and held the way Norway holds the oil's, so that the next century of the fishery is not priced at zero? That may be the restitution instrument running forward, or it may be a different instrument entirely. Chapter 3 raised the question and this chapter leaves it standing. What Norway settles is only that instruments of this kind can be built and kept.

---

Norway proves the thing can be built. It does not show every place how to build it, and it leaves four things unpriced.

The first is the barrel itself. The fund saves the rent, and money in a fund can pay for repair, and for substitutes where substitutes exist. But if a future use turns out to need petroleum's particular chemistry and nothing else will do, no rate of compounding brings the oil back. Norway has saved what the barrel earned. It has not replaced the barrel.

The second is the carbon, and it begins with people who will never receive a krone from the fund: the delta farmers of Bangladesh, working land the sea claims more of each decade; the herders and farmers at the margin of the Sahel, where the rains have stopped keeping their old schedule; the people of the low-lying Pacific island states, whose entire national territories stand barely above the tide. The carbon from the shelf's oil and gas did not stay in the North Sea. It is in the one atmosphere, and its costs land hardest on them. The fund holds more than $2 trillion in trust for 5.6 million people. The carbon falls on hundreds of millions who are not among them, and no part of the architecture reaches them. Nearer the platforms, the North Sea's disturbed ecosystems recover slowly where they recover at all.

The third is scale. The fund works by compounding on the output of the rest of the world's economy, so the solution needs an outside to invest in. If every extracting country built one tomorrow, there would not be enough outside economies to absorb them all. What Norway built is a real solution for one country and one the planet cannot adopt by copying. The global version of the problem is unsolved, and it is the version that matters most.

The fourth is the hardest to see. The fund turns the oil's present value into money. It does not put a price on what the oil would have been worth left in the ground, for uses nobody has imagined yet. Norway priced the present well. Pricing the future on purpose is a different problem, and nobody has solved it.

These four gaps are work that remains, not a flaw in the proof. Norway proves that a partial solution exists. The rest is to close the gaps in places that already have Norway's foundations and, harder and more important, in places where the foundations themselves must be built against the resistance of the same extraction economy whose revenue would pay for them.

---

Two strikes came eleven years apart: Oloibiri in 1958, and Block 2/4, three days before Christmas, in 1969. The industry was the same, the hydrocarbons were the same, and the same world market was buying. Above one strike a country wrote rules that keep the rent and limit how fast it can be spent, and a fishing nation of fewer than 4 million people became the holder of a fund worth more than $2 trillion. Above the other there was no bond and no reserve, and six decades of production left the people of the Delta the contamination, the flares, the broken fisheries, and a cleanup still running behind a schedule set in 2011. Neither outcome was inevitable. People engineered both, in years that can be named, under pressures that were real in both places.

The choice has now been watched in both directions. The mechanism that strips value out of a place and leaves the costs behind can be interrupted, because one country interrupted it and has kept it interrupted for more than 30 years. Norway did not finish the job. Its carbon bill is unpaid, and the people it falls on live a long way from Oslo. What it would take to finish it, in Norway and everywhere else, is the question the remaining chapters take up. The next one starts much closer to home, with one family, in a house in Connecticut.

The drill came up three days before Christmas carrying the same news it carries everywhere. What happened next was a decision. It always is.
