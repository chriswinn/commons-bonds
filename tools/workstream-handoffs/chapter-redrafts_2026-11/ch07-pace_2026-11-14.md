# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 7: On Other Worlds

## The habitat

Imagine a habitat on Mars. It doesn't exist, and neither does the woman who runs its water, but imagine her at her desk on an ordinary morning with a request in front of her.

Ten thousand people live in the habitat. It has been there for several decades, long enough that children have been born in it and have grown up and had children of their own. Everything those ten thousand people drink comes from three deposits of ice under the ground nearby. So does the water for the crops in the growing domes, and so does the oxygen, which the habitat makes by splitting water. At the rate the habitat uses water now, the three deposits will last about fifty years.

The request comes from a trading consortium. It wants her permission to take the ice out faster and sell what the habitat doesn't need this year. The numbers in the request are sound. The consortium would earn back its investment within five years, and the habitat would share in the profit. The request also says, on a later page, what the faster pace does to the supply: the fifty years become about twenty.

She is the administrator of the habitat's resources, and the decision is hers. Her office is two corridors from the kindergarten. She walks past the growing domes every morning on her way to work, and this morning she will walk past them again on her way to meet the consortium. Her own grandchildren are among the ten thousand.

The consortium is offering a return in five years, and it is asking for thirty years of the water. What should she say? And how does she know?

## Her answer

She doesn't need a theory to answer. She needs to think about four things, and any careful person in her chair would think about the same four.

The first is whether anything else could do the water's job. Nothing can. People drink water, crops take it up, and the oxygen plant splits it. No other substance on Mars does any of those three things. She has read proposals for pulling water from the thin atmosphere, and for bringing ice in from comets. None of them runs today at the scale of ten thousand people, and none is certain to be running in twenty years. If the ice is gone, she has nothing to put in its place.

The second is what the remaining water is worth as it runs down. To an empty planet, the ice under the ground is plentiful. To ten thousand people with three deposits, it isn't. Each ton that is left matters more than the ton before it, because there are fewer tons between her people and the day there are none. The value climbs slowly at first and then steeply, and somewhere ahead, at a point she can't locate exactly, it stops being a slope and becomes a cliff.

The third is what the faster drilling does to the water she keeps. Drilling fluids can foul the ice next to the ice being taken. Pulling one deposit out quickly can crack the rock that holds the next one. Gases released from the ground have to be scrubbed out of the habitat's air by machines that are already busy. And there is one more thing under that ground that she can't put a number on. If anything is alive in the ice of Mars, it is the only life anyone has found beyond Earth, and careless drilling could end it.

The fourth is who will be living in the habitat in thirty years. On Earth, economists argue about how much a cost that falls decades from now should count today, and many of their methods shrink it until it nearly disappears. She can't do that, because she knows who is thirty years out. The children in the kindergarten will be adults raising their own children, and her grandchildren will be among them. For her, the future isn't a number to be marked down. It is the same people, a little older, and their thirst will count exactly as much then as hers does now.

Put the four together and her answer is plain. She keeps the deposits in reserve, and she turns the consortium down. She charges more for water as the supply falls, so that waste gets expensive long before the supply gets short. She spends money on recycling, so that every ton is used many times. Five years of profit, even a real profit honestly shared, is small beside what the habitat would lose, and the people who would lose it are the same people being offered the profit.

I don't think that answer surprises you. I think you had it before she finished reading the request, and that is why I put her on Mars. This book has argued that when something shared is used up, the price leaves out most of the cost, and that the cost lands on people who had no say. A fair reader could accept every case so far and still object. Maybe all of that is a fact about Earth's politics, about lobbyists and captured regulators and the particular bargains of the last two centuries. Maybe a careful planner starting fresh, with no lobby in the room, would count the way the market counts.

She is that planner. She has no lobby and no ideology, and she counts the way this book counts: what can't be replaced, what the remainder is worth, what the taking damages, and who is there afterward. In her habitat that counting is so obvious that it hardly feels like a method. So why isn't it obvious here?

## One number at a time

Start with her situation exactly as it is, and change one thing.

Give her more ice. Not three deposits but three thousand. Now a ton taken this year makes no real difference to what is left, and the pressure that made each remaining ton precious falls away. She can say yes to more.

Put the first situation back, and change a different thing. Suppose something else could do water's job, so that a habitat short of ice could switch to it. Then the people of thirty years from now aren't trapped by her decision, because they have another road. Again she can say yes to more.

Make the drilling clean, so that taking one ton does no harm to the next. Or suppose the people of thirty years from now really will be better supplied than she is, so that a ton matters less to them than to her. Each change loosens her answer a little further.

Make all four changes together, and her careful count and the consortium's simple one arrive at the same figure. When a resource is plentiful, replaceable and clean to take, and the future is well provided for, the price the market sets is close to the whole cost, and there is nothing left over to land on anyone. The right answer is to go ahead.

Now run the changes the other way. Make the resource scarce, with no sure replacement, with damage that outlasts the taking, and with a future that depends on it. The full cost climbs far above the market's price, and the right answer becomes "only with great care" or "no." Through all of this the four questions stayed the same. Only the facts changed, and the answers changed with the facts.

Earth is one of those settings. It isn't a different kind of place from her habitat. It is the same kind of place with milder numbers. Take coal. There is far more coal on Earth than there is ice under her habitat, but it is being used up and none is being made. Other things can do coal's main job, which is making electricity, and that counts in coal's favor. The damage outlasts the mine by generations: black lung in the miners, acid water draining from old workings, carbon in the air. And the fourth question, how much the people of the future count, is the one economists on Earth have fought over for decades.

That fight has a shape. In 2006 a review of the economics of climate change, led by Nicholas Stern for the British government, argued that a person's welfare shouldn't count for less because they are born later, and it used a rate of about 1.4 percent a year to mark down future costs. William Nordhaus, who built one of the most widely used economic models of the climate, argued for marking them down at around 4 to 5 percent a year, a rate taken from what money actually earns when it is invested. The gap looks small. Compounded over a century, it decides whether a ton of carbon burned today carries a large bill or a small one. Earlier, in 1977, the economist John Hartwick had shown what it takes to leave the people who come after no worse off: a society has to reinvest what it earns from a resource that runs out. The bonds in this book are built on that idea.

I am not going to settle the argument between Stern and Nordhaus here. I only want you to see why the administrator never has it. The future people in her habitat have faces, and she passes them in the corridor. The future people on Earth are strangers, and it is easy to mark a stranger down.

So every one of her four questions has an answer on Earth too. The numbers are milder, and they are mild enough that for a century nobody was forced to ask. If the counting only ever said "no," though, a reader would be right to distrust it. Does it ever say yes?

## Other worlds

It does, and the quickest way to see that is to carry the four questions to a few more places. All of them are imagined as places to mine, though the bodies themselves are real.

Take an asteroid made mostly of iron, with nobody living on it or near it. Iron is among the most plentiful metals there is. Thousands of other asteroids hold it, and so does Earth. Mining this one fouls no air and no water, because it has neither, and it moves no one from their home. Nobody thirty years from now, or three hundred, will be short of iron because this rock was mined. All four questions come back empty, so the full cost of the iron is close to whatever a buyer will pay for it. The answer is to go ahead.

Now go to the Moon and ask about two things in the same ground. The first is the gray dust that covers the surface. A settlement could pile it up for shielding or bake it into bricks. There is more of it than anyone could use, and digging it harms very little beyond making more dust. Go ahead. The second is a rare form of helium, called helium-3, that the Sun has been driving into that dust for billions of years. In 1986 a team of fusion engineers at the University of Wisconsin estimated that the lunar surface holds about a million tons of it. Almost nothing uses helium-3 today. But one design for a fusion reactor would run on it, and if that design ever works, nothing else could take its place. Nobody knows whether it will work.

For a case like that, Chapter 6 gave a rule, the Asymmetric Regret Rule: when you can't know the odds, compare the two ways of being wrong, and avoid the one you can't undo. Leave the helium where it is, and if fusion never comes, nothing has been lost, because leaving it costs nothing. Mine it and scatter it for some small use now, and if fusion does come, the fuel is gone for good. So the answer for helium-3 is to wait. The Moon gets two answers, one for its dust and the opposite one for its helium, and they come from the same four questions asked of two different things.

A comet makes the rule point the other way. Suppose a comet from the far edge of the solar system swings past, close enough to reach for a few months, carrying ice and other frozen material. Then it leaves, and it won't be back for thousands of years. Taking from it harms no one and nothing. Here the mistake that can't be undone is waiting, because the thing you waited for is gone. The answer is to take what is useful, now.

Europa is harder. Europa is a moon of Jupiter with an ocean of liquid water under a shell of ice, and that ocean is one of the few places in the solar system where scientists think life might exist. Suppose someone proposed to mine the ice. Three of the questions can be answered. The third can't. If something lives in that ocean and mining destroys it, what was that worth? No figure for that would mean anything, and this book doesn't write one down. It leaves the line open and says so. An open line doesn't mean the counting stops. One way of being wrong can never be undone, and the other costs only time, so the answer is to wait and study.

That makes five answers in four places: go ahead, go ahead, wait, take it now, and wait because the cost can't be priced. A creed would have given the same answer every time. These came out differently because the places are different.

And none of those places had the thing that has been in every chapter of this book, a bill that someone gained from and someone else was left to pay. On the asteroid and the comet there was no one to leave it with. So where does that bill come from, and what makes it vanish from the accounts?

## The crew that breathes its own dust

Go back to the habitat, and give its people a second decision. Under the ground near them are rare earths, the metals that electronics depend on. They can keep importing electronics from Earth, or they can mine the metals and make their own.

The mining is dirty, as it is on Earth. It throws off fine dust and chemical waste. But consider where the dust goes. It goes into the same air system that everyone in the habitat breathes from. The waste has to be stored inside the same sealed walls, because there is no river to carry it away and no downstream for it to go to. A miner who gets sick is treated in the habitat's one small hospital, by doctors everyone shares. The people who would gain from the mine and the people who would pay for it are the same ten thousand people, and they can see all of it.

So nothing in the habitat is severed. Chapter 5 gave the name severed cost to a cost cut loose from the party that drew the value and left with whoever couldn't refuse it. Here there is nobody to leave it with. The cost can be large. The habitat may decide the metals are worth it, or decide they aren't. Either way, the people deciding are the people who will pay, and they are deciding with the cost in view. When the same people gain and pay, and know it, the unpaid part of the bill is zero.

Both halves of that sentence matter. Suppose the dust carried a hazard that nobody in the habitat could detect. Then they would be paying a cost they had never agreed to, and the unpaid part would not be zero, even with everyone inside the same walls.

Now split the gain from the cost and watch the bill appear. Imagine a different venture, also invented. Investors on Earth pay for a mine on an asteroid. A crew of twelve goes out to run it for ten years. The investors will get the metal. The crew carries everything else: the chance of dying out there, the chance that the company fails and can't pay to bring them home, whatever ten years in that place does to their health, and whatever ten years away does to them and to the families they left.

Go down that list the way the administrator would. The crew can see the danger of the work, and their contracts pay them extra for it. They accepted that cost knowingly, and it is covered. The other three are not. Nobody has set aside the money to bring twelve people home from a bankrupt mine. Nobody has paid for the medical care they will need afterward. And the cost of the long absence can't be known by anyone until it has been lived.

This is where the book's instrument does its work, before the first load is shipped. Say that the mine expects to ship a thousand tons. Estimate the rescue and the medical care, and divide by a thousand. That is what each ton owes. As each ton ships, that amount goes into a reserve that the company can't touch, held by a trustee on the crew's behalf. If a cost turns out never to happen, its share goes back to the investors. The fourth cost, the absence, stays on the books as an open line, which means the reserve is a floor and everyone knows it.

That is the new thing this book proposes, shown small: a price for each act of extraction, with a reserve posted against it in advance. It also shows what a severed cost needs before it can exist. It needs a second party to carry it. And if the second party would object, it needs some way to keep the cost out of sight. On Earth that way has a name.

## Distance

The name is distance. It comes in four kinds, and you have met each of them already in this book.

The first is distance in space. The coal of McDowell County was dug in West Virginia and burned in steel mills and power plants far from the county. The people who got the steel and the electricity never saw the creeks run orange, and the people who lived by the creeks couldn't send them the bill.

The second is distance in time. Much of the cost of burning that coal will fall on people who haven't been born. They can't object or bargain, and by the time they are old enough to add it up, everyone who gained will be gone.

The third is social distance, which is the gap between people who share a place and a time but don't have the same standing. In 2008 the banks and the families who lost their homes lived in the same towns under the same laws. They were not equally able to be heard, and they were not equally able to walk away.

The fourth is distance in what people know, and the nearest case of it is my own. In Chapter 1 I told you about the apartment I rented for three hundred dollars a month less, and the drive I tested once, late at night, in forty-eight minutes. In working hours the trip took two and a half hours each way, and the lease cost me thirteen hundred hours in a year. Nobody hid those hours from me. I could have found them out with one drive on a weekday morning. I didn't, because nothing on the listing or in the lease prompted the question. The person who gained from that lease and the person who paid for it were both me, and what kept them apart was only what the first one didn't know.

Two habits keep that last kind of distance in place. One is routine. When a cost has been paid for long enough, it gets called the cost of doing business, and people stop seeing it as a cost. The other is plenty. When there seems to be no end to something, nobody counts it. The air took in our smoke for two centuries, and the oceans gave up their fish, and for a long time each looked as if it could go on doing so forever.

One thing is missing from that list, and it is missing on purpose. Force is not a kind of distance. Where people can be made to bear a cost, nothing needs to be hidden from anyone. Force is a way of assigning a cost, not a way of concealing it, and the end of this chapter comes back to it.

Now look at the habitat again. Nobody is far away, because everyone lives inside one set of walls. The people of thirty years from now are in the kindergarten two corridors from the administrator's office. Her own family breathes the air she is responsible for. And nobody can believe the water is endless, because every ton of it is counted. The four kinds of distance aren't weak there. They aren't available. A cost in the habitat can still be large, and its people can still choose badly, but nothing can keep the cost out of their sight.

Now turn that around. Think of someone who grew up in that habitat and came to Earth for the first time. She wouldn't notice our resources first. She would notice that the air is free, and that nobody makes it or meters it. Water falls from the sky. The temperature holds without a machine. Something overhead that nobody maintains screens out the worst of the Sun's radiation. Everything her habitat budgets for, down to the breath, Earth hands out without being asked. It hands out so much that we have never had to learn what any of it costs, and in that sense the plenty is the widest distance we have.

So the difference between her world and ours is not that her people are wiser than we are. Her world has no distance in it, and ours is full of it. That is the shield this book sets out to take down. But a shield can't come down everywhere at once, so where should the work start?

## What to hold back

It should start with the things that can't be replaced. The four questions don't weigh every resource the same, and the first question, whether anything else can do the job, sorts them faster than the others.

It has to be asked twice. Can something else do this job today? And could something else do every job that people might need this for over the next several centuries, including jobs nobody has thought of yet? For some resources the two answers match. For others they are far apart, and I call that difference the substitutability gap. Where the gap is wide, a society that plans to last should hold some of the resource back.

Coal is at the narrow end. Sun, wind, water, the atom and the heat of the Earth can all make electricity, and nobody has proposed a future that needs coal and nothing else. Sand, stone and ordinary iron ore are beside it. The reasons to leave coal in the ground are the damage it does and the carbon it releases. Running out of it is not one of them.

Oil, copper and zinc come next. Other things can do most of what they do now, and no distant need points to them alone.

Uranium, lithium and cobalt are in the middle. Each has a rival in sight: thorium for uranium, sodium for lithium, iron-based batteries for cobalt. But the rivals aren't proven at full scale, and some future uses may turn out to need these exact metals.

The rare earths and the platinum family are at the wide end. Their magnetic and chemical properties are what make strong small motors, fuel cells and advanced electronics possible. Substitutes exist for some of today's uses. Nobody has a path to substitutes for all of them, and the technologies of the next few centuries are more likely than not to need these same properties.

Helium-3 stands apart. If fusion ever runs on it, it belongs at the very top of the list, and if not, it is a curiosity.

This ranking is only the first of the four questions. How much of a resource is left, and what the taking damages, still have to be asked of each one. But it tells a society where to look first. The metals at the wide end of the list are also the metals inside the motors, magnets and batteries that the move away from coal and oil depends on. Chapter 9 takes up what that means: the world is about to dig very large amounts of the things it can least afford to waste, and it has a chance to price them correctly from the first ton.

## Two fair objections

A careful reader will have two objections by now, and both are fair.

The first is that Mars is too simple. A sealed habitat has ten thousand people, three deposits and one administrator. If she gets the water wrong, people die within a decade, and everyone knows why. Earth has eight billion people. Its resources are only partly mapped, and new deposits and new inventions keep changing what is scarce. The harm arrives slowly and far from its cause. A lesson from the simple place, this reader says, proves nothing about the complicated one.

Earth is more complicated, and I agree with that much. What the complication changes is how well we know the answers to the four questions. On Earth the amount left is an estimate, the substitutes are disputed, the damage is hard to trace, and the weight of the future is argued over. The complication doesn't change the questions. In both places something is being used up faster than it comes back, some people gain from that and some people pay for it, and the price either includes the cost or leaves it out.

A physics teacher does the same thing with a frictionless plane. No such surface exists. But a block sliding on one shows the law of motion with nothing in the way, and once a student has seen the law there, she can add friction back and understand a real block on a real ramp. Every working science keeps a few impossible objects like that one, and it uses them to test whether its reasoning gives the right answer in a case where the right answer isn't in doubt. The habitat is this book's frictionless plane. If the four questions had told the administrator to sell the water, you would have known that something was wrong with the questions.

The second objection comes from the other side. On the asteroid, the counting agreed with the market. So where the market is right, this reader says, the framework adds nothing, and where it disagrees with the market, why should anyone trust it?

On the asteroid it does add nothing, and it should add nothing there. The market's price for that iron is right, because there is no damage, no one displaced and no future made poorer. A measuring tool that disagreed with a correct reading would be broken. One that agreed with every reading would be useless. This one agrees with the market on the asteroid and on the Moon's dust, and it departs from the market at the habitat's water, and for helium-3, and for a ton of coal. In each of those cases you can point to the question that made the difference.

One objection is left, and it is the oldest. Maybe distance and unpaid bills belong to modern industry, or to modern markets. Did they begin there?

## Older than any of our systems

They didn't. The same arrangement appears in two civilizations that never knew of each other, thousands of years before anything we would call an industry or a market.

The pyramids at Giza went up around 2550 BCE. Building the whole complex took about seventy years, and the largest pyramid, built for the king Khufu, took about twenty. For a long time people pictured slaves under the whip. The excavations led by Mark Lehner in the workers' town, and by Zahi Hawass in the workers' cemetery, found something different. The builders were skilled craftsmen, along with farmers called up in rotation. They were fed from the king's stores. Their injuries were treated, and when they died they were buried with care near the monument they had worked on.

They were also not free to refuse. The king's economy was the only economy there was, with no other employer and no other place to go. The king got what the pyramid was for: his standing among the living and his provision for the afterlife. The laborers supplied the years and the bodies.

Twenty-three centuries later and a continent away, Qin Shi Huang conquered the warring states of China and became its first emperor. He ordered a wall along the northern frontier, which ran about five thousand kilometers, and a tomb for himself near the present-day city of Xi'an. The clay soldiers that farmers found there in 1974 are one small part of that tomb. The historian Sima Qian, writing about a century after the dynasty fell, reported that three hundred thousand to five hundred thousand conscripts worked on the wall at one time, and that more than seven hundred thousand worked on the tomb. They were farmers, prisoners and convicts, and the histories record that great numbers of them died. The Qin state did not disguise its philosophy, which set quotas and punished anyone who failed to meet them. Within four years of the emperor's death, rebellions had brought the dynasty down. The wall and the soldiers are still there.

I won't put a price on what those laborers were owed, in either country. This book doesn't write down a dollar figure for forced labor anywhere.

But look at what the two cases share. Egypt and Qin China had different gods, different writing, and different ideas of what a ruler was, and neither knew the other existed. In both, one group gained and another paid, and the cost went to whoever couldn't refuse it. And in both, almost nothing was hidden. The laborers knew what the work cost them, and the work was done in the open, at the foot of the monument. There was no distance in space or in knowledge to hide behind, and none was needed, because the people who bore the cost had no power to refuse it. That is what I meant by force. It doesn't hide a cost. It assigns one, in plain view.

So the arrangement isn't a product of capitalism, or of any system we argue about now. It is older than all of them. Each age has run it through whatever system it had, and the pyramids and the wall have outlasted every one of those systems.

The administrator's question, then, was never only hers. She asked what a thing costs when everything is counted and who will be there to pay it. She could answer in a morning, because in her habitat nothing stood between the people who would gain and the people who would pay. On Earth a great deal stands between them, and the only way to answer is to do the counting by hand, one cost at a time.

The next chapter does that for a single ton of coal, dug in McDowell County, West Virginia, in 1960. The mine sold that ton for just under five dollars. What did it cost?
