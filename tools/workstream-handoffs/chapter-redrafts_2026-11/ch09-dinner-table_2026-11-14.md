# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 9: Pricing Honestly

## A decision not yet made

In 1979 a test of seabed mining was run on the floor of the Pacific Ocean. The seabed in that part of the Pacific lies about five thousand metres down, and it is scattered with dark lumps of rock about the size of potatoes. The lumps are called nodules, and they contain nickel, cobalt, copper and manganese. The test was meant to find out whether a machine could gather them up. The machine weighed about a hundred tonnes. It was lowered to the bottom and driven across it, and it cleared a strip about eight metres wide and left two deep furrows where it had passed.

In 2023 a research ship went back to see what the strip looked like. The scientists found it within seven or eight hours of their first dive. One of the leaders of the expedition was Daniel Jones, of Britain's National Oceanography Centre. "Forty-four years later," he said, "the mining tracks themselves look very similar to when they were first made." Some of the life had returned. The small animals that live in the mud were back in about the numbers found on untouched seabed nearby. The larger animals that live attached to the nodules were not back, because the nodules they need were gone, and it takes tens of millions of years for a nodule to form. The team published its findings in the journal *Nature* in 2025.

The strip is in a stretch of seabed between Hawaii and Mexico that is called the Clarion-Clipperton Zone. It doesn't belong to any country. An international treaty, the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, calls the deep seabed "the common heritage of mankind." In 1994 the treaty created a body to look after it, called the International Seabed Authority, with its headquarters in Kingston, Jamaica. Companies and governments need a contract from the Authority to work on the seabed. So far it has issued thirty-one contracts, and every one of them is for exploring. It has never issued a contract for mining, because mining needs a set of rules and the Authority has not finished writing them.

It has been working on those rules for years. In June 2021 the island nation of Nauru tried to force the pace. Nauru sponsors a subsidiary of a mining firm called The Metals Company, and it invoked a clause in the treaty that gives the Authority two years to complete the rules once a member asks. The two years ended in July 2023, and the rules were not done. The Authority's council met again in July 2026 and still did not adopt them. The talks are scheduled to start again in March 2027. In the meantime the company has tried another route. The United States never joined the treaty, and it has its own law on seabed mining, passed in 1980. By January 2026 the company's American subsidiary had applied to the United States government for a permit to mine part of the zone under that law.

So somebody has to decide, either in Kingston or in Washington. I take no position here on what they should decide. I bring the case up because of when it is happening. In every earlier chapter the decision was already over. In McDowell County, in Libby and on the Chesapeake, the taking was finished before anyone counted what it cost, so the only thing left to do was write the bill afterward. Chapter 8 wrote one of those bills. A ton of McDowell coal sold at the mine in 1960 for just under five dollars, and when the costs it left behind are counted today they come to more than five hundred dollars. Chapter 5 ended with a question: what would it take to post the bond before the cost is cut loose, when the party that gained is still there to pay it? Chapter 7 asked that question of an administrator on another world, and she was imagined. The administrator in this case is real. The resource is real. And nothing has been taken yet.

How would anyone go about deciding something like that? There is a method, and every part of it is already in use somewhere. It has four steps. I will call them Classify, Reserve, Invest and Reassess. After the four steps there is a fifth piece, for whatever gets taken anyway, and that piece is the bond this book is named for.

## Classify

The first step is to sort resources into groups, because using up one resource does not put the same things at risk as using up another. One question does the sorting. If we use this up, will the people who come after us be able to replace it?

For some resources the answer is yes. Iron is one. There is a great deal of it on this planet and on most of the rocky bodies near it. Coal is another, when it is used as fuel, because we already know how to heat a house and run a power grid without it. Coal does have a cost, which is the damage it does to lungs, to creeks and to the air, and somebody has to pay for that damage. But burning coal will not leave anyone two hundred years from now without something they need and can't get. Sunlight, wind and rain are in this group as well, though for a different reason. They renew themselves. As long as we use them no faster than they come back, using them takes nothing from the future.

For other resources the answer is no, or at least not yet. Chapter 5 followed the rare-earth elements to Baotou. They occur in workable amounts in only a few places in the world, and for many of the things they are used for, no other material will do the job. The platinum-group metals are in the same position. Helium is too, because once it is released it rises through the atmosphere and escapes into space. Phosphorus is another case: every crop on Earth needs it, and nothing can take its place. The last example is the water in the great fossil aquifers. The Ogallala, under the American plains, is one of them. It took thousands of years to fill, and it is being pumped out in a few decades.

There is also a group in the middle. These are resources for which a substitute exists, or is on its way, but which are limited and valuable until the substitute takes over. Oil is the clearest example.

Chapter 7 gave a name to what separates these groups. It called it the substitutability gap, and from here on I will call it the gap. When the gap is narrow, people in the future can replace what we use. When the gap is wide, they can't. Three bins are enough to work with: low, middle and high. Each bin gets a different treatment. For a resource in the low bin, the job is to charge for the damage it does and then let the market handle the rest. For a resource in the middle bin, the job is to set aside a share of the proceeds for the people who come later, which is what Norway has done with its oil. For a resource in the high bin, the treatment is the next step.

No new science is needed to do this sorting. Geologists, materials scientists, agronomists and hydrologists already know most of what it requires, and parts of it are already done. The European Union keeps a list of the raw materials it considers critical. Under a law that took effect in May 2024, that list names thirty-four materials, and it marks seventeen of them as strategic. In the United States, the Geological Survey publishes an assessment of every major mineral each year. The one thing nobody has done is combine those pieces into a single plain ranking of how hard each resource would be to replace. The people who hold the data could do that in a matter of months.

Some economists will say the sorting isn't necessary. Their argument is that when a resource gets scarce its price goes up, and a high price gives people a reason to invent a substitute. History gives them real support. In 1980 the economist Julian Simon made a bet with the biologist Paul Ehrlich that five metals would be cheaper in ten years than they were that day, and Simon won the bet. So substitutes usually do arrive, and that is the reason the third step puts money into finding them. The sorting is there for the cases where a substitute might not arrive.

Now try the sorting on the seabed. The metals in the nodules go in the middle bin, or they are heading there, because some batteries on the road today use neither nickel nor cobalt. The seabed is a separate question from the metals. A nodule took tens of millions of years to form, and a strip of seabed that was cleared in 1979 looked the same forty-four years later. Very little on Earth sits higher in the high bin than that.

## Reserve

The second step applies to anything in the high bin. Take a share of what is known to exist, and leave that share where it is.

This is a different thing from a stockpile. To build a stockpile you dig the material up and then store it, and storage costs money, because it takes a warehouse, guards and insurance. A reserve left in the ground needs none of those. The ground stores it for free. There is platinum in South African rock that has been there for two billion years, and it will stay there for another two billion unless somebody goes in and takes it out.

So what does a reserve cost? It costs one thing, which is the money that would have been made by selling the reserved share sooner. For most of these materials that amount is small, and the reason is worth spelling out. In any given year, the amount mined is limited by how much the world wants to buy and by how fast the mines can run. It is not limited by how much is left in the ground. So if a country sets aside a fifth of a deposit, and that fifth is a part it would not have reached for decades anyway, it has given up very little today.

What the country gets in return is a choice it can make later. Suppose a substitute arrives and the material stops mattering. Then the reserve can be released, and little has been lost. Now suppose no substitute arrives. Then the reserve becomes more valuable every year, as the rest of the material is used up, and it is still there. Chapter 6 called the reasoning behind this the asymmetric regret rule, and it works by comparing two mistakes. The first mistake is to reserve something that turns out not to be needed. The cost of that mistake is that some revenue arrives later than it could have. The second mistake is to mine all of something that turns out to have no replacement. The cost of that mistake falls on everyone who comes afterward, and it is permanent. The first mistake can be undone and the second can't, so the sensible thing is to guard against the second.

A reader who trusts markets may object that a reserve is a government picking winners. It isn't, because a reserve gives nothing to any producer. All it does is keep one option from being closed. Everything outside the reserved share goes on being bought and sold the way it was before.

Nothing new has to be built for this step either. Most industrial countries already keep strategic reserves of oil, and many of them keep stockpiles of metals as well. What those countries don't have is a principle that tells them what to hold back and how much of it. The sorting in the first step answers the question of what. The regret rule answers the question of how much.

The seabed already has a reserve of this kind. In 2012 the Authority closed nine large blocks of the Clarion-Clipperton Zone to all mining. In 2021 it closed four more. The thirteen blocks together cover about 1.97 million square kilometres. So however the Authority rules on the rest of the zone, people inside it have already reasoned the way this step does.

## Invest

A reserve buys time, and the third step is about how to use that time.

The first use is to look for substitutes, so that fewer resources belong in the high bin. Some of that work is on batteries that are built on sodium or on iron, which need less lithium and no cobalt. Some of it is on magnets that work without rare earths, and on recycling that recovers metals from material that has already been mined. Some of it is on ways of farming that take less water from the aquifers and waste less phosphorus. Money spent on this work does three useful things at the same time. It makes the scarce material less important. It makes it less likely that the reserve will ever be needed. And because less has to be mined, it reduces the damage that mining does.

The second use of the time is to find other places to get the material. Chapter 7 made the argument for the place that sounds strangest, and the argument is simple. An asteroid with no life on it has no town beside it, no river below it and nobody living downwind. If every cost of mining is counted honestly, then for some metals a rock like that will one day be the cheaper source. The sooner it can be reached, the less has to be taken from ground where things live. I can't say when that day will come, and neither can anyone else.

One rule covers both uses, and the rule is not "never extract." The rule is to take first from whichever source costs the least after every cost has been counted. For most materials, and for a long time to come, the source that costs least will be a substitute. For a few materials it will eventually be somewhere other than Earth.

The argument over the seabed is really an argument about this step. The people who want to mine the nodules say that the world needs nickel and cobalt for batteries, and that mining those metals on land does terrible harm. Both of those things are true. The people who oppose mining say that the need may not last, because batteries are changing. So the two sides disagree about how fast the substitutes are coming, and that is a question about the third step.

## Reassess

The sorting in the first step will be wrong in some places. The fourth step is to go back and redo it.

It can be wrong in two directions. A material that was put in the high bin may stop belonging there. If magnets no longer need rare earths, or batteries no longer need lithium, then part of the reserve can be released. A material that was put in the low bin may also turn out to belong higher. Cobalt is an example. A generation ago it was treated as an ordinary industrial metal, and today both the United States and the European Union list it as critical. Helium is a harder lesson. In 1996 the United States Congress ordered the federal helium reserve to be sold off, at a price set by a formula. In 2010 the National Research Council reviewed the sale and found that it had kept the price of helium below what the market would have set. Helium cannot be manufactured, and once it is released it doesn't come back. The United States was selling it cheaply anyway.

So the list has to be revised on a regular schedule, and the scientific bodies that keep it now can do the revising. This is already normal practice. The European Union has revised its list about every three years since 2011. The fourth step changes only one thing, which is that a revision has consequences. When a material moves up the list it gets a reserve, and when a material moves down it can lose one.

One argument is left to the appendix. It is the question of how much weight to give a cost that will fall a hundred years from now, compared with a dollar today. Economists disagree about the right rate. Nicholas Stern is at one end of that disagreement and William Nordhaus is at the other, and the appendix shows how the numbers change under each choice.

This step also explains why the order of the seabed decision matters. If the decision is to wait, it can be looked at again at the next review. If the decision is to mine, it can't be taken back. The evidence for that is a strip of seabed that had not changed in forty-four years.

## Post the bond

The four steps decide what should be left alone. But most of what the world uses will still be taken out of the ground. For that part, the question is the one Chapter 5 asked: who pays what it costs?

This book's answer is a bond. Here is how a bond of this kind works, one feature at a time.

The party that takes the value is the party that posts the bond. That means the company that mines the ton or pumps the barrel.

The size of the bond is the cost that the price leaves out. Chapter 6 showed how to measure that cost for a single unit, such as one ton or one barrel. Chapter 8 measured it for a ton of McDowell coal.

The bond is held for the people who bear the cost. Sometimes those people can be named, as they can in a mining town or a fishing community, and then it is held for them. Sometimes they have not been born yet, and then it is held in a fund that they will inherit.

The bond is released when the cost has been made good. If the company restores the land, it gets back the money that was set aside for restoring it. Some costs can never be made good. For those the bond is never released, and it goes to the people who are left with the loss.

The bond cannot be sold. Chapter 5 tested it against the four features that the legal scholar Katharina Pistor says turn a claim into capital. The bond lacks two of them: it can't be converted into money, and it can't be transferred to someone else. So it is a debt, and the only thing anyone can do with it is pay it.

Chapter 7 showed a small version of this. In that example, investors on Earth sent a crew of twelve to mine an asteroid. For each ton the mine shipped, a set amount went into a reserve. The company could not touch the reserve, and a trustee held it on the crew's behalf. If one of the costs never happened, its share went back to the investors.

Something close to this already exists, and it is called a reclamation bond. Since 1977, American law has required a coal company to put up money before it opens a surface mine, as a guarantee that the land will be put back afterward. The idea is right, and the money is posted at the right time, which is before the digging starts. The trouble is the amount, which is far too small. In 2021 the group Appalachian Voices counted more than 633,000 acres of mined land in Appalachia that were still waiting to be reclaimed. It estimated that the work would cost between $7.5 billion and $9.8 billion. The bonds posted to pay for it came to about $3.8 billion. When a company goes bankrupt and its bond is that far short, there are only two outcomes. Either the public pays the difference or the creek stays orange, as Chapter 2 described.

The McDowell ton shows the same shortfall from the other side. The appendix adds up everything that was ever paid against the costs of each ton, by anyone. The total is at most about $88 to $100. Of that, only $8 to $13 came from the companies that mined the coal. Most of what was paid came from the public.

So it is easy to say what the repair would be. Set the bond at what the work will really cost. Raise it when costs rise. Check it again as the mine gets older. And extend it beyond the land, to the other costs that can be measured: the lungs, the water and the air. None of this is a new idea. In 1990 the economists Robert Costanza and Charles Perrings proposed that a bond be posted in advance against environmental harm. As Chapter 5 noted, their bond and this one differ in size. They set theirs against the worst damage that could happen. This one is set against the measured cost of a single act of extraction.

This is the point where the rules for the seabed are stuck. In 2026 the members of the Authority had still not agreed on who would be liable for damage to the seabed, or on how that damage would be paid for.

## The border

If a bond stops at a country's border, the largest costs stay where they are, because they fall in other countries. The rare earths in a phone were refined next to the villages near Baotou. Oil has been pumped from the Niger Delta since the 1950s, and Chapter 4 described what that left the people who live there. Until very recently most of the world's cobalt came from the Democratic Republic of the Congo. In 2014 UNICEF estimated that about forty thousand children worked in the mines in the south of that country, and Amnesty International reported the figure in 2016. In cases like these, the people who gain from a material and the people who pay for it often don't live on the same continent.

Suppose one country priced its own mining honestly and kept importing everything else at the old prices. That country would not have solved the problem. It would have moved it. Its own mines would close, and the same ore would arrive by ship from a country that left the cost behind in someone's village.

The answer is to charge an import what a producer at home would have been charged. The European Union already does a version of this, under a regulation it adopted in 2023. The regulation covers steel, cement, aluminium, fertiliser, electricity and hydrogen, and it requires importers to account for the carbon that was released in making them. Importers started reporting in October 2023. Since the first of January 2026 they have had to pay. There is one detail that matters a great deal: if the exporting country has already charged for that carbon, the amount is deducted. So an exporter that wants to keep its European customers can charge the carbon price at home, and keep the money in its own country. That gives other countries a reason to adopt a carbon price of their own. It did not take a treaty. It took one market that was large enough that other countries wanted to keep selling in it. The mechanism is new, it covers only carbon, and it may still be challenged under trade law. But it exists, and it is collecting money.

There is a serious objection to this whole approach, and it comes from the poorer countries. They point out that the rich countries became rich by burning coal and clearing land, and that they paid none of these costs while they did it. Now that they are rich, they propose to charge everyone. The economist Ha-Joon Chang called this kicking away the ladder.

The objection is right about the history, so any answer has to begin with the history. Whoever ran up the old cost owes it. The countries that filled the atmosphere first owe the largest share of what that has cost, and the researchers on climate debt whom Chapter 5 credits have been calculating that share for years. A border charge that ignored all this really would kick the ladder away. A charge that takes it into account has to do two things. It has to give poorer exporters more time to adjust, as part of its design. And it has to send money in the other direction, for the cost that has already been imposed on them.

## Why it hasn't happened

If all these pieces exist, and the reasoning is this plain, why hasn't it been done?

The economist Mancur Olson gave the answer in 1965. When a few people each have a great deal to gain, they organize. When many people each have a little to lose, they don't. That is the situation here. The companies that would have to post the bond know exactly what it would cost them. The people the bond would protect are scattered, or poor, or not yet born.

Chapter 5 described how this worked in the United States in 2008. The net worth of American households fell by roughly $11 trillion, by the Federal Reserve's count. About 4.1 million families lost their homes to completed foreclosures, by CoreLogic's count. The government rescued the banks within days. For the most part it did not rescue the households.

Chapter 5 also said that Sweden shows a different choice was possible. In the 1930s Sweden rescued its banks and its households together. Sixty years later it faced another banking crisis, and what it did then teaches the other half of the lesson. In the autumn of 1992 Sweden's banks were failing. The government guaranteed that the people who had deposited money in the banks, and the people who had lent to them, would be paid. It did not extend that guarantee to the banks' shareholders. The state took over two of the largest banks, Nordbanken and Gota. It set up two companies, Securum and Retriva, to take the banks' bad loans. Over the next few years those companies sold the loans off, and they returned what was left to the treasury. By 1995 the banks were profitable again. The economist Lars Jonung advised Sweden's prime minister during the crisis, and he later judged that the final cost to taxpayers was close to zero.

Consider who paid in Sweden. The shareholders had owned the banks and had collected the gains in the good years, and they were the ones who took the loss. The public put up the money for the rescue, and the public got ownership of the banks and much of its money back. That is the reasoning behind the bond. Sweden's government applied it after the damage was done, because it decided to. The United States had the same choice in 2008, and it chose differently.

That leads to another question. Who wrote the rules that made the American choice the easy one? Chapter 5 borrowed its tools for that question from James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock. They built a tradition called Public Choice, which treats the writing of rules as something that interested parties work to influence. Tullock described rent-seeking, which means spending money to win a privilege, as opposed to spending it to make something. I use their vocabulary, and as I said in Chapter 5, I do not sign on to Buchanan's larger politics. Their vocabulary has always been missing its other half. It can tell you who shaped a rule. It has never included a count of what the rule cost and who was handed the bill, and a ledger supplies that count. With both halves together, something becomes possible that neither half can do alone: the people on the losing side can be told exactly how much they have at stake. Olson's many each lose only a little, but that is true only for as long as nobody adds it up.

There is one more reason it hasn't happened, and it is the one to watch for most closely. When a hidden cost comes to light and people get angry about it, paying the cost is not the cheapest way to respond. The cheapest way is to issue a certificate. The Kimberley Process is an example. It began in 2003, and it certifies that a rough diamond did not pay for a rebel war. That is worth knowing. But the certificate does not say what the miner was paid, and it does not say what the mine left behind. A buyer who pays more for a certified stone can't tell where the extra money went. The cost is still where it was.

That gives us a test for any fix, including the fixes in this chapter. The test has two questions. Did money reach the people who bear the cost? And can the party that owes the money be let off with paperwork alone? A bond is designed to pass both. It is held for the people who bear the cost, and it is released only when the cost has actually been made good. If the party that posts a bond is allowed to certify for itself that the bond has been paid, then the bond is no better than a diamond certificate.

## The pieces that exist

It helps to count what is already in place.

Carbon has a price in a growing part of the world. In its 2026 report the World Bank counted 87 carbon taxes and trading systems in operation. Together they cover just over 29 percent of global emissions. Most of the prices are low. The average is about $21 a tonne. In 2023 the United States Environmental Protection Agency estimated that a tonne of carbon dioxide does $190 of damage, so the average price is a small part of the cost. Still, the machinery works. It has been defended in courts and in parliaments, and each year it covers more.

Funds built on natural resources exist. Chapter 4 told the story of Norway's. It also told the story of Alaska's. In 1976 the voters of Alaska wrote into the state constitution that at least a quarter of the state's oil and mineral royalties must go into a permanent fund. Since 1982 that fund has paid a dividend to every resident every year, and it holds close to $90 billion.

The usual reply is that Norway is small, rich and trusting, so what works in Norway won't work anywhere else. Chapter 4 answered that reply at length, with places that are nothing like Norway. Alaska is one of them. Chattanooga, Tennessee, is another: a utility owned by the city built a fibre network across its whole territory, over a lawsuit from a private carrier, and it runs the network at a profit. Mondragón, in the Basque country of Spain, is a third. A federation of companies owned by their workers has competed there in ordinary markets since 1956. Vienna is a fourth. About six in ten of its residents live in public or non-profit housing, which the city began building in 1919. None of these places had Norway's conditions. In each one, people built the thing on purpose and then defended it.

Reserves exist. Most industrial countries hold oil in strategic storage. In 2010 China restricted its exports of rare earths, and Japan responded by paying to develop a supply outside China. The United States has one rare-earth mine, at Mountain Pass in California. It closed in 2002, its owner went bankrupt in 2015, it was bought in 2017, and it reopened in 2018. The European law of 2024 sets targets for how much of each strategic material should be mined, refined and recycled inside Europe. All of these were done for the sake of security. None was done for the sake of the future. But they rest on the same judgment as the reserve in this chapter, which is that some materials are too hard to replace to be left to this year's price.

Bonds exist, as the section on the bond described. So does the habit of looking before digging. Since 1970 American law has required a study of the environmental effects of any major federal project before the project starts, and many other countries have adopted the practice. A study like that does not put a price on anything. What it does is state in public what will be lost.

There is also a piece in finance. Chapter 5 described the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010, which required banks to hold more of their own money against their own risks. The law was partial, and parts of it were loosened later. Even so, it is the idea behind the bond, applied to banks.

And there is the Authority in Kingston. It is a body that has to rule before anything is taken, and the thing it rules on is a resource that a treaty says belongs to everyone.

Every one of these pieces is partial. Each was built for its own reasons, in the middle of its own crisis, and nobody designed any of them to fit with the others. But every part of what this chapter describes is already operating somewhere, and each has survived enough elections that people treat it as ordinary. So nothing in this chapter has to be invented. The work that remains is to connect the pieces, to size them honestly, and to extend them to the costs they don't yet cover.

## Two objections of right

The strongest objections that remain are about rights. There are two, and they come from opposite directions.

The first says that property rights already solve the problem. In 1960 the economist Ronald Coase showed that when rights are clearly assigned and bargaining is cheap, the parties to a harm will settle it between themselves, and it does not matter which of them holds the right at the start. There is a good deal of evidence that owners take care of what they own. Fisheries recover when the catch is divided into shares that people can own. Forests grow back when the owner earns something from timber that is left standing. I don't dispute any of this. When something is used up in the present, by people who are able to sit down at a table together, property is usually the best tool available.

It fails in three places, and they are the three places this book is about. The first is that people who have not been born hold no deeds and can't make an offer, so the bargain Coase described takes place without its largest party. The second is that costs do not stop at fences. Acid drainage, carbon and the dust from a tailings pond cross every property line. The third is that a market can only price what people know now. If the most important use of a deposit has not been invented yet, the deposit will be sold for what it is worth today. Elinor Ostrom showed that communities can govern a shared resource well without private title and without the state. Her principles work where the people who bear the costs share the fate of the resource. The cases in this book are the ones where they don't share it.

The second form of the property objection goes deeper, because it is not an argument about what works. The philosopher Robert Nozick made it in 1974, in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*. He argued that a person holds something justly if it was justly acquired and justly transferred, and that no calculation of efficiency or need gives anyone else the right to override that. Seen that way, a bond is a charge imposed by force on property that its owners came by honestly. This is not the same objection as Coase's. It is also not the same as the fear of a state that reaches too far, which Chapter 10 takes up.

The answer comes from Nozick's own account. Nozick followed the philosopher John Locke on how a person may justly take something out of what is held in common. Locke's condition was that the taking is just only if enough and as good is left for others. That condition is known as the Lockean proviso. The plainest case of failing it is a stock that can't be replaced and is drawn down until nothing is left, because then there is not enough and as good for the people who come after, whatever they are willing to pay. The same test applies to a living stock that is taken faster than it renews. On Nozick's own terms, an acquisition that fails the proviso is not a just acquisition, and so the title that follows from it is not the clean title the objection assumes. The bond is the price of meeting the proviso. It is the payment that leaves the others whole. So the bond does not override the boundary Nozick drew. It enforces it.

The objection from the other direction is that all of this hands nature over to the market. Chapter 5 answered the first form of that objection. Karl Polanyi and Nancy Fraser refused to treat nature as an asset, and I don't claim they would approve of putting a price on it. What I do say is that the bond counts a cost, and it creates nothing that can be traded. Chapter 5 also promised a fuller answer to Brett Christophers, and this is where it is owed.

Christophers has written two books that bear on this one. The first is *The Price Is Wrong*, from 2024. It argues that getting the price right will not save the climate, because investors follow profit and not price. Solar and wind power became cheap. The money still did not arrive at the scale that was needed, because cheap power is not especially profitable power. Christophers concludes that markets can't do this job and that public investment has to do it. I think he is right about investment, and it does not change any of the steps above. Charging coal what it costs will not build a single wind farm. What it will do is show how large a subsidy coal has been receiving, and who has been paying it. Public investment has always needed that argument, and it has never had it in figures.

His earlier book is the sharper threat to this one. It is *Rentier Capitalism*, from 2020. It describes an economy that earns more and more from owning scarce things and charging people for access to them, and less and less from making anything. At first sight, a bond posted over a natural resource looks like exactly that: fence off the commons, put a title on it, and collect. But an asset is useful to a rentier only if it can be owned, can be sold, and pays a stream of income. The bond has none of those three features. Nobody owns it as property. It can't be sold. It pays no return to anyone. It is a record of what is owed, and it is cancelled when that amount is paid. What the rentier has actually been doing all along is leaving a cost unpaid. The bond names that cost and sends the bill back.

## What a framework does

The hardest question comes from people who have tried to pass a law. They point out that economists have agreed for thirty years that carbon should carry a price. After those thirty years, most of the world's carbon still carries none, and where it does carry a price, the price is a fraction of the damage. If the world can't manage that much, why would anyone think it can manage this? Where is the coalition, and where is the treaty?

I don't have a coalition or a treaty. Chapter 5 said as much: this book does not pretend to write the legislation. No book could, because the route is different in every country, and the people who live in each country are the ones who will find it.

What a book can do is smaller than that, and it has to come first. Here are three examples. In 2003 the geographer David Harvey gave a name to wealth that is gathered by taking and not by making. He called it accumulation by dispossession. Harvey passed no law. But many people had watched this happen to their neighbourhoods and their land, and after Harvey they had a word for it. People cannot organize against something they have no name for. In 1990 Elinor Ostrom set out the principles by which communities had governed shared forests and fisheries for centuries. Governments and companies had treated those communities as backward, and by naming their principles Ostrom gave the communities standing against them. More recently Daniel Susskind has done something similar for economic growth, by asking what it is for.

Harvey named the taking. This book adds the amount. A ton of McDowell coal sold for just under five dollars and left costs of more than five hundred. The book adds a word for what was left, a way to measure it, and an instrument sized to it. Those are things people can use. A county commission can use them to set a reclamation bond. A state can use them to decide what to do with its royalties. A delegate in Kingston can use them too.

Whether anyone will use them is a separate matter, and there is a real reason for doubt. The philosopher Stephen Gardiner named that reason in 2011, in *A Perfect Moral Storm*. He called it "the tyranny of the contemporary." The generation that has the power to act is never the generation that pays for failing to act, and the people who would hold it to account don't exist yet. Think about what that means for a bond. The generation that could post a bond for the future decides for itself whether to post it. If it posts the bond, it pays. If it doesn't, it answers to nobody. Nobody in that account has to be a villain. The temptation to put the decision off is built into the situation, and it lasts longer than good intentions do.

The only honest answer is to design against that temptation, and that is what a bond is for. A bond is a way for a generation to tie its own hands before the temptation arrives, the way a constitution does. It has also been done. Norway set up its fund in 1990 and made the first deposit in 1996. In 2001 it adopted a rule that limits how much of the fund any government may spend, and in 2017 it made that rule stricter. It taxes the carbon from its own oil platforms. Through every change of government since then, living Norwegians have bound themselves on behalf of people who are not yet born. Gardiner explains why the future usually loses. Norway is the evidence that it doesn't have to lose.

So think about the people who have been in this book. There is a miner in McDowell County, West Virginia. There is a waterman on the Chesapeake Bay, and a villager who lives beside the waste ponds at Baotou. There is a farmer who pumps water from the Ogallala. And there is an administrator on another world, deciding how much of the ice to leave. These five will never meet. Each one is standing in a place where a cost lands, or is about to land. It is the same cost every time, and it is moved by the same means. And somewhere there is a person with a vote on the seabed who has not yet cast it. The question that remains is no longer about policy. It is about what those five, and that person, and you, and I, have to do with one another. That is the subject of the last chapter.
