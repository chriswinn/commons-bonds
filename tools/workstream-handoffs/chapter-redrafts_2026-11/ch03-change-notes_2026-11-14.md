### One strongest-critic read of draft B (run once; where a hostile but fair reader would stop)

1. **Who is "the industry," and is the waterman part of it?** The chapter says the tongs and dredges ground the reef down, and it also says the watermen are the people left paying. Then the column says "the industry" has paid zero and asks "the parties who drew it down, and their successors" to fund the rebuilding. Darling closed in 1979. A critic will ask who exactly gets the bill: the packers, the dredge fleets, the cities that ate the oysters, or today's watermen and oyster houses. (A critic may also point out that harvesters pay state oyster taxes and fees; I have not checked what those fund.) Do you want to name the party?

2. **If the public has already spent more than the floor, what is unpaid?** The floor is $20 to $37 million for one river. The public has spent about $115 million across the Bay, and Virginia's share alone is about the size of the floor. The draft says the two can't be subtracted and that the point is who paid. But two paragraphs earlier it says the cost "stayed home and went unpaid." Should that sentence say "went unpaid by the people who took the value"?

3. **Is $120 million against $7 million a fair comparison?** It sets today's price for a single oyster on a restaurant plate against today's dockside price for a bushel, and applies both to a harvest that was mostly shucked, packed in gallons and shipped. A plate price also pays for the restaurant's rent and staff. A critic will call it the kind of number the chapter says it avoids. Keep it, qualify it, or give the dock figure alone?

4. **Does the moratorium disprove Hardin, or prove him?** Hardin's second cure is "rule imposed from above," and the 1985 moratorium was imposed by state governments. The draft says the watermen tied up "by agreement." A critic who knows the history will ask how many agreed and how many were ordered. Is there a source for the agreement, or should the claim be narrower?

5. **Disease.** MSX and Dermo are named as the second cause of the oyster's collapse. A critic will say parasites, not people, did much of the damage, and that a bill for the reef cannot be sent to anyone for a disease. The floor prices only reef bottom lost by 1981 in the James. Is it worth one sentence saying the floor counts ground-down bottom and not oysters killed by disease?

6. **Menhaden: can the chapter claim a cost that its own sources say has not been measured?** Nesslage and Latour both say there is no data on menhaden in the Bay. The draft then says the cost "stays here, on the rockfish, the ospreys, and the watermen," and the osprey link is given as a suspicion. A critic will say the honest claim is that nobody has been willing to pay to find out. Does the paragraph need to say that instead?

7. **Is the watermen's disaster a case of cost severance, or of competition and conservation?** The draft says the oyster harvest is undercut by Gulf Coast farms and by aquaculture, and that the sanctuaries are closed to harvest. A critic will say the first is ordinary market competition and the second is the very public restoration the chapter credits. What is the cost that was shifted onto the watermen, and who shifted it? The draft's answer ("Gulf Coast operations that never bore the costs the Bay's recovery is now carrying") is one clause; it may need to be the point of the paragraph.

Also noted, not a critic point: "When the proposal was to raise the harvest, the committees acted with confidence" has no vote or date behind it in the drafts. Colden's quotation supports it for menhaden; nothing on file supports it for striped bass.
