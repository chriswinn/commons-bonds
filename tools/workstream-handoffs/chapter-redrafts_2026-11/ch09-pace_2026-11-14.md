# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 9: Pricing Honestly

## A decision not yet made

In 1979 a machine weighing about a hundred tonnes was lowered to the floor of the Pacific Ocean, some five thousand metres down, and driven across it. It was a test. The seabed there is scattered with dark lumps of rock the size of potatoes, called nodules, which hold nickel, cobalt, copper and manganese, and the machine was built to find out whether they could be gathered up. It cleared a strip about eight metres wide and left two deep furrows where it had passed.

In 2023 a research ship went back to look. The scientists on board found the strip within seven or eight hours of their first dive. "Forty-four years later," said Daniel Jones of Britain's National Oceanography Centre, who co-led the expedition, "the mining tracks themselves look very similar to when they were first made." The small animals that live in the mud had come back in something like their old numbers. The larger animals that live attached to the nodules had not, because the nodules were gone, and a nodule takes tens of millions of years to form. The team published what it found in the journal *Nature* in 2025.

That strip lies in a stretch of seabed between Hawaii and Mexico called the Clarion-Clipperton Zone. No country owns it. The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea calls the deep seabed "the common heritage of mankind," and in 1994 it set up a body to look after it, the International Seabed Authority, which sits in Kingston, Jamaica. The Authority has given out thirty-one contracts to explore the seabed. It has never given one to mine it, because it has never finished writing the rules under which mining would be allowed.

It has been trying to finish for years. In June 2021 the island nation of Nauru, which sponsors a subsidiary of a mining firm called The Metals Company, invoked a clause in the treaty that gave the Authority two years to complete the rules. The two years ran out in July 2023 with no rules. The Authority's council met again in July 2026 and again adopted none, and the talks are set to resume in March 2027. Meanwhile the company has gone another way. The United States never joined the treaty, and by January 2026 the company's American subsidiary had applied to the United States government for a permit to mine part of the zone under an American law passed in 1980.

So somewhere, in Kingston or in Washington, somebody has to decide. I take no position here on what they should rule. What interests me is that this is the thing the earlier chapters kept arriving too late for. In McDowell County and Libby and on the Chesapeake, the taking was done before anyone counted, and all that was left to do was write the bill afterward. Chapter 8 wrote one. A ton of McDowell coal sold at the mine in 1960 for just under five dollars, and the costs it left behind, counted today, come to more than five hundred. Chapter 5 ended by asking what it would take to post the bond before the cost is cut loose, and not after the party that gained has gone. Chapter 7 put that question to an imagined administrator on another world. This administrator is real, the stock is real, and nothing has been taken yet.

How would anyone decide a thing like that? There is a way to go about it, and none of its parts is new. It has four steps, which I will call Classify, Reserve, Invest and Reassess, and then a fifth piece for whatever gets taken anyway, which is the bond this book is named for.

## Classify

Start by sorting. Different resources put different things at risk, and the question that sorts them is a simple one: if we use this up, could the people who come after us replace it?

For some things the answer is yes. Iron is plentiful on this planet and on most of the rocky bodies near it. Coal, as a fuel, can be replaced, because we already know how to heat a house and run a power grid without it. What coal costs is the damage it does, to lungs and creeks and the air, and that damage has to be paid for. But nobody two hundred years from now will be stuck because we burned it. Sunlight, wind and rain belong in this group too, for a different reason: they come back by themselves. Used no faster than they renew, they cost the future nothing.

For other things the answer is no, or not yet. The rare-earth elements that Chapter 5 followed to Baotou are found in workable amounts in only a few places, and for many of their uses nothing else will do. The platinum-group metals are like that. So is helium, which leaks out of the atmosphere into space once it is released. So is phosphorus, which every crop on Earth needs and nothing replaces. And so is the water in the great fossil aquifers, such as the Ogallala under the American plains, which took thousands of years to fill and is being pumped out in decades.

Between the two sits a middle group, where a substitute exists or is coming but the resource is finite and valuable in the meantime. Oil is the clearest case.

Chapter 7 gave what separates those groups a name, the substitutability gap, and I will just call it the gap from here on. Where the gap is narrow, the future can replace what we use. Where it is wide, it can't. Three bins are enough: low, middle, high. For the low bin, the job is to charge for the damage and let the market do the rest. For the middle bin, it is to capture a share of the proceeds for the people who come later, the way Norway has done with its oil. The high bin is where the next step comes in.

None of this needs a new science. Geologists, materials scientists, agronomists and hydrologists already know most of what the sort requires. The European Union keeps a list of raw materials it considers critical, and under a law that took effect in May 2024 the list names thirty-four of them, seventeen of which it marks as strategic. The United States Geological Survey publishes an assessment of every major mineral each year. What nobody has yet done is put those pieces into one plain ranking by how hard each resource would be to replace, and that is a matter of months of work for people who already have the data.

An economist of a certain school will say the sort isn't needed, because scarcity raises prices and high prices call forth substitutes. There is real history behind that. In 1980 the economist Julian Simon bet the biologist Paul Ehrlich that five metals would be cheaper in ten years, and Simon won. Substitution usually does win, which is why the third step puts money into it. The sort is for the cases where it might not.

Try it on the seabed. The metals in the nodules belong in the middle bin, or on their way there, because batteries that use neither nickel nor cobalt are already on the road. The seabed itself is another matter. A nodule that took tens of millions of years to form, and a strip that looks the same after forty-four, sit about as high in the high bin as anything on Earth.

## Reserve

For anything in the high bin, set aside a share of what is known to exist and leave it where it is.

That is different from a stockpile. A stockpile is dug up and then stored, and storing things costs money: a warehouse, guards, insurance. A reserve in the ground needs none of that. The ground is free storage. Platinum that has sat in South African rock for two billion years will sit there for two billion more unless somebody goes and gets it.

So what does a reserve cost? Only the money that would have been made by selling the reserved share sooner. For most of these materials that is not much, because what limits mining in any given year is how much the world is buying and how fast the mines can run, not how much is left in the ground. A country that sets aside a fifth of a deposit it would not have reached for decades has given up very little today.

What it has bought is an option. If a substitute arrives and the material stops mattering, the reserve can be released and little was lost. If no substitute arrives, the reserve is worth more every year the rest runs down, and it is there. Chapter 6 called the reasoning behind this the asymmetric regret rule. Compare the two ways of being wrong. Reserve something that turns out not to be needed, and the cost is some revenue that came later than it might have. Fail to reserve something that turns out to have no replacement, and the cost is a door closed on everyone who comes afterward, for good. When one mistake can be undone and the other can't, you guard against the one that can't.

A reader who trusts markets may object that this is a government picking winners. But a reserve hands nothing to any producer. It is a decision not to close an option, and everything outside the reserved share is still bought and sold as before.

Nothing new has to be built to do it, either. Most industrial countries already keep strategic reserves of oil, and many keep stockpiles of metals. What they lack is a principle for what to hold back and how much. The sort in the first step supplies the what, and the regret rule supplies the how much.

The seabed already has a reserve. In 2012 the Authority closed nine large blocks of the Clarion-Clipperton Zone to any mining, and in 2021 it added four more. Together the thirteen cover about 1.97 million square kilometres. Whatever the Authority rules about the rest, somebody there has already reasoned the way this step does.

## Invest

A reserve buys time. The third step is what the time is for.

The first use of it is to find substitutes, so that the high bin gets smaller. That means batteries built on sodium or on iron, which need less lithium and no cobalt. It means magnets that work without rare earths, recycling that recovers metals from what has already been mined, and farming that draws less from the aquifers and wastes less phosphorus. Money spent this way does three things at once. It makes the scarce material matter less. It makes the reserve less likely ever to be needed. And it cuts the damage done by whatever mining still goes on, because there is less of it.

The second use is to find other places to get the material. Chapter 7 made the case that sounds strangest and is the simplest. A lifeless asteroid has no town beside it, no river below it and no one downwind. If the full cost of mining is counted honestly, rock like that will one day be the cheaper source for some metals, and the sooner it can be reached the less has to come out of living ground. I can't say when that day comes. Nobody can.

The rule that ties the two together is not "never extract." It is to take first from whichever source costs least once every cost is counted. For most materials, for a long time to come, that will be a substitute. For a few, it will eventually be somewhere other than Earth.

This is where the seabed argument is actually being fought. The case for mining the nodules is that the world needs nickel and cobalt for batteries, and that mining them on land does terrible harm, which is true. The case against is that the need may not last. Both sides are making a claim about the third step: how fast the substitutes are coming.

## Reassess

The sort will be wrong in places, so it has to be redone.

It can be wrong in either direction. A material in the high bin can drop out of it. If magnets stop needing rare earths, or batteries stop needing lithium, part of the reserve can be released. A material in the low bin can also turn out to belong higher. A generation ago cobalt was an ordinary industrial metal, and today it is on the critical lists of the United States and the European Union. Helium is the sharper lesson. In 1996 the United States Congress ordered the federal helium reserve sold off at a price fixed by formula, and a 2010 review by the National Research Council found that the sale had held the price of helium below what the market would have set. The United States was selling cheaply something that cannot be made and does not come back.

So the list is revised on a schedule, by the same scientific bodies that keep it now. The European Union has revised its list about every three years since 2011. What changes under this step is only that the revision has consequences: a material that moves up gets a reserve, and one that moves down can lose it.

There is one argument I am leaving to the appendix, which is how heavily to weigh a cost that falls a century from now against a dollar today. Economists disagree about that rate, with Nicholas Stern at one end and William Nordhaus at the other, and the appendix shows what each choice does to the numbers.

Reassessment also explains why the order of the seabed decision matters. A decision to wait can be revisited at the next review. A decision to mine cannot, on the evidence of a strip of seabed that has not changed in forty-four years.

## Post the bond

The four steps decide what to leave alone. Most of what the world uses will still be taken, and for that the question is Chapter 5's: who pays what it costs?

The answer this book gives is a bond. Here is how one works.

Whoever takes the value posts it. That is the company that mines the ton or pumps the barrel.

It is sized to the cost that the price leaves out. Chapter 6 showed how to measure that for a single unit, one ton or one barrel, and Chapter 8 did it for a ton of McDowell coal.

It is held for whoever bears the cost. Where those people can be named, as a mining town or a fishing community can, it is held for them. Where they haven't been born, it is held in a fund they will inherit.

It is released when the cost has been made good. If the land is restored, the money for restoring it comes back. For a cost that can never be made good, the bond is never released, and it passes to the people who are left with the loss.

And it cannot be sold. Chapter 5 put the bond to Katharina Pistor's test and showed that it lacks the two features that let a claim circulate as capital: it can't be converted into money and it can't be transferred. It is a debt, and the only thing anyone can do with it is pay it.

Chapter 7 showed this in miniature, with a crew of twelve sent to mine an asteroid for investors on Earth. Each ton shipped paid into a reserve the company couldn't touch, held by a trustee for the crew, and whatever was never needed went back to the investors.

The nearest thing to this that already exists is the reclamation bond. Since 1977, American law has required a coal company to post money before it opens a surface mine, to guarantee that the land will be put back. It is the right idea, and it is posted at the right time. It is also far too small. In 2021 the group Appalachian Voices counted more than 633,000 acres of mined land in Appalachia still waiting to be reclaimed. It put the cost of the work at $7.5 billion to $9.8 billion and the bonds posted against it at about $3.8 billion. When a company goes bankrupt with its bond that short, the public pays the difference or the creek stays orange, as Chapter 2 described.

The McDowell ton shows the same thing from the other side. By the appendix's count, at most about $88 to $100 was ever paid against the costs of each ton, by anyone, and only $8 to $13 of that came from the companies that mined it. Most of what was paid came from the public.

So the repair is easy to state. Size the bond to what the work will really cost. Raise it as costs rise. Check it again as the mine ages. Extend it past the land to the other costs that can be measured, the lungs and the water and the air. None of that is a new idea. In 1990 the economists Robert Costanza and Charles Perrings proposed a bond posted in advance against environmental harm, and as Chapter 5 noted, the difference is in the size: theirs was set against the worst damage that could happen, and this one is set against the measured cost of a single act of extraction.

The rules for the seabed are stuck on exactly this. Among the questions the Authority's members had still not settled in 2026 were who is liable for damage on the seabed and how it would be paid for.

## The border

A bond that stops at the border leaves the largest costs where they are. The rare earths in a phone were refined beside the villages near Baotou. Oil has been pumped from the Niger Delta since the 1950s, and Chapter 4 described what that left the people who live there. Until very recently most of the world's cobalt came from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where UNICEF estimated in 2014 that about forty thousand children worked in the mines of the south, a figure Amnesty International reported in 2016. The people who gain from these materials and the people who pay for them often don't share a continent.

A country that priced its own mining honestly and went on importing everything else unpriced would have moved the problem and not solved it. Its own mines would close, and the same ore would arrive by ship with the cost left behind in someone else's village.

The answer is to charge imports what a domestic producer would have been charged. One version of this is already running. Under a regulation adopted in 2023, the European Union requires importers of steel, cement, aluminium, fertiliser, electricity and hydrogen to account for the carbon released in making them. Importers began reporting in October 2023, and since the first of January 2026 they have had to pay. If the exporting country has already charged for that carbon, the amount is deducted. That detail is what makes the thing spread. An exporter that wants to keep its European customers can charge the carbon price at home and keep the money. It needed no treaty. It needed one market large enough that others wanted to stay in it. The mechanism is new, it covers only carbon, and it may yet be challenged under trade law. But it is real, and it is collecting.

There is a serious objection to all of this, and it comes from the poorer countries. The rich countries got rich burning coal and clearing land without paying any of these costs. Now that they are rich, they propose to charge everyone. The economist Ha-Joon Chang called this kicking away the ladder.

The objection is right about the history, and the answer has to start there. Whoever ran up the old cost owes it. The countries that filled the atmosphere first owe the largest share of the bill for it, and the climate-debt work that Chapter 5 credits has been calculating that share for years. A border charge that ignored this would be the ladder kicked away. One that is honest about it does two things: it gives poorer exporters longer to adjust, by design, and it sends money the other way for the cost already imposed.

## Why it hasn't happened

If the pieces exist and the logic is this plain, why hasn't it been done?

The economist Mancur Olson answered that in 1965. A few people with a great deal to gain will organize, and many people who each lose a little will not. The companies that would post the bond know exactly what it would cost them. The people it would protect are scattered, or poor, or not yet born.

Chapter 5 told how that played out in the United States in 2008. Household net worth fell by roughly $11 trillion, by the Federal Reserve's count, and about 4.1 million families lost their homes to completed foreclosures, by CoreLogic's. The banks were rescued within days, and the households mostly were not.

Chapter 5 also said that Sweden shows another way was open. In the 1930s Sweden rescued its banks and its households together. Sixty years later it showed the other half of the lesson. In the autumn of 1992 Sweden's banks were failing, and the government guaranteed that their depositors and lenders would be paid. It did not guarantee the shareholders. The state took over two of the largest banks, Nordbanken and Gota. It moved their bad loans into two companies it set up for the purpose, Securum and Retriva, and those companies sold the loans off over the next few years and returned what was left to the treasury. The banks were profitable again by 1995. The economist Lars Jonung, who advised Sweden's prime minister during the crisis, later judged that the final cost to taxpayers was close to zero.

Look at who paid. The people who had owned the banks, and had taken the gains in the good years, took the loss. The public, which put up the money, got the ownership and much of the money back. That is the bond's logic applied after the fact by a government that decided to apply it. The United States in 2008 had the same choice and made a different one.

Then who wrote the rules that made the American choice the easy one? Chapter 5 borrowed the tools for that question from James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock. They built the tradition called Public Choice, which treats rule-making as something interested parties work to shape, and Tullock described rent-seeking, which is spending money to win a privilege and not to make anything. I use their vocabulary without signing on to Buchanan's larger politics, as I said there. What the vocabulary needs, and has never had, is the other half of the account: a count of what the privilege cost and who was handed the bill. Their tradition can tell you who shaped the rule. A ledger tells you what the rule took, and from whom. Put together, they can do something neither does alone, which is tell the people on the losing side exactly how much they have at stake. Olson's many, who each lose a little, lose a little only for as long as nobody adds it up.

There is one more reason it hasn't happened, and it is the one to watch for. When a hidden cost comes to light and people get angry, the cheapest response is not to pay the cost. It is to issue a certificate. The Kimberley Process, which began in 2003, certifies that a rough diamond did not pay for a rebel war. That is worth knowing. But the certificate says nothing about what the miner was paid or what the mine left behind, and the buyer who pays extra for a certified stone has no way to tell where the extra went. The cost is still where it was.

That gives a test for any fix, including the ones in this chapter. Did money reach the people who bear the cost? And can the party that owes it be let off by paperwork alone? A bond is built to pass both. It is held for the people who bear the cost, and it is released only when the cost has actually been made good. A bond that its own issuer can certify as paid is a diamond certificate.

## The pieces that exist

Count what is already in place.

Carbon has a price in a growing part of the world. The World Bank counted 87 carbon taxes and trading systems in operation in its 2026 report, covering just over 29 percent of global emissions. Most of those prices are low. The average is about $21 a tonne, against the $190 that the United States Environmental Protection Agency estimated in 2023 as the damage a tonne does. But the machinery works, it is defended in court and in parliaments, and it covers more each year.

Resource funds exist. Chapter 4 told the story of Norway's, and of Alaska's, where voters in 1976 wrote into the state constitution that at least a quarter of oil and mineral royalties go into a permanent fund. That fund has paid a dividend to every resident every year since 1982, and it holds close to $90 billion.

The usual reply is that Norway is small, rich and trusting, and that what works there won't work elsewhere. Chapter 4 answered that at length with places that look nothing like Norway. Alaska is one. In Chattanooga, Tennessee, a city-owned utility built a fibre network across its whole territory, over a lawsuit from a private carrier, and runs it at a profit. In Mondragón, in the Basque country of Spain, a federation of worker-owned companies has competed in ordinary markets since 1956. In Vienna, about six in ten residents live in public or non-profit housing, which the city began building in 1919. None of them needed Norway's conditions. Each was built on purpose and then defended.

Reserves exist. Most industrial countries hold oil in strategic storage. After China restricted rare-earth exports in 2010, Japan paid to develop a supply outside China. The one rare-earth mine in the United States, at Mountain Pass in California, closed in 2002, saw its owner go bankrupt in 2015, was bought in 2017 and reopened in 2018. The European law of 2024 sets targets for mining, refining and recycling its strategic materials at home. Each of these was done for security and not for the future's sake, but each rests on the same judgment, that some materials are too hard to replace to leave to this year's price.

Bonds exist, as the last section said, and so does the habit of looking before digging. Since 1970 American law has required a study of the environmental effects of any major federal project before it starts, and many other countries have adopted the practice. The study puts no price on anything, but it sets down in public what will be lost.

There is a piece in finance too. Chapter 5 described the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010, which made banks hold more of their own money against their own risks. It was partial, and parts of it were later loosened. It is still the bond's idea, applied to banks.

And there is the Authority in Kingston: a body that has to rule before anything is taken, on a stock that a treaty says belongs to everyone.

Every one of these is partial. Each was built for its own reasons, during its own crisis, and none was designed with the others in mind. But every part of what this chapter describes is already running somewhere, and has lasted through enough elections to be ordinary. Nothing here has to be invented. The work is to connect the parts, size them honestly, and extend them to what they don't yet cover.

## Two objections of right

The strongest objections left are about rights, and they come from opposite directions.

The first says that property rights already solve this. Ronald Coase showed in 1960 that when rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, the parties to a harm will settle it between themselves, whoever holds the right at the start. There is a great deal of evidence that owners look after what they own. Fisheries recover when the catch is divided into shares that can be owned. Forests grow back when the owner profits from standing timber. I don't dispute any of it. For things that are used up in the present, by people who can sit at a table, property is usually the best tool there is.

It fails in three places, and they are the three this book is about. People who haven't been born hold no deeds and can't bid, so the bargain Coase described is missing its largest party. Costs don't stop at fences: acid drainage, carbon and the dust from a tailings pond cross every property line there is. And a market can price only what people know now. A deposit whose most important use hasn't been invented will be sold for what it is worth today. Elinor Ostrom showed that communities can govern a shared resource well without private title or the state, and her principles work where those who bear the costs share the resource's fate. The cases in this book are the ones where they don't.

The second version of the property objection goes deeper, because it is not about what works. Robert Nozick argued in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, in 1974, that a holding is just if it was justly acquired and justly transferred, and that no calculation of efficiency or need entitles anyone else to override it. On that view a bond is a charge laid by force on property its owners came by honestly. It is a different objection from Coase's, and different again from the fear of an overreaching state, which Chapter 10 takes up.

The answer is in Nozick's own account. He held, following John Locke, that taking from what is held in common is just only where enough and as good is left for others. That condition is called the Lockean proviso. Its plainest case is a stock that can't be replaced, drawn down until nothing is left: then there is not enough and as good for the people who come after, at any price. But the same test applies to a living stock taken faster than it renews. An acquisition that fails the proviso is not, on Nozick's terms, a just acquisition, and the title that follows from it is not the clean title the objection assumes. The bond is what the proviso costs. It is the payment that leaves the others whole, and so it enforces a boundary Nozick drew himself.

The objection from the other direction is that all this hands nature to the market. Chapter 5 met the first form of it. Karl Polanyi and Nancy Fraser refused to treat nature as an asset, and I don't claim they would approve of pricing it; the bond counts a cost and creates nothing that can be traded. Chapter 5 also promised a fuller answer to Brett Christophers, and it is owed here.

Christophers has written two books that bear on this one. *The Price Is Wrong*, from 2024, argues that getting the price right will not save the climate, because investors follow profit and not price. Solar and wind power became cheap, and the money still didn't come at the scale needed, because cheap power is not especially profitable power. His conclusion is that markets can't do this job and public investment has to. I think he is right about investment, and it changes nothing in the steps above. Charging coal what it costs does not by itself build a single wind farm. What it does is show the size of the subsidy that coal has been getting, and who has been paying it, and that is the argument public investment has always needed and never had in figures.

His earlier book is the sharper threat. *Rentier Capitalism*, from 2020, describes an economy that earns more and more from owning scarce things and charging for access to them, and less from making anything. On its face a bond posted over a natural resource looks like exactly that: fence off the commons, put a title on it, collect. But a rentier's asset has to be ownable, saleable and good for a stream of income, and the bond is none of those. Nobody owns it as property. It can't be sold. It pays nobody a return. It records what is owed and is cancelled when that is paid. The thing the rentier has been doing all along is leaving a cost unpaid, and the bond names that cost and sends the bill back.

## What a framework does

The hardest question comes from people who have tried to pass a law. Economists have agreed for thirty years that carbon should carry a price, and after thirty years most of the world's carbon carries none, and where it does the price is a fraction of the damage. If the world can't manage that, what makes anyone think it can manage this? Where is the coalition, and where is the treaty?

I don't have them, and Chapter 5 said so: this book does not pretend to write the legislation. No book could. The route is different in every country, and it will be found by the people who live there.

What a book can do is smaller, and it comes first. In 2003 the geographer David Harvey gave a name, accumulation by dispossession, to wealth gathered by taking and not by making. He passed no law. But people who had watched it happen to their neighbourhoods and their land had a word for it afterward, and you cannot organize against a thing you can't name. In 1990 Elinor Ostrom set out the principles by which communities had been governing shared forests and fisheries for centuries, and by naming them she gave those communities standing against governments and companies that had treated them as backward. Daniel Susskind has lately done something similar for economic growth, by asking what it is for.

Harvey named the taking. What this book adds is the amount: a ton of McDowell coal that sold for just under five dollars and left costs of more than five hundred. It adds a word for what was left, a way to measure it, and an instrument sized to it. A county commission setting a reclamation bond can use those. So can a state deciding what to do with its royalties, or a delegate in Kingston.

Whether anyone will is another matter, and there is a real reason to doubt it. The philosopher Stephen Gardiner gave it a name in *A Perfect Moral Storm*, in 2011: "the tyranny of the contemporary." The generation that has the power to act is never the one that pays for not acting, and the people who would hold it to account don't exist yet. A generation that could post a bond for the future decides for itself whether to post it, pays for it if it does, and answers to nobody if it doesn't. Nobody in that story needs to be a villain. The temptation to put it off is built in, and it outlasts good intentions.

The only honest answer is to design against it, and that is what a bond is for. It is a way for a generation to tie its own hands before the temptation arrives, as a constitution does. And it has been done. Norway set up its fund in 1990 and made its first deposit in 1996. In 2001 it adopted a rule limiting how much of the fund any government may spend, and in 2017 it tightened that rule. It taxes the carbon from its own oil platforms. Through every change of government since, a living generation of Norwegians has bound itself on behalf of people who are not yet born. Gardiner explains why the future usually loses. Norway is the evidence that it doesn't have to.

So think of who has been in this book. A miner in McDowell County, West Virginia. A waterman on the Chesapeake Bay. A villager beside the waste ponds at Baotou. A farmer pumping from the Ogallala. An administrator on another world, deciding how much of the ice to leave. They will never meet. Each of them is standing where a cost lands or is about to land, and it is the same cost each time, moved by the same means. And somewhere a person with a vote on the seabed has not yet cast it. What remains to ask is no longer a question about policy. It is what those five, and that person, and you, and I, have to do with one another, and that is the last chapter.
