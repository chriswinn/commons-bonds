## 3. One strongest-critic read of draft B (run once; questions for the author)

Read as a hostile but fair economist who has not read the appendix. Where that reader would stop:

1. **"Most of it is being paid by the public" rests on how the black lung fund is classified.** The fund is financed by an excise tax on coal, which the industry pays, and only its shortfall is borrowed from the Treasury. The drafts count the whole $44 billion as "not paid by the industry directly" and get $8 to $13 a ton for the industry. A critic will say the tax is an industry payment and the sentence is wrong as written. Is the claim "paid by the public," or "paid after the fact, by later coal sales and the Treasury, and not by the companies that mined this coal"? The second is safer and the drafts lean toward it, but the chapter's strongest sentence, used twice, says "the public."
2. **Is the headline anything more than the EPA's carbon number?** The chapter says so itself (92 percent; "my own term adds 7 percent"), but only in section 2, and the full admission that the gap disappears without carbon arrives in section 9. Would you rather the reader hear "this number stands or falls with the carbon price" in the same breath as the headline?
3. **Why is all the carbon charged to the mine, and at today's price?** The same carbon is charged to the barrel in Norway. A critic will ask whether the power plant, the steel mill and the consumer are then charged nothing, and whether a 2023 damage figure belongs on a ton sold in 1960. The drafts state the choice in two sentences. Is that enough, or does it need a reason?
4. **The "paid" figure divides a national total by local tons.** The drafts admit it and say the error runs against the argument. A critic will say a figure known to be wrong by "a wide margin" should not be in a table. Could a national figure (national payments over national tonnage) replace it, or sit beside it?
5. **The second check cannot fail.** The drafts say the premium is at least one by construction and "is not evidence." Then why is it one of the two checks, and one of the three ways in the title? The honest answer in the drafts is that it measures size, not direction. Is that enough to keep it in the title's count?
6. **How many of the table's cells are your own estimates?** Community costs ($5–15), other public costs ($5–10 billion), the Deepwater revenue, the Deepwater continuing damage (no primary source), the Libby revenue (unconfirmed), and the land and resettlement parts of replacement cost. Each is labeled. A critic will add them up. Do you want any of them dropped from the table before a specialist reads it?
7. **The opening's largest loss is one the record does not pin on the spill.** The chapter is candid that the herring collapse was never clearly tied to the oil. A critic may say the chapter opens on a loss it cannot attribute. The drafts turn that into the point (an Open line). Does that work for you as the first picture of the chapter, given what Ch 5 promised?
