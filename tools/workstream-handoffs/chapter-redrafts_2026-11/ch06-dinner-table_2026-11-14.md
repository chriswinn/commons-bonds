# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 6: Three Ways of Counting

## The Sound

On the evening of March 23, 1989, an oil tanker called the Exxon Valdez left the pipeline terminal at Valdez, Alaska. It sailed at 9:12, and it was carrying about 53 million gallons of crude oil. There was ice floating in the shipping lanes that night, so the captain ordered the ship to leave the lanes and go around the ice. Then he gave control of the wheelhouse to the third mate. He told the mate to turn back into the lanes when the ship reached a certain point, and he went to his quarters. The ship never made that turn. At 12:04 the next morning it ran aground on Bligh Reef, in a body of water called Prince William Sound.

About 11 million gallons of oil spilled into the sound. Over the following weeks, storms, wind and currents spread the oil along some 1,300 miles of shoreline, and it reached a village 460 miles from the reef. Shortly after the spill, the Pacific herring that live in the sound spawned along its shores. By then the oil had reached a large part of the places where the herring spawn and gather.

The herring were important to almost everything else that lived in the sound. Marine mammals ate them, and so did birds and other fish. People fished them too, and they fished them for several purposes: for food, for bait, for their roe and for the spawn that herring leave on kelp. In the late 1980s the herring catches had been the largest on record. After the spill, people who had been harmed by it sued, and the lawsuits were combined into a single case that more than 32,000 people joined. The court divided the people who were asking for compensation into three groups, which were commercial fishermen, Native Alaskans and landowners. When the case reached the Supreme Court, the Court described them as people who depended on Prince William Sound for their livelihoods.

So what did the spill cost? The public record gives at least three different answers to that question, and the three answers are not the same number.

The first answer is what it cost to clean up the oil. When the cleanup was at its largest, 10,000 people were working on the beaches, with about 1,000 boats and roughly 100 aircraft. The work went on for more than four summers before it was stopped. Exxon says it spent about $2.1 billion on the cleanup. Even so, some beaches were never cleaned. Years later, researchers who were paid from the settlement money found that oil was still lying under the surface of some shorelines, and that some of that oil was as toxic as it had been in 1989.

The second answer is what Exxon was required to pay for the damage. In 1991 the company reached a settlement with the United States government and the State of Alaska. It was fined $150 million. The court then forgave $125 million of that fine, because Exxon had cooperated in the cleanup and had paid some private claims. Exxon also paid $100 million in restitution for the injuries to fish, wildlife and land, and it agreed to pay $900 million over ten years toward restoring them. Separately, it paid another $303 million directly to fishermen, property owners and other private parties. After that there was a trial. The jury awarded the commercial fishermen $287 million to compensate them for what they had lost. The same jury also awarded $5 billion in punitive damages against Exxon, which is money meant to punish a defendant and not to compensate anyone. An appeals court cut the $5 billion in half. Then, in 2008, nineteen years after the spill, the Supreme Court ruled that the punitive award could be no larger than the compensation. A lower court had calculated the compensation at $507.5 million, so that became the limit.

The third answer is what was actually lost, and here the record has the least to say. The State of Alaska paid for a study of one kind of loss. It is the loss to people who never use the sound themselves but who value the fact that it is there, and economists call that passive use value. The study estimated that loss at $2.8 billion. Nobody knows how many animals died in the spill. The best estimates are 250,000 seabirds, 2,800 sea otters, 300 harbor seals, 250 bald eagles and as many as 22 killer whales.

The herring are a separate matter. In 1993 the adult herring population of the sound crashed. That was four years after the spill, and it was two years after the settlement was approved. The catch that year was about 14 percent of what it had been the year before. The herring fishery opened again briefly in 1997 and 1998, and by the trustees' account it has been closed since. So for most of the years since the spill, there has been no herring season in Prince William Sound. A council of state and federal trustees manages the settlement money, and that council lists the herring as a resource that is not recovering. The same council also says plainly that the spill's effect on the herring population as a whole was never clearly established. It names disease, predation and the poor survival of young fish among the things that have kept the population down. The settlement had allowed for surprises of this kind. It left a window, from 2002 to 2006, during which the governments could claim up to $100 million more for injuries that nobody could have foreseen in 1991. In 2006 the governments began a claim for $92 million, and the money was meant to clean up the oil that was still there. In 2015 they told the court that they would not pursue the claim.

So there is a number for the cleanup. There is a number for the settlement, another for the punishment, and another that comes from a study. But the herring fishery fed the animals of the sound and supported the people who lived around it, and it has not come back. For that fishery there is no number at all.

Which of these numbers is the cost of the spill? No single one of them is. The cleanup figure tells us what it took to try to put things back the way they were, and not everything was put back. The settlement and the court awards tell us what was paid, and what was paid is a different thing from what was owed. The study and the counts of dead animals are attempts to measure what was lost. The largest loss has no figure, because nobody can yet say how much of it the spill caused. An honest account of that night needs all three kinds of number, and it needs to know which kind each one is.

## What is owed

Chapter 5 ended with a claim. The claim was that the bonds posted against extraction cover only a small fraction of the cost that extraction leaves behind. A claim like that has to be backed by a number, and the number has to come from a method that a skeptical reader can check. So I will start with the case this book knows best, which is a ton of coal leaving a mine in McDowell County, West Virginia, in 1960. That ton sold at the mine mouth for $4.71. That is the average price the U.S. Energy Information Administration records for bituminous coal in that year. What did that ton cost the people who lived after it was sold?

It cost them two different kinds of thing.

The first kind is something that later people can no longer have. Once the ton has been mined and burned, it is gone. Nobody who comes afterward can use it, and nobody can decide to leave it in the ground. How much that loss matters depends on whether something else can do the job the coal did. Suppose a substitute exists that does everything coal did, at a price people can afford. Then later generations have lost very little. Now suppose no substitute exists. Then they have lost everything the coal could have given them. For that reason this part of the cost is adjusted, year by year, according to how likely it is that no adequate substitute exists yet. For coal that is burned to make electricity, substitutes are arriving quickly. For some other resources they may never arrive. Two examples are helium used in the coldest laboratory work, and water from an aquifer that took ten thousand years to fill.

The second kind is damage that keeps happening after the sale is over. Black lung kills miners decades after they worked their last shift. Acid drains out of a closed mine for generations. The carbon from the burned coal stays in the air for centuries. A substitute for coal does nothing to stop any of this. A solar panel does not clean a creek. This book calls that continuing stream of damage the externality tail. The word tail is borrowed from statistics, where it means the long part of a curve that never quite reaches zero.

To find what the extraction owes, add those two kinds of cost together across all the years in which they occur. Years in the distant future are counted for less than years in the near future. The total is what the extraction owes, and this book's name for that total is the residual commons value. It is the value the ton had for everyone other than the people who bought it and sold it. The whole idea can be written as a formula that fits on one line:

$$\mathrm{RCV} = \int_{t_0}^{\infty} \bigl\{[1 - S(t)] \cdot U(t) + E(t)\bigr\} \cdot D(t)\, dt$$

Each letter stands for one of the things I just described. *S* is the chance that a substitute exists in year *t*. *U* is what a unit of the resource is worth to the people who are alive in that year. *E* is the damage that is still arriving in that year. *D* is the weight we give to a year that far in the future. The formula says nothing that the paragraphs above it did not already say. Its job is to hold the argument still while the numbers are put in. Readers who want the derivation will find it in the Technical Appendix.

Two more terms follow from this one, and the rest of the chapter uses both of them. The first is the accountability bond, which is whatever has actually been set against the cost. The second is the severed cost that Chapter 5 named, which is what is owed minus the bond. When this chapter talks about the gap, it means the result of that subtraction.

Here are the numbers for the McDowell ton. When a ton of coal from the Pocahontas seams is burned, it releases about 2.61 metric tons of carbon dioxide. That figure comes from multiplying two things: the Environmental Protection Agency's emission factor, and the heat content of those seams as reported by the U.S. Geological Survey. An average American bituminous coal holds less heat, and it releases about 2.32 tons. In 2023 the EPA estimated that one ton of carbon dioxide does $190 of damage. The EPA based that estimate on a 2022 study in the journal *Nature* by Kevin Rennert and colleagues, and their own central figure was about $185. At $190 a ton, the carbon from one ton of McDowell coal does about $496 of damage. If I used the national average coal, the figure would be $441.

The damage that does not come from carbon is much smaller. Health costs are $2 to $4 for each ton of coal. Environmental cleanup is $1 to $3. Community costs are $5 to $15, and I should say that this last range is my own estimate and not a published one. Added together, these come to $8 to $22 a ton. As for the first kind of cost, which is the ton that is no longer there for anyone later, I have set it at the lowest value the definition allows.

When these figures are put through the formula, the McDowell ton comes out between $525 and $540. The answer is a range because it depends on how much weight we give to distant years. I ran the calculation three times. Each time I used a discount rate that starts at a different level and declines toward 2 percent, and the three starting levels were 1.4 percent, 4 percent and 5.5 percent. The three answers were $526, $537 and $541. The first rate is the one Nicholas Stern used in his review of climate economics, and the last is the one associated with William Nordhaus. The answer hardly changes from one rate to the next. That is because about 92 percent of it is the carbon term, and the lost access to the coal itself accounts for only about 7 percent.

That gives this chapter its headline. A ton of coal that sold for $4.71 in 1960 carries a cost of about $525 in today's dollars. Those two prices come from two different eras. I put them next to each other, but I do not divide one by the other, because most of what that ratio would measure is sixty years of inflation. A fairer comparison is with the price of the same kind of coal today, which is $40 to $140 a ton depending on its grade. Compared with that price, the cost is about 4 to 13 times what the buyer pays.

I should be clear about how little of that number is my own work. The total for the damage comes from other people. The carbon figure is the EPA's. In 2011 Paul Epstein and colleagues estimated what coal costs the American public over its whole life cycle, and they put it at between a third and more than half of a trillion dollars a year. In the same year Nicholas Muller, Robert Mendelsohn and William Nordhaus looked only at air pollution from coal-fired power. They found that it does damage worth 0.8 to 5.6 times the value the industry adds to the economy. So the finding that coal's unpriced damage is as large as its price, or larger, belongs to them. The term I contributed adds 7 percent. I also made a choice that a reader should know about. I charged all of the carbon to the ton at the mine. I charged none of it to the power plant that burned the coal or to the household that used the electricity. If the carbon were divided differently among them, the figure per ton would be lower, but the total would not change by a dollar.

Chapter 8 follows one ton through every part of the damage. There are eight parts, and the total comes to $510. The question in this chapter is different. Is $525 to $540 a real number? Or is it a number I could have made come out any way I wanted? There are two ways to check it, and neither of them uses the EPA's damage figure.

## First check: what would it cost to replace it?

The first check comes from engineering. Suppose we decided to undo the damage. What would that work cost?

For carbon, that work already has a name and a list of prices. It is called direct air capture, and it means machinery that pulls carbon dioxide back out of the air. A few plants are running today, such as the Climeworks installations in Iceland, and they do the job for roughly $600 to $1,000 for each ton of carbon dioxide. Plants built at commercial scale within the next decade are expected to do it for $300 to $600. The International Energy Agency and the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change both describe a path that could bring the price down to $100 to $300 by the middle of the century.

Now apply those prices to the 2.61 tons of carbon dioxide that one McDowell ton released. I also add my own rougher estimates for restoring the land that was stripped and for resettling the people who had to leave, and those two estimates together are less than 4 percent of the total. The result depends on which set of prices is used. At the prices hoped for in 2050, replacement costs between $290 and $875 for each ton of coal. At the prices expected within a decade, it costs between $812 and $1,658. At today's prices it costs between $1,595 and $2,702. The middle of the middle case is about $1,235.

Every one of those figures is larger than the headline, and that is what we should expect. Taking a ton of carbon dioxide out of the air costs $300 to $600. The EPA says the same ton does $190 of damage if it stays in the air. Repairing a harm usually costs more than the harm itself. This means the check cannot confirm the headline to the dollar, and I do not treat it as if it did. What this check can do is contradict the headline. Suppose undoing the damage had turned out to cost only $50 for each ton of coal. Then a claim that the coal owed $525 would have been in trouble. In fact it costs at least $290 to undo, and that figure assumes the most hopeful prices for machines that have not been built yet.

Replacement cost has a limit, and the next check exists because of that limit. An engineer can tell you the price of a machine that removes carbon. No engineer can tell you the price of bringing back the coal seam, or the mountaintop that was above it, or a county of about a hundred thousand people that now holds fewer than twenty thousand. A replacement figure covers the things that can be rebuilt, and it leaves out the things that cannot. That makes it a floor under what it would cost to set things right. What about the part that nothing can rebuild?

## Second check: what can't be bought back?

The second check begins with the market price of the resource. Then it asks how much more the resource is worth when taking it cannot be undone.

This idea did not start with me. In 1974 Kenneth Arrow and Anthony Fisher explained why a loss that cannot be reversed should count for more than a loss that can be, and Claude Henry showed the same thing separately that year. Their reason was that once the thing is gone, every later chance to decide differently is gone too. The formula I use to turn their idea into a number is my own, and I want to be exact about that. It is a simple construction and it is not their model. What it produces is a check on the headline. It is not a finding.

Here is how it works. Begin with the price the resource sells for. Raise that price a little to account for scarcity. For McDowell coal the scarcity factor runs from about 1.27 to 1.31. Then raise the price a lot to account for irreversibility. The irreversibility factor depends on how likely it is that the loss can never be restored, whatever anyone is willing to spend. For fossil carbon, and for a mountain whose top has been removed, the physical evidence puts that likelihood between 85 and 95 percent. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change says this about carbon in its sixth assessment. Emily Bernhardt and Margaret Palmer documented it for mountaintop mining in 2011. When the likelihood is 85 percent, the factor is a little under 7. When it is 95 percent, the factor is 20.

A ton of McDowell coal sells today for $40 to $140. For that ton, the premium figure runs from about $340 to about $3,670, and the center of the range is near $1,115. There are two plain reasons the range is so wide. One is that different grades of coal sell at very different prices. The other is that moving from an 85 percent chance of permanence to a 95 percent chance makes the answer three times larger. That second reason is not a sign that the method is sloppy. The method is telling us how much depends on a single question, and the question is whether the loss is truly permanent.

There is one thing this check cannot show, and a reader should hold me to it. Both of its factors are at least one. So the result can never be lower than the market price, no matter what the facts are. The premium figure comes out at 8.5 to 26 times the price, but that is not evidence that the coal was underpriced. The check starts by assuming there is a premium. All it measures is how large the premium is.

What do we get from the two checks, then? They are built from different data. The first check uses engineering costs and does not use a market price. The second uses the market price and does not use engineering costs. Neither one uses the EPA's $190. Their centers land close to each other, at around $1,100 to $1,250, and both are above the headline. I am not claiming that this agreement proves anything. Both ranges are wide, and two wide ranges will overlap easily. My claim is narrower than that. Two different ways of approaching the question both say that $525 to $540 does not overstate what the ton owes.

Those are two ways of counting what is owed, and they make up one side of the ledger. The other side does not involve any model. It is a matter of record. What was actually paid, and who paid it?

## Third count: what was paid, and by whom?

The third way of counting is to read the receipts.

Before reading them, we need to know what counts as a payment. There are two ways a cost can be covered. The first is an instrument, which means money that is posted or paid against a named cost or risk. A reclamation bond is an instrument, and so is a tax on each ton, a fund or a court judgment. An instrument counts even when nobody knew how large the cost would turn out to be at the time the money was put up. The second way is the consent of the person who bears the cost. Consent counts only as far as that person could see the cost and was free to refuse it. Think of a worker whose wage included extra pay for a danger he could see. He agreed to that danger. He did not agree to a harm he could not see. And a person who is forced to bear a cost has not agreed to anything. A cost is severed when someone bears it and it was not covered in either of these ways.

Now for the receipts on McDowell coal. The largest one is the federal Black Lung Disability Trust Fund. According to the Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Research Service, the fund had paid out about $44 billion through 2009, which is the last year for which a running total has been published. The trust fund gets its money from an excise tax on coal that is sold today. When the tax does not bring in enough, the fund borrows from the general fund of the United States Treasury. As of September 2024 the fund owed $5.1 billion. The second receipt is the reclamation bonds that the coal companies have posted. After their known shortfalls are subtracted, those bonds are worth about $3 billion to $5 billion. The third is the cost of bankruptcies. Between 2014 and 2016, bankruptcies moved about $0.9 billion of mining liabilities onto taxpayers. Finally, I estimate that public programs have carried another $5 billion to $10 billion in miners' compensation and medical costs. That last figure is my own estimate and has not been published anywhere.

All of that together comes to $53 billion to $60 billion. About 600 million tons of coal were mined in McDowell and the counties next to it. If we divide the money by the tons, everything that anyone paid comes to $88 to $100 a ton. I know that this figure is too high. The black lung total is for the whole country, and I have divided it by the tonnage of only a few counties. I keep the figure anyway, because the error works against my own argument. If I overstate what was paid, I understate what was left unpaid.

Not all of that $88 to $100 came from the industry. The part the industry paid directly, through reclamation bonds and private settlements, is $8 to $13 a ton. An even smaller part was posted ahead of time against damage that had not happened yet. That part is the reclamation bonds alone, and it comes to $5 to $8 a ton. Nearly all of the rest was paid after the harm was done, and it was paid through a public fund.

Now we can subtract. The ton owed $525 to $540. At most, $88 to $100 was paid against it by anyone. So at least $425 to $450 a ton was left unpaid. If we measure against what the industry itself paid, the gap is about $512 to $532.

The other cases in this book come out the same way. There is one exception, and it is worth a look.

The exception is Deepwater Horizon. In 2016 BP made its own final estimate of what the spill had cost the company, and the figure was about $61.6 billion. BP paid that amount. It paid because the law was able to reach it: BP was a solvent company that everyone could identify, and the litigation forced it to pay. The damage to the Gulf did not stop there. The damage that continued beyond what BP paid is estimated at another $8 billion to $12 billion. So in this case, unusually, the party that caused the cost paid most of the bill that could be documented.

At Libby, the federal cleanup has cost more than $600 million. In 2008 W.R. Grace agreed to pay $250 million of that back to the government. That payment covered part of the cleanup. None of it went to the people who were sick or to the families of the people who had died. The public paid for the rest of the cleanup, which was $350 million and more. Grace's bankruptcy trust has paid some money to injured people in Libby. But I have not found any source that itemizes those payments, so I leave that line without a figure.

Norway is the case in which a country paid deliberately and ahead of time. The bank that manages Norway's petroleum fund reports that the fund held 21,268 billion kroner at the end of 2025, which is about $2.0 trillion. Norway has produced roughly 55 billion barrels of oil and gas, with the gas counted at its equivalent in oil. Divide the fund by the barrels and the result is about $36 a barrel.

Then there is Prince William Sound. There the company paid for the cleanup and paid the settlement, and the punitive award against it was cut to about a tenth of what the jury had set. Nobody has paid anything for the herring.

When the receipts are placed side by side, one thing stands out. In some places nothing was paid at all. In one place the company that did the damage paid for most of it, and it took years of litigation to make that happen. Everywhere else, where a bill is being paid at all, most of it is being paid by the public.

Now read down the last column. In every row, what was owed is more than what was paid. The direction never flips, and that is the strongest thing the table has to say. The replacement check also counts the same tons of carbon that the headline counts. If that carbon price is wrong, then those rows are all wrong together. So what the table shows is a pattern in the record. It is not a proof.

The table also raises the question a fair reader would ask next. Every row shows a gap. Is the method able to return a small gap? Is it ever able to return no gap at all?

## Norway

If the method reported a disaster every time it was used, it would be useless. So I will try it on the best-managed extraction in the world.

Chapter 4 told the story of how Norway came to manage its oil, and I will give only the short version here. Norway passed a law creating a fund in 1990, and it made the first deposit in 1996. The money the state earns from oil and gas goes into the fund. The government is allowed to spend only the return the fund is expected to earn. That limit was set at 4 percent in 2001, and it was lowered to 3 percent in 2017. Norway has also taxed its own carbon since 1991. No country has come closer to following the rule that the economist John Hartwick wrote down in 1977. His rule says that if you use up a resource that cannot be renewed, you should invest what it earned. That way the people who come later inherit the wealth, even though they cannot inherit the oil.

How well has Norway followed that rule? It has followed it very well. The Ministry of Finance and Statistics Norway report the state's net income from petroleum for the years 1971 through 2025, and it comes to about $25 a barrel. Part of the profit from oil comes from the resource itself and not from the work of producing it, and economists call that part the rent. The Norwegian state takes 70 to 80 percent of the rent, which means the whole rent is roughly $32 to $36 a barrel. The fund holds about $36 a barrel. So Norway has saved essentially all of the rent its oil produced. Because the money was invested and grew, it has saved a little more than that.

Why, then, does the table show a gap for Norway? The reason is that the rent was never the only cost. When a barrel of oil is burned, it releases about 0.37 tons of carbon dioxide. At the EPA's $190 a ton, that is about $70 of damage. I have not run the full calculation for Norway, so the $70 covers the carbon and nothing else, and the true figure is higher. Take away the $36 that is in the fund, and at least $34 a barrel is left. Across everything Norway has produced, that comes to about $1.9 trillion.

Even that subtraction gives Norway more credit than it has earned. The fund was created to save oil wealth for later generations of Norwegians. It was never intended to pay for carbon. None of the money goes to the people the carbon harms, and most of those people are not Norwegian. A payment covers the cost it was set against, and it does not cover any other cost. If we take the costs one at a time, Norway has covered the rent in full, and it has not covered the carbon at all.

The fund does not put the barrel back in the ground, and it does not take the carbon out of the air. What it does is the thing Hartwick asked for. Norwegians chose this arrangement through their own institutions, and they can see what they are choosing. For them, the cost of using up their own oil is covered in two ways, by their consent and by the fund. For everyone else, the cost arrives with nothing set against it.

So the method was able to tell two cases apart. It found that McDowell left nearly everything unpaid. It found that Norway paid one cost in full and paid nothing toward another. Norway is closer to honest pricing than any other place, and it is still short by a sum nearly as large as the fund itself, measured by the carbon alone.

That is a smaller gap than McDowell's. Can there be a case with no gap?

## When the answer is zero

There can be, and it is important that there can be. If a method were never able to answer "nothing is owed here," it would not really be measuring anything.

Consider a forest that is cut no faster than it grows. Its owner replants, and the owner has also posted a bond against the damage that logging does to the streams. Later generations lose nothing in this case, because the forest is still there for them. Whatever damage the logging does has money standing behind it. The gap is zero.

Or consider sand that is dug from a pit. The pit operates under a permit, and the permit requires a bond large enough to restore the land. Ordinary sand is so plentiful that nobody at any time in the future will miss this particular load. The bond pays for filling the hole. Here, too, the gap is zero.

Or consider a person who chooses a long commute. This person knows exactly what the commute will cost in hours, and has decided that the lower rent is worth it. The person saw the cost and was free to refuse it. That counts as consent, so nothing is owed.

These cases show where the method stops applying. A cost is severed only when two things are true. Something is being used up faster than it can restore itself, and the cost falls on someone who did not agree to it and was not paid for it. A fishery that is harvested within what it can replace owes nothing for the fish. The method does not say that all extraction causes harm. It also does not say that the right thing is always to leave things alone.

The same cases show that every line in the ledger can be in one of three states. Chapter 8 will use all three. A line can be Zero. That means someone looked for the cost and it is not there, as with the sand. A line can be Filled. That means there is a figure, and the work behind it is shown. Or a line can be Open. That means the cost is real and belongs in the account, but no figure has been entered, because nobody can honestly supply one yet. The herring of Prince William Sound are an Open line. The payments to the sick at Libby are another. So is the price of coerced labor, which Chapter 5 left to the scholars whose work it is. An Open line is not the same as a zero. It tells us that the total above it is a floor.

There are two fair objections to all of this, and this is the place to answer them.

The first objection is that I picked my parameters. If I had used a different carbon price, or a different guess about substitutes, or a different weight on the future, I could have produced any number I liked. Part of that is true. The next section shows exactly how far the number moves and what makes it move. But the objection would be stronger if the result could never fail, and it can fail. Suppose the record somewhere showed a bond that was as large as the cost it stood against. That case would count against the pattern. The forest and the sandpit describe what such a case would look like. Among the fossil fuel cases, I have not found one. The arithmetic does not guarantee that outcome. The reason is that no law anywhere yet requires anyone to post money against what a later generation will lose.

The second objection is that two analysts who use this method on the same mine will arrive at two different numbers. That is correct. It is also true of every method for estimating the cost of something that has not finished happening. It is true when the task is to take apart a nuclear plant, or to fund a pension, or to judge a regulation. People who work in those fields do not regard a range as a failure. What they regard as a failure is a range whose causes are hidden. So this framework follows a rule. Every figure has to report four things: the value that was used and how it was reached, the checks that could be run, what was paid and by whom, and the price the figure is being compared with. When two analysts disagree, they can then see which input they disagree about, and they can go and look it up.

Which of the inputs matters most?

## What moves the number

The answer is carbon. Carbon makes up 92 percent of the McDowell figure, and the price the government puts on it has changed with each American administration. Under President Obama, the federal government valued a ton of carbon dioxide at about $42. The first Trump administration used $1 to $7. The Biden administration started at about $51, and then in 2023 the EPA published $190. The atmosphere was the same under all four numbers.

Here is what happens to the McDowell figure when the carbon price changes. Suppose carbon dioxide is priced at $51 a ton and not $190. Then the ton of coal owes about $165 to $180. That is still more than was paid, but it is only 1.2 to 4.5 times the price of the coal today. Now suppose carbon has no price at all. Then the ton owes about $30 to $45, and that is less than the $88 to $100 that I counted as paid. In other words, the whole gap for McDowell depends on carbon costing at least something like $20 to $25 a ton. If a reader believes that carbon does no damage, that reader should reject most of my number. I would rather say this here than have someone discover it later.

Two points should be added. The first is about the $88 to $100. As I explained, that figure is overstated, because it divides national payments by local tons. So when the carbon price is set to zero, the comparison is tilted against finding a gap. The second point is that zero is not a neutral choice. Leaving carbon out of the count amounts to claiming that carbon does exactly no harm, and no body of evidence supports that. The low figures and the high figures are all positions that someone has taken. I use $190 because it is the most recent official estimate from the agency whose job is to produce one. And I show what happens to the result if you use something else.

The second thing that changes the number is whether substitutes arrive. In my calculation I assumed that 85 percent of what coal does can eventually be done by something else. If nothing could ever replace coal, the figure would go up from about $525 to about $573. For coal this effect is small, because most of coal's cost is damage. For a resource whose cost is mostly lost access, the question of substitutes would decide nearly everything.

Economists have argued about this for a long time, and the framework takes a side. In 1974 Robert Solow argued that when a resource runs down, other forms of capital can take its place almost without limit. If that is right, a society that reinvests what the resource earned leaves its descendants no poorer. Herman Daly spent his career arguing the opposite. He held that some of what nature provides has no substitute at any price, and that treating it as replaceable is the central mistake in the standard accounts. The formula in this chapter agrees with Daly about the principle and with Solow about the method. It accepts that substitution can be nearly complete for some things. It also accepts that substitution can stop well short for others, such as helium for the coldest laboratory work, water from a fossil aquifer or a stable climate. And it treats the matter as a question of fact that has to be answered one resource at a time.

This is also the reason I would rather argue about substitutes than about discount rates. A discount rate expresses how much weight the living owe to the unborn. People have disputed that since Frank Ramsey raised the question in 1928, and nobody has settled it. Stern and Nordhaus chose rates that are far apart. In 2001 Martin Weitzman showed what to do when the right rate is uncertain: the rate that should be used declines over time toward the lowest of the candidates. That declining schedule is the one I use. For McDowell the choice hardly matters, as the three results earlier in the chapter showed. The question of how fast a substitute can be built is a different kind of question. Engineers and hydrologists can work on it, and their answers improve over time.

Everything in this section has been about how large the costs are once they are in the count. A different question comes before that one. What is allowed into the count to begin with?

## The gates

A method like this one needs a rule about what gets in. Without one, it would only be a way of adding up grievances. Anyone could propose any cost, and the total would tell us more about the analyst than about the mine. So a cost is admitted to the count only if it passes four gates. Two of the gates decide whether the cost belongs. The other two check that it is stated in a form that can be added up.

The first gate is a test. This book calls it the Commons Inversion Test, and it has three steps.

The first step is to say at what level the claim is being made. That means saying who it applies to, over how long a time, and which substitutes are being counted. The same activity can pass the test at one level and fail it at another. For example, one person's attention during one evening restores itself by the next morning. Whether the attention of a whole generation restores itself is a different claim, and it has to be argued separately.

The second step is to check that the cost is attached to something shared, and not to something that is privately owned. To check this, we ask what would be different if the shared thing did not exist at all.

The third step is to turn the scarcity around. Imagine that the shared thing were unlimited, or that it restored itself at no cost. Would the cost disappear? If it would, then the cost comes from using up something finite, and it belongs in the count. If the cost would still be there even in a world of plenty, then it is a cost of something else.

Let me try the test on the coalfield. The claim is that black lung is a cost of McDowell coal. The people who bear it are the miners, their families and the public programs that treat them, and they bear it across their lifetimes. The shared thing is breathable air underground and the health of the people who work there. Now suppose the lungs of a mining workforce could take in any amount of dust without being harmed. In that case the disease would vanish, and so would its cost. The cost passes the test.

Now let me try it on something small. In Chapter 1 I signed a lease on a home that was two and a half hours from my work in each direction. The claim is that those hours were a cost, to one person, for as long as the lease lasted. The shared thing in this case is the time of one life, and it is shared only between me and the person I would be a year later. If a person had unlimited hours, would spending five of them a day cost anything? It would not. So the cost passes the test. It passes even though only one person is involved and nobody did anything wrong.

What kinds of claim do not pass? Here are three. A claim that mining cost a town its sense of meaning fails. The test needs us to describe a world in which the shared thing is unlimited, and nobody can describe a world with unlimited meaning. A claim also fails if the only way to make the cost go away is to imagine that someone else pays it. Every cost goes away for the person bearing it when another person pays. That tells us something about the bond, and it tells us nothing about the cost. The third kind of claim passes the second step and fails the third. Suppose twenty boats share a fishery, and the crews quarrel over who may fish where. That quarrel would continue even if the fish were endless. It is a problem of governing a shared thing and not a problem of using it up, and the work of Elinor Ostrom is where to take it. I return to her at the end of the chapter.

The second gate requires the cost to be stated in dollars per unit, per year. If a cost is given only as a ranking or a score, it waits outside until someone converts it to dollars and shows how the conversion was done. The third gate requires the cost to add up to a finite sum. A claim of infinite damage would swallow every other line in the ledger. Both of these gates are checks of form, and most costs that have actually been measured pass them without trouble.

The fourth gate is the other gate that decides whether a cost belongs. It says that a cost may not be counted twice. Suppose the collapse of a town is entered once as lost community and then again as lost habitability. One of those entries has to be removed, or else the two have to be redefined so that they do not overlap.

There are two things the gates deliberately do not do. They do not prove that a particular mine caused a particular harm. That has to be shown for each case, with the evidence and where it came from, as I tried to do for the herring. The gates also do not decide who bears a cost or what that person knew. That question belongs to the bond side of the ledger. It is the reason the commute comes out differently depending on what the person signing the lease could see. The cost of my hours passed the gates. Whether that cost was severed depends on whether I knew what I was agreeing to, and I did not know.

When I ran the test across the cases in this book, it turned up ten kinds of shared thing that extraction draws on. They are the habitability of a place, space, time, institutions, the knowledge and culture passed down among kin, ecosystems, the political process, the trust between strangers that this book calls cohesion, what a society is able to know, and the autonomy of persons. This list is not complete, and it was never meant to be complete. The ten are examples. They are the kinds that happened to come up in the cases this book examines. A reader who runs the same test on other cases will find other kinds, and may well divide these ten differently. Political traditions also disagree about which of these things are truly shared. A classical liberal holds that autonomy belongs to the individual. A civic republican holds that autonomy depends on institutions that people hold in common. The test does not settle that argument, and it does not need to. It takes whatever a tradition holds in common, and it asks what was drawn from that and who paid. The appendix lists the ten along with the costs that each one revealed, and it works seven cases through all four gates.

In this chapter, every cost that passes those gates falls largely on people who have not been born yet. Why should anything be owed to them?

## People not yet born

Most of what the McDowell ton owes is owed to people who were not alive in 1960. A good part of it is owed to people who are not alive now. Any account that sends a bill to the future has to answer a hard question about those people. The hardest version of the question comes from the philosopher Derek Parfit.

Parfit published *Reasons and Persons* in 1984. In it he pointed out that large choices change which people get born. Compare a world that burns its coal quickly with a world that burns it slowly. The two worlds will not contain the same people, because different couples will meet and different children will be conceived. Now think about the people who inherit the hot, depleted world. They cannot say they would have been better off under the other policy, because under the other policy they would not exist. So whom did the policy wrong? Parfit called this the non-identity problem. It is the deepest objection there is to any claim that the present can owe something to the future.

Parfit's response was to stop asking about particular people and to ask about outcomes. Whoever ends up living in the future, we can ask a question about their world. Is it worse than the world some other set of people would have had? If it is, then something of value has been lost. That is true even though no single person can point to himself and say he was the one who was robbed. This book stands on that footing. The residual commons value does not put a price on what was taken from one particular miner's grandchild. It puts a price on what will be missing from the world that the next people, whoever they are, will have to live in.

Parfit did not claim that he had solved his problem. Nobody since has solved it, and I have not solved it either. What his work gives this book is a serious way of holding that the future has a claim, and one that has been examined for a long time. Because of it, the claim does not depend on my asking you to feel it.

Parfit's answer tells us that future people count. It does not tell us how to measure what they lose. For that, the book draws on the economist and philosopher Amartya Sen. Sen argued that a life should be judged by what a person is actually able to do and to be. He called these capabilities. Living to old age is one, being healthy is another, and taking part in the life of a community is another. In Sen's terms, what extraction takes from the future is a narrowing of what people will be able to do. According to the estimates for McDowell County, a man there lives thirteen years less than the national average. A county like that has lost a capability, and the loss can be counted in years. The residual commons value is an attempt to express that narrowing as a figure.

A third philosopher supplies the piece that the calculation depends on most. John Broome made his case in *Counting the Cost of Global Warming* in 1992, in his 1994 paper "Discounting the Future" and in *Climate Matters* in 2012. He argued three things. First, the rate at which a society discounts future harm is a moral choice. It cannot be read off a market interest rate as if it were a fact about the world. Second, there is no ethical defense for counting a person's wellbeing for less merely because that person lives later. Third, a generation that knowingly imposes harm on the generations after it owes them restitution. This book holds the first of those positions, and it builds the third into its design. On the second, I show the results under several discount rates, so that the reader can see what difference the choice makes. Each of the three philosophers does a different job. Parfit gives future people standing. Sen says what it is that they stand to lose. Broome says how that loss should be weighed across time.

For harm that has already been done, the book stands on different ground. The people who were wronged in the past are particular people, with names and descendants. That ground belongs to the economics of reparations, and Chapter 5 described it. William Darity Jr. and A. Kirsten Mullen built the method for pricing a harm that has already happened and has been handed down. The method is theirs, and the framework adopts it. This book sometimes prices the legacy of a past harm, such as the years of life lost in McDowell County. Wherever it does, it is borrowing their way of working and applying it to a different injury.

None of this will persuade a person who holds that the living owe nothing to the unborn. I do not know of any argument that can force that conclusion on someone who refuses it. But the refusal has a price, and the price ought to be visible. Think of a child born in McDowell County this year, in a county that has lost four-fifths of its people. If nothing is owed to the unborn, then that child has no claim on anyone for coal that was sold for $4.71 a ton more than sixty years before she was born.

## Borrowed and added

Has all of this been done before? A good deal of it has. A reader is owed an exact account of which parts.

The idea that a price can leave out a cost that falls on third parties belongs to Arthur Pigou, who published it in 1920. So does the remedy, which is to charge the cost back through a price. The economics of a resource that runs out belongs to Harold Hotelling, from 1931. Hotelling showed how the owner of a mine should value what is still in the ground, and in 1989 Salah El Serafy showed how an owner should keep the sale of that asset separate from true income. Both of them describe depletion from the owner's side. This framework prices the other side, which is the loss to people who are not the owner. The rule that a society should reinvest what a depleted resource earns is Hartwick's. Partha Dasgupta carried that kind of accounting up to the scale of whole nations. His fullest statement of it is *The Economics of Biodiversity*, the review he wrote for the British Treasury in 2021. Hartwick and Dasgupta work at the level of a country's books. The framework works at the level of a single ton or a single barrel.

The idea of a bond that is posted before the damage happens is not mine either. In 1990 Robert Costanza and Charles Perrings proposed that a company should post a bond "equal to the current best estimate of the largest potential future environmental damages." The company would get the bond back only if the damage did not happen. That proposal is the closest thing to what this book proposes, and the two differ on one point. Their bond is sized to the worst case, taken as a whole. The reserve in this book is sized to the cost that each unit leaves unpaid, and it grows as the units are taken out.

Counting the damage after it has happened is an established practice. Under American law, public trustees assess the injury that a spill does to natural resources. Then they recover the cost of restoring those resources from whoever was responsible. That is what happened at Prince William Sound. It happened again, on a far larger scale, after Deepwater Horizon. This method works when there is a responsible party that can be found, that can pay, and that is within reach of the law. There is also the international study known as The Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity, and the true cost accounting that grew from it. They put prices on what nature provides to whole sectors and food systems. They have the same aim as this book, and they work at a different scale. They do not attach a figure to a single unit of extraction, and they do not compare that figure with what was paid.

Then there is Elinor Ostrom. I want to be careful here, because her work is often set against the kind of thing this book does, and it should not be. In *Governing the Commons*, published in 1990, Ostrom showed that people who share a resource are not doomed to ruin it. Fishing villages, irrigation districts and the users of a groundwater basin have governed what they share for generations, and they have done it under rules they made themselves. Her cases have one feature in common. The people who bear the costs of using the resource are, for the most part, the same people who share its fate and help to make its rules. The cases in this book are the ones in which that feature is missing. The company that mined McDowell did not live under the mountain. The people who will live with the carbon from Norway's oil had no vote in Norway. Some of the people who bear the cost are absent or scattered. Others are present but overmatched. Scholars in Ostrom's own tradition are already working on that second situation. In 2021 Praneeta Mudliar and Tomas Koontz studied watershed groups in India and the United States. They found that her design principles "can conceal and perpetuate power asymmetries" even while they improve environmental outcomes. Ostrom asks how people who share a resource can govern it so that it lasts. This book asks what is owed when the people who bear the cost had no hand in making the rules. One place can need both answers.

The diagnosis is older than this book as well. I mean the observation that powerful parties shift costs onto people who cannot refuse them. Chapter 5 credited it to K. William Kapp and to the scholars of environmental justice who came after him. Karl Polanyi saw the same thing, and so did Nancy Fraser after him. Both of them declined on principle to put a price on it. Their ground was that treating nature and people as things with prices was the source of the trouble in the first place. I do not claim that they would approve of what this chapter does. I count the cost so that it can be charged to the party that caused it. I am not proposing that anything be sold.

What, then, is added? There are three things, and each is narrower than a new theory.

The first is a figure at the level of the single act. It says what one ton or one barrel owes, and it puts the lost access and the continuing damage into the same sum.

The second is the subtraction. The totals for the damage already existed. What was missing was the comparison, unit by unit, between what was owed and what was paid, with the payers named. This chapter's main finding comes from that comparison. Where a bill is being paid at all, most of it is being paid by the public.

The third is a reserve that is posted in advance against the unpaid figure. All of the cases in this chapter look backward. They price extraction that has already happened, and they read the receipts. A reserve that is sized ahead of time has not yet been tried on a real case, and I do not want to suggest that it has.

There is one more tool to hand on. Much of what this chapter has priced cannot be undone. That changes how a decision should be made when the facts are uncertain. In 1951 the statistician Leonard Savage proposed a rule for choosing when the odds cannot be known. Look at how much you would regret each choice if it turned out to be wrong, and then take the choice whose worst regret is smallest. Now apply that rule to extraction. Suppose we leave a resource in the ground, and later we learn that we could safely have used it. We have lost some time, and the resource is still there. Suppose instead that we take it, and later we learn that we should not have. Nothing will bring it back. The two regrets are not the same size. This book calls the result the Asymmetric Regret Rule. It says that when you cannot tell whether a loss can ever be made good, you should treat it as a loss that cannot. The rule does not replace the ordinary weighing of costs and benefits in cases where mistakes can be corrected. It is meant for the cases where they cannot be. Chapter 7 relies on it, and Chapter 9 applies it to particular decisions.

Everything in this chapter was counted on Earth. On Earth air is free, and the oceans dilute what is poured into them. The people who pay usually live a long way from the people who gain. Would the same accounts hold in a place where none of that is true? Think of a mining crew on an airless rock, who have to make every breath they take. Would they need a method like this one, or would the bill simply be in front of them? If the method works only where costs can be hidden, then it describes this planet. If it works there too, then it describes something more general. The next chapter goes there.
