# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 8: What Things Actually Cost

## The rail car

Picture a rail car loaded with coal, leaving McDowell County, West Virginia, in 1960. Someone bought that coal, and someone was paid for it. We know roughly what the price was, because the federal Energy Information Administration keeps the records. In 1960 a ton of bituminous coal sold at the mouth of an American mine for $4.71 on average, which is just under five dollars. Once the buyer had paid and the car had pulled out, the market considered the matter closed.

But you and I have already seen that it wasn't closed. In Chapter 2 we read the rest of the bill for that coal, one column at a time. There was a column for the miner's lungs, one for the creeks, one for the town, one for the children who left, and one for the air. I left the amounts blank in every column, because it was enough for that chapter to show that the columns exist. That leaves a question I have owed you since then: what does the whole bill come to?

In this chapter I write the bill out for one ton of McDowell coal. I go line by line, and for each line I tell you who paid it. I also need to tell you how I chose the numbers. Wherever the sources give a range, I use the bottom of the range, so every figure you see is the lowest one I can defend. Some figures come from outside records. Others are my own estimates, built on those records, and I will tell you each time which kind you are looking at. When we reach the end we will add the lines up and put the total next to the $4.71.

## The lungs

The first line was paid by the miner, and he paid it with his body. You met Robert Bailey in Chapter 2, so you know how this works. Coal dust gets deep into the lungs and scars them, and the scarring spreads until the lungs can no longer do their work. A miner who dies of black lung loses between eight and thirteen years of the life he would otherwise have had.

The miner paid first, and then the public paid. The federal government runs a program that pays benefits to miners with black lung. Through 2009 that program had paid out about $44 billion, according to the Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Research Service. Nobody has published a reliable total for the years after 2009. The benefits come out of a trust fund, and the fund has had to borrow money to keep paying them. As of September 2024 it owed $5.1 billion.

How much is that for each ton of coal? The program began in 1969. If you spread the $44 billion across all the coal mined in the United States since then, the benefits come to between $1 and $1.50 a ton. And black lung is only the part of the health cost that has been documented best. The dust didn't stay in the mines, so people who lived nearby and never worked underground breathed it too, and they have higher rates of heart and lung disease. Some coal companies went bankrupt before they had paid what they owed their sick miners, and those miners' medical bills moved to Medicare and Medicaid. You can see the result in one number: a boy born in McDowell County can expect to live about thirteen years less than the average American boy.

When I add those other costs to the black lung benefits, I estimate the health line at somewhere between $2 and $4 a ton. I want to be clear about where that comes from. The black lung figure is from an outside record. The rest of the range is my own estimate. I use the bottom of it, so the first line of the bill is $2.

## The creeks

The second line is paid by the people who live downstream from a mine. Here is how the damage happens. Some of the rock around a coal seam holds sulfur. When mining breaks that rock open, the sulfur meets air and water and turns into sulfuric acid. The acid then pulls heavy metals out of the broken ground and carries them into the streams. This is called acid mine drainage, and it is the reason the creeks below old mines run orange. It also keeps going after the mine closes. It can run for a century or more, and for as long as it runs, somebody has to treat the water.

The law was supposed to cover this. Since 1977 a federal law called the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act has required coal companies to post bonds, which are sums of money set aside ahead of time to pay for cleaning up their mines. The bonds turned out to be too small. In 2021 a group called Appalachian Voices counted about 633,000 acres of mined land across Appalachia that were still waiting to be cleaned up. It estimated that the cleanup would cost between $7.5 billion and $9.8 billion. The bonds available to pay for it added up to about $3.8 billion. So between $4 billion and $6 billion of the cleanup has no money behind it.

The creeks are not the only damage outside the miner's body. In some places companies took off the whole top of a mountain to reach the coal, and they pushed the leftover rock into the valleys, where it buried the streams. In 2012 a research team led by Michael Hendryx surveyed people who lived near those mines. They were about twice as likely to say they had had cancer as people in similar communities with no such mining nearby. There is also the mercury. Coal contains mercury, and when the coal was burned the mercury went into the air and later settled in the oceans. It has been measured in fish as far away as the North Atlantic.

I estimate all of that at between $1 and $3 a ton. As with the health line, the records above are from outside sources and the range is mine. I take the bottom, which is $1, and that brings the bill to $3.

## The town

The third line was paid by the people who stayed in the county. Part of it was also paid by public programs that spend money on their behalf. In 1950 nearly a hundred thousand people lived in McDowell County. By 2020 just over nineteen thousand did, so about four out of every five people were gone. They left because the work left. The mines had brought in machines, and a machine cuts coal with a fraction of the men. Then U.S. Steel, the company that had anchored the county, wound its mines down across the 1980s, and about twelve hundred jobs went with them.

The people who are still there live in one of the poorest places in the United States. In 2022 the median household in the county earned $28,235 a year, while the median household in the whole country earned about $67,500. The county's poverty rate was 37.6 percent. It ranked sixteenth-poorest among all the counties in the United States. And in 2015 drug deaths there ran at 141 for every 100,000 people, at a time when the national rate was roughly 16.

Think about what it costs to run a county that has lost four-fifths of its people. It still needs schools, a water system, fire protection and a hospital. Those cost nearly as much as before, but now only a fifth of the people are there to pay for them. Each store that closes gives the next store one less reason to stay open. Some of what is missing gets filled in by food assistance, disability payments and Medicaid, and the state and federal governments pay for those. In fact, when the United States began the 1961 pilot that became its modern food stamp program, the very first stamps were handed out in this county, in the town of Welch.

Nobody outside this book has put a price per ton on the decline of a county, so this figure is mine too. I estimate it at between $5 and $15 a ton, and I take $5. That makes three lines, and the bill stands at $8. Notice that $8 is already more than the $4.71 on the 1960 invoice, and we have not yet reached the largest line.

## The air

The largest line will be paid by people who haven't been born yet. Coal is mined so that it can be burned. Burning it releases carbon dioxide, and carbon dioxide stays in the air and warms the climate for everyone who comes afterward. Those people could not be at the table when the price of the coal was set, because they did not exist yet.

To put a number on this line I need two things. First I need to know how much carbon dioxide one ton of this coal releases. The Environmental Protection Agency publishes a standard factor for bituminous coal: 93.28 kilograms of carbon dioxide for every million British thermal units of heat, which is the unit used to measure how much energy a fuel holds. McDowell County mined a seam of coal called the Pocahontas seam, and by the figures of the United States Geological Survey and the Energy Information Administration that coal holds about 28 million of those units in every ton. When you multiply the two numbers, you find that one ton of McDowell coal releases about 2.61 metric tons of carbon dioxide. The average American bituminous coal holds less heat, so it releases less, about 2.32 tons.

Second, I need to know what each of those tons of carbon dioxide costs. I did not produce that figure, and neither did this book. It is the United States government's own estimate. In 2023 the Environmental Protection Agency estimated that one metric ton of carbon dioxide does $190 worth of damage. The agency drew on a study that Kevin Rennert and his colleagues published in the journal *Nature* in 2022. Economists call this figure the social cost of carbon.

Now the multiplication is simple. A ton of McDowell coal releases 2.61 tons of carbon dioxide, each of those costs $190, and so this line of the bill is $496.

That single line is many times larger than all the other lines put together. Here is another way to see its size. Today a ton of coal sells for somewhere between $40 and $140, depending on the grade, and $496 is more than three times the highest of those prices. Add it to the three lines before it and the bill now stands at $504. If I had used the top of each of my ranges and not the bottom, it would stand at $518.

Even so, the carbon figure leaves something out. Once coal has been burned for heat, it can't be used for anything else. It can't be used to make steel, or as a raw material for chemicals, or for any use that nobody has thought of yet. The economist Harold Hotelling described the idea underneath this in 1931. He pointed out that when a resource can't be renewed, using some of it now carries a cost, because that part will not be there to use later. Hotelling had in mind the mine owner, who gives up income he could have earned in the future. The loss I am describing falls on different people: those who come later and never owned the coal at all. The carbon figure also misses most of what a warming climate does to living systems, which is the kind of loss that Robert Costanza and his colleagues have worked to put a value on. I have not entered a figure on this bill for either of those losses.

## The lines that are harder to count

Four more lines belong on the bill. I should tell you plainly that they are harder to count than the first four, and that no outside source puts a price per ton on any of them. I have entered each one at a dollar or two. You could fairly ask why I enter them at all. My reason is that the costs are real, and if I left them off the bill I would be entering them at zero, which is the one figure I know to be wrong. Together the four come to $6 of the total.

The first of the four is the children, and Chapter 2 explained how this cost works. A family spends years of money, attention and worry on raising a child. In most places the child grows up and stays nearby, so what the family put in comes back to the place that raised them. In McDowell County the children grew up and left, because there was no work to stay for. Everything their families had spent on raising them went to work in other cities, and those cities had paid nothing toward it. The families who stayed often had only one thing of value to hand down, a house, and it stood in a place where houses had stopped selling. I estimate this line at between $1 and $5 a ton, and I enter it at $2.

Look at what a price of $4.71 does. It shows the world one number, and everything else on this bill stays hidden behind it. The word I use for that is masking. I took the word from computer security, which is the field I worked in later in my career. There, masking means hiding a true value behind a presented one. For a long time I didn't connect the word to anything older than my own trade. But the idea is much older.

In 1896 the poet Paul Laurence Dunbar published a poem called "We Wear the Mask." It begins: "We wear the mask that grins and lies, / It hides our cheeks and shades our eyes." Dunbar was writing about the face that Black Americans had to show the world in order to survive, and about what it cost them to hold that face in place. In 1903 W.E.B. Du Bois described the condition underneath the mask in his book *The Souls of Black Folk*. He called it a veil, and he described "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity." In 1952 Frantz Fanon carried the idea into the colonies, and into the economies that were built on them, in *Black Skin, White Masks*. Of the four, his ground is the closest to this chapter's. That same year Ralph Ellison published the novel *Invisible Man*, whose narrator says, "I am invisible, understand, simply because people refuse to see me."

Those writers understood what a hidden cost does to the people who carry it. They understood it long before I had a word for it, and I want the idea credited to them. What this book adds to their work is a ledger. It gives an amount for each cost, and it names who paid.

The second of the four lines is knowledge. The people of a mining community knew things that were true only of their own ground. They knew how to read one particular seam of coal. They knew how to keep one valley's water system working, and how the creek behaved in a hard winter. They knew those things because the same families had stayed in the same place long enough to learn them. When the families scattered, the knowledge went with them to places where it was no use to anyone. The same thing happened to the churches, the civic clubs, and the neighbors who could carry a family through a bad year. I enter this line at $1.

The third line is politics. The first lines of this bill went unwritten for decades. You might assume that was because nobody knew about the costs, but that is not the reason. The historian Alan Derickson has shown that doctors could identify black lung as a disease caused by the work early in the twentieth century. Yet it did not become grounds for federal compensation until 1969. It took the Farmington mine disaster of 1968, a strike by West Virginia miners, and the work of the Black Lung Association to force Congress to act.

The carbon figure on this bill has been fought over in the same way. Under President Obama, a federal working group set the social cost of carbon at about $42 a ton. That figure counted damage anywhere in the world. Under President Trump the figure was cut to between $1 and $7, and the way it was cut was to count only the damage that falls inside the United States. In 2021 Gernot Wagner and other economists criticized that change as having no grounding in the usual practice of weighing costs against benefits. Under President Biden the figure went back to about $51, and in 2023 the Environmental Protection Agency raised it to the $190 I have used here. The physics of carbon did not change from one administration to the next. What changed was the answer to a political question: how much of the cost should be counted at all? One side of that question has far more money to spend on it than the other. Oil and gas companies alone reported well over $100 million in federal lobbying in 2024, by the count of the research group OpenSecrets.

The money and the public attention that go into keeping a bill unwritten are themselves a cost. I enter that cost at $1, and of all the figures in this chapter it is the one most likely to be too low. I also want to credit the two bodies of work that gave me the vocabulary for this line. The first belongs to the economists and writers who have studied the racial wealth gap and made the case for reparations, among them William Darity Jr., A. Kirsten Mullen, Darrick Hamilton, Dalton Conley and Ta-Nehisi Coates. They showed how to name the people and the coalitions that shaped an arrangement. The second belongs to James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock, who founded the study of public choice. They described the reasoning of a company that spends money to win favorable rules, which economists call rent-seeking. I use that vocabulary without taking on the rest of their political program, and Chapter 9 says more about both.

The fourth line is speed. This coal lay in the ground for hundreds of millions of years. Most of it came out within about a century, and it came out fastest in the decades after the machines arrived. The country did not need the coal at that rate. It came out that fast because the machines made it possible, and because the companies earned more by mining faster. Suppose it had come out more slowly. There would have been time for other kinds of work to grow up in the county, and time for other sources of energy to be developed. A higher price would have slowed it, and we have seen a price do exactly that. After the oil shocks of the 1970s, energy suddenly cost more, and for the next two decades the United States steadily used less energy for each dollar it produced, according to the Energy Information Administration. Coal's price never sent that signal, because the price left out most of the cost. I enter this line at $2.

## The sum

Here is the whole bill for one ton of McDowell coal, with the lowest figure on every line.

- The lungs: $2
- The creeks: $1
- The town: $5
- The air: $496
- The children: $2
- Knowledge: $1
- Politics: $1
- Speed: $2

**Total: $510**

In 1960 the ton sold for $4.71. Written out now, its bill comes to $510. You may want to divide one number by the other, and I am not going to, because those are prices from two different eras and a dollar in 1960 was not a dollar today. The fair comparison is with what a ton of coal sells for now, which is $40 to $140. Against that price, the bill is about 3.6 to 13 times what the buyer pays.

Look at where the total comes from. Almost all of it is the air: $496 of the $510. That line rests on the government's figure and not on mine. The other seven lines, which rest partly or wholly on my own estimates, come to $14 together.

You will also remember that Chapter 6 gave a figure of $525 to $540 for this same ton. The two figures are related, and I should say how. Chapter 6 starts from the first four lines of this bill. Then it adds a term for what burning the coal takes away from the people who come later, which is the cost I left without a figure in the section on the air. So the two totals are not independent checks on each other, because they share the carbon line.

## What was paid, and by whom

Some of this bill was paid. But most of what was paid came from the public, and here is the breakdown.

The largest payment by far is the black lung benefits, which came to about $44 billion through 2009. They were paid out of a public trust fund. That fund is filled in two ways: by a tax on coal as it is sold, and by money borrowed from the federal treasury. Next, coal companies posted reclamation bonds worth about $3 billion to $5 billion, once you allow for the bonds that fell short. Then, between 2014 and 2016, three coal companies went bankrupt, and about $865 million of what they owed sick miners moved onto the public. Finally, I estimate that another $5 billion to $10 billion in miners' compensation and medical costs was paid over the years, most of it by public programs.

Add those together and you get about $53 billion to $60 billion. McDowell County and the counties around it produced about 600 million tons of coal. Divide the payments by the tons, and they come to between $88 and $100 a ton from everyone who paid anything. I should warn you that this figure is too high. The black lung benefits went to miners all across the United States, and I have divided them by the tons of a single coalfield. I leave the figure too high on purpose, because the error runs against my own argument: the more I count as paid, the less remains unpaid.

How much of that $88 to $100 did the coal industry pay? It paid between $8 and $13 a ton directly, through its bonds and its private settlements. And only the bonds were put up in advance, before the damage they were meant to cover. They come to between $5 and $8 a ton.

Now we can set what was paid beside what was owed. Take Chapter 6's figure of $525 to $540 a ton. At least about $425 to $450 of it was never paid by anyone. If you count only what the industry paid, the unpaid part is about $512 to $532. Most of the unpaid part is the air, and the people it is owed to have not been born.

## Not only here

You might wonder whether McDowell County is a special case. The earlier chapters show that it isn't. In Libby, Montana, the federal cleanup has cost more than $600 million. That is about six times the roughly $100 million the mine is estimated to have taken in, and the company repaid $250 million of it. In the Gulf of Mexico, BP paid about $61.6 billion after the Deepwater Horizon spill. It is the rare case in which the company that did the damage paid most of the bill, and it paid because lawsuits forced it to. At Baotou, in Inner Mongolia, nobody has published a count of the damage that could be set beside the revenue, so I can't give you a figure at all. Norway is the case that runs the other way. It shows that money can be set aside against a bill like this while the resource is still being sold. The fund Norway built from its oil holds about $36 for every barrel of oil and gas the country has produced.

I should also tell you that the method does not find a gap everywhere it looks. There are two situations in which it returns zero. The first is when a perfect substitute exists for the thing that was used up. In that case nothing has been taken from the people who come later, because they can use the substitute. The second is when a resource renews itself and is used no faster than it renews. A fishery is the plain example. If the harvest stays below the rate at which the fish replace themselves, it leaves no bill behind, and Chapter 3 showed what happens to a bay when the harvest runs faster than that. So the bill appears only where use runs ahead of what can be restored. With coal it always does, because coal does not come back.

## What honest pricing doesn't mean

Writing this bill doesn't mean the coal should have stayed in the ground. Cheap energy lifted a great many people out of poverty. The steel made with this coal built railroads, ships and bridges. The men who mined it did hard, skilled work, and that work was worth doing. The bill doesn't ask whether the coal should have been mined. It asks who should have paid what the mining cost, and it shows that for the most part the buyer of the coal didn't.

It doesn't mean the market was fake, either. The coal was real and the money was real, and $4.71 was a true price for what it covered. What it covered was the work of getting the coal out of the ground. Everything else in this chapter happened alongside that sale. It happened to people who were never asked to agree to it, and none of it reached the invoice. So the market was incomplete, and this chapter has filled in the part it left out. I am not the first to say that a price can leave a cost out. The economist Arthur Pigou described it more than a century ago, and Chapter 6 says what this book borrows from him and what it adds.

So the rail car that left McDowell County in 1960 carried two bills. One was for $4.71, and it was paid on the spot. The other was for $510, and most of it is still owed. That leaves the next question. What would a country that decided to pay a bill like that actually do?
