# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 6: Three Ways of Counting

## The Sound

At 9:12 on the evening of March 23, 1989, the oil tanker Exxon Valdez left the pipeline terminal at Valdez, Alaska, carrying about 53 million gallons of crude oil. There was ice in the shipping lanes, and the captain ordered the ship out of the lanes to go around it. He handed the wheelhouse to the third mate, with instructions to turn back at a certain point, and went to his quarters. The turn was never made. At 12:04 the next morning the ship ran aground on Bligh Reef, in Prince William Sound.

About 11 million gallons of oil went into the water. Storms, wind and currents carried it along some 1,300 miles of shoreline, and it reached a village 460 miles from the reef. Shortly after the spill, the Pacific herring of the sound spawned along its shores, and the oil had reached a large part of the places where they spawn and gather.

The herring mattered to almost everything else in the sound. Marine mammals, birds and other fish ate them, and people fished them: for food, for bait, for their roe and for the spawn they leave on kelp. The catches of the late 1980s had been the largest on record. When the lawsuits were gathered into a single case, more than 32,000 people had joined it, and the court sorted those who asked for compensation into three groups: commercial fishermen, Native Alaskans and landowners. The Supreme Court later described them as people who depended on Prince William Sound for their livelihoods.

What did the spill cost? There are at least three answers on the public record, and they don't match.

The first answer is the cost of cleaning up. At its peak the cleanup put 10,000 workers on the beaches, with about 1,000 boats and roughly 100 aircraft, and it ran for more than four summers before it was called off. Exxon says it spent about $2.1 billion on it. Not every beach was cleaned. Researchers paid from the settlement later found oil still lying under the surface of some shorelines, some of it as toxic as it was in 1989.

The second answer is what the company was made to pay for the damage. In 1991 Exxon settled with the United States and the State of Alaska. It was fined $150 million, and the court forgave $125 million of the fine in recognition of its cooperation in the cleanup and its payment of some private claims. It paid $100 million in restitution for the injuries to fish, wildlife and land, and it agreed to pay $900 million over ten years toward restoring them. It paid another $303 million directly to fishermen, property owners and others. Then came the trial. A jury awarded the commercial fishermen $287 million for their losses, and it awarded $5 billion in punitive damages against Exxon. An appeals court cut the $5 billion in half. In 2008, nineteen years after the spill, the Supreme Court held that the punitive award could be no larger than the compensation, which a lower court had put at $507.5 million.

The third answer is what was lost, and the record is thinnest here. The State of Alaska paid for a study of the loss to people who never use the sound but value its being there, which economists call passive use value, and the study put that loss at $2.8 billion. No one knows how many animals died. The best estimates are 250,000 seabirds, 2,800 sea otters, 300 harbor seals, 250 bald eagles and as many as 22 killer whales.

And then there are the herring. In 1993, four years after the spill and two years after the settlement was approved, the adult herring population of the sound crashed, and that year's catch was about 14 percent of the catch the year before. The fishery opened briefly in 1997 and 1998, and by the trustees' account it has been closed since. For most of the years since the spill there has been no herring season in Prince William Sound. The council of state and federal trustees that manages the settlement money lists the herring as a resource that is not recovering. It also says plainly that the spill's effect on the herring population as a whole was never clearly established. It names disease, predation and the poor survival of young fish among the things that have kept the population down. The settlement had left a window, from 2002 to 2006, in which the governments could claim up to $100 million more for injuries nobody could have foreseen in 1991. In 2006 they began a claim for $92 million, to clean up the oil that was still there. In 2015 they told the court they would not pursue it.

So there is a number for the cleanup, a number for the settlement, a number for the punishment and a number from a study. For a fishery that fed a sound and the people around it, and that has not come back, there is no number at all.

Which of these is the cost of the spill? None of them is, alone. The cleanup figure is what it took to try to put things back, and things were not all put back. The settlement and the awards are what was paid, which is a different matter from what was owed. The study and the death counts are attempts at what was lost, and the largest loss has no figure, because no one can yet say how much of it the spill caused. An honest account of that night needs all three kinds of number, and it needs to know which kind each one is.

## What is owed

Chapter 5 ended on a claim: the bonds posted against extraction are a small fraction of the cost it leaves behind. A claim like that needs a number, and a number needs a method that a skeptical reader can check. So take the case this book knows best, a ton of coal leaving a mine in McDowell County, West Virginia, in 1960. It sold at the mine mouth for $4.71, the average the U.S. Energy Information Administration records for bituminous coal that year. What did that ton cost the people who came after the sale?

It cost them two kinds of thing.

The first is what later people can no longer have. The ton is gone, and nobody who comes afterward can use it or decide to leave it where it was. How much that matters depends on whether something else can do the same job. If a substitute exists that does everything the coal did, at a price people can pay, then later generations have lost very little. If there is no substitute, they have lost all of it. So this part of the cost is weighted, year by year, by the chance that no adequate substitute exists yet. For coal burned to make electricity, substitutes are arriving quickly. For helium in the coldest laboratory work, or water from an aquifer that took ten thousand years to fill, they may never arrive.

The second is the damage that keeps running after the sale. Black lung kills miners decades after their last shift. Acid drains from a closed mine for generations. Carbon from the burned coal stays in the air for centuries. A substitute for coal does nothing about any of this. A solar panel does not clean a creek. This book calls that stream of continuing damage the externality tail, borrowing the statistician's word for the long part of a curve that never quite reaches zero.

Add the two together across all the years they run, count the distant years for less than the near ones, and the sum is what the extraction owes. This book's name for the sum is the residual commons value. It is what the ton was worth to everyone except the people who bought and sold it. Written as a formula, the whole idea fits on one line:

$$\mathrm{RCV} = \int_{t_0}^{\infty} \bigl\{[1 - S(t)] \cdot U(t) + E(t)\bigr\} \cdot D(t)\, dt$$

Here *S* is the chance that a substitute exists in year *t*, *U* is what a unit of the resource is worth to the people alive that year, *E* is the damage still arriving that year, and *D* is the weight given to a year that far off. The formula adds nothing to the two paragraphs above it. It only keeps the argument still while the numbers go in, and the Technical Appendix carries the derivation for readers who want it.

Two more terms come out of it, and the rest of the chapter uses both. What has actually been set against the cost is the accountability bond. What is owed, less the bond, is the severed cost that Chapter 5 named. When this chapter speaks of the gap, that subtraction is what it means.

Now the numbers for the McDowell ton. Burning a ton of coal from the Pocahontas seams releases about 2.61 metric tons of carbon dioxide. That figure is the Environmental Protection Agency's emission factor multiplied by the heat content of those seams as the U.S. Geological Survey reports it. An average American bituminous coal holds less heat and releases about 2.32 tons. In 2023 the EPA put the damage done by one ton of carbon dioxide at $190. Its estimate drew on a 2022 study in *Nature* by Kevin Rennert and colleagues, whose own central figure was about $185. At $190, the carbon from one McDowell ton does about $496 of damage. On the national average it would be $441.

The damage that isn't carbon is far smaller. Health costs come to $2 to $4 a ton and environmental cleanup to $1 to $3. Community costs come to $5 to $15, and that last range is this book's own estimate, not a published one. Together they are $8 to $22 a ton. The first kind of cost, the ton no longer there for anyone later, I have taken at the lowest value the definition allows.

Put through the formula, the McDowell ton comes to between $525 and $540. The spread comes from the weight given to distant years. I ran the calculation three times, with discount rates that start at 1.4 percent, 4 percent and 5.5 percent and all decline toward 2 percent, and the answers were $526, $537 and $541. The first rate is the one Nicholas Stern used in his review of climate economics, and the last is the one associated with William Nordhaus. The result barely moves, because about 92 percent of it is the carbon term. The lost access to the coal itself supplies about 7 percent.

That is the headline for this chapter: a ton that sold for $4.71 in 1960 carries a cost of about $525 in today's dollars. Those are two prices from two eras, and I set them side by side without dividing one by the other, because the ratio would mostly measure sixty years of inflation. The fair comparison is with what such coal sells for now, which is $40 to $140 a ton depending on its grade. Against that price the cost is about 4 to 13 times what the buyer pays.

I should say how little of that number is mine. The damage total is other people's work. The carbon figure is the EPA's. In 2011 Paul Epstein and colleagues estimated that coal costs the American public between a third and more than half of a trillion dollars a year over its life cycle. In the same year Nicholas Muller, Robert Mendelsohn and William Nordhaus found that the air pollution from coal-fired power alone does damage worth 0.8 to 5.6 times the value the industry adds. The finding that coal's unpriced damage rivals or exceeds its price is theirs. My own term adds 7 percent. I have also made a choice a reader should know about: all the carbon is charged to the ton at the mine, and none to the power plant that burned it or the household that used the electricity. A different split would lower the figure per ton without changing the total by a dollar.

Chapter 8 follows one ton through every part of the damage, eight parts in all, and arrives at a total of $510. Here the question is a different one. Is $525 to $540 a real number, or one I could have made come out however I liked? It can be checked in two ways, and neither check uses the EPA's damage figure.

## First check: what would it cost to replace it?

The first check comes from engineering. Suppose we wanted to undo the damage. What would the work cost?

For carbon the work has a name and a price list. Direct air capture is machinery that pulls carbon dioxide back out of the air. The plants running today, such as the Climeworks installations in Iceland, do it for roughly $600 to $1,000 a ton of carbon dioxide. Plants at commercial scale within the next decade are expected to do it for $300 to $600, and the International Energy Agency and the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change describe a path to $100 to $300 by the middle of the century.

Apply those prices to the 2.61 tons that one McDowell ton released, and add this book's rougher estimates for restoring the stripped land and resettling the people who had to leave, which together are less than 4 percent of the total. Replacement comes to between $290 and $875 a ton of coal at the prices hoped for in 2050, between $812 and $1,658 at the prices expected within a decade, and between $1,595 and $2,702 at today's prices. The middle of the middle case is about $1,235.

Every one of those figures is larger than the headline. That is what one would expect, since it costs more to take a ton of carbon dioxide out of the air, at $300 to $600, than the $190 of damage the EPA says the ton does while it stays there. Repair usually costs more than the harm would have cost to prevent. So this check can't confirm the headline to the dollar, and I don't read it that way. What it can do is contradict. If undoing the damage had turned out to cost $50 a ton of coal, a claim that the coal owed $525 would have been in trouble. It turned out to cost at least $290, and that is under the kindest assumptions about machines not yet built.

Replacement cost has a limit, and the limit is what the next check is for. An engineer can price a machine that removes carbon. No engineer can price the return of the coal seam, or of the mountaintop above it, or of a county of about a hundred thousand people that now holds fewer than twenty thousand. A replacement figure covers what can be rebuilt and leaves out what can't, so it is a floor under the cost of setting things right. What about the part that nothing can rebuild?

## Second check: what can't be bought back?

The second check starts from the market price and asks how much more a thing is worth when taking it can't be undone.

The idea is not mine. In 1974 Kenneth Arrow and Anthony Fisher, and separately Claude Henry, showed why a loss that can't be reversed should be weighed more heavily than one that can: once the thing is gone, so is every later chance to decide differently. The formula I use to turn that idea into a number is mine, and I want to be exact about that. It is a simple construction, not their model, and its output is a check on the headline, not a finding.

It works like this. Start with what the resource sells for. Raise that a little for scarcity, by a factor that runs from about 1.27 to 1.31 for McDowell coal. Then raise it a lot for irreversibility, by a factor that depends on how likely it is that the loss can never be restored at any price. For fossil carbon, and for a mountain whose top has been removed, the physical record puts that likelihood between 85 and 95 percent. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change says so of carbon in its sixth assessment, and Emily Bernhardt and Margaret Palmer documented it for mountaintop mining in 2011. At 85 percent the factor is a little under 7. At 95 percent it is 20.

For a McDowell ton that sells today for $40 to $140, the premium figure runs from about $340 to about $3,670, with its center near $1,115. The range is wide for two plain reasons. Coal of different grades sells at very different prices, and the distance between an 85 percent chance and a 95 percent chance of permanence triples the answer. That second reason is not sloppiness in the method. It is the method reporting how much depends on one question, which is whether the loss is truly permanent.

There is something this check cannot show, and a reader should hold me to it. Both of its factors are at least one, so the result can never come out below the market price. A premium figure of 8.5 to 26 times the price is therefore no evidence that the coal was underpriced. The check assumes there is a premium and measures only its size.

So what do the two checks buy? They are built from different data. The first uses engineering costs and no market price. The second uses the market price and no engineering costs. Neither uses the EPA's $190. They land near each other, around $1,100 to $1,250 at their centers, and both land above the headline. I don't claim that this agreement proves anything, because both ranges are wide, and wide ranges overlap easily. The claim is narrower. Two different roads to the question both say that $525 to $540 does not overstate what the ton owes.

Those are two ways of counting what is owed, and they make up one side of the ledger. The other side has no model in it at all. It is a matter of record. What was actually paid, and who paid it?

## Third count: what was paid, and by whom?

The third way of counting reads receipts.

First, what counts as a payment. A cost can be covered in either of two ways. One is an instrument: money posted or paid against a named cost or risk, such as a reclamation bond, a tax on each ton, a fund or a court judgment. An instrument counts even if nobody knew how large the cost would be when the money was put up. The other is the consent of the person who bears the cost, and consent counts only as far as that person could see the cost and was free to refuse it. A worker whose wage included a premium for a danger he could see had agreed to that danger. He had not agreed to a harm he could not see, and a person forced to bear a cost has agreed to nothing. A cost is severed when someone bears it and it was covered in neither way.

Now the receipts for McDowell coal. The largest is the federal Black Lung Disability Trust Fund, which the Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Research Service report had paid out about $44 billion through 2009, the last year for which a running total is published. The fund is fed by an excise tax on coal sold today, and when the tax falls short, it borrows from the general fund of the United States Treasury. As of September 2024 it owed $5.1 billion. The coal companies have posted reclamation bonds which, after their known shortfalls, are worth about $3 billion to $5 billion. Bankruptcies between 2014 and 2016 moved about $0.9 billion of mining liabilities onto taxpayers. And I estimate that another $5 billion to $10 billion in miners' compensation and medical costs has been carried by public programs. That last figure is my estimate and not a published one.

Altogether that is $53 billion to $60 billion. Divide it by the roughly 600 million tons mined in McDowell and the counties next to it, and everything paid by anyone comes to $88 to $100 a ton. That figure is too high, and I know it is too high. The black lung total covers the whole country, and I have divided it by the tonnage of a few counties. I keep it because the error runs against my own argument. Overstating what was paid understates what was left unpaid.

Of that $88 to $100, the part the industry paid directly, through reclamation bonds and private settlements, is $8 to $13 a ton. The part that was posted ahead of time, against damage still to come, is the reclamation bonds alone, at $5 to $8 a ton. Nearly everything else was paid after the harm was done, through a public fund.

Now subtract. The ton owed $525 to $540, and at most $88 to $100 was paid against it by anyone. At least $425 to $450 a ton was left unpaid. Measured against what the industry itself paid, the gap is about $512 to $532.

The other cases in this book read the same way, with one exception worth looking at.

Deepwater Horizon is the exception. BP's own final estimate of what the spill cost it, made in 2016, was about $61.6 billion. The company paid it, because the law could reach a solvent company that everyone could identify and the litigation forced the payment. The damage to the Gulf that continued beyond what BP paid is estimated at another $8 billion to $12 billion. So here, unusually, the party that caused the cost paid most of the bill that could be documented.

At Libby the federal cleanup has cost more than $600 million. In 2008 W.R. Grace agreed to repay $250 million of that to the government. The payment covered part of the cleanup. It was not paid to the sick or to the families of the dead, and the public carried the remaining $350 million and more. What Grace's bankruptcy trust has paid to injured people in Libby is not itemized in any source I have found, so I leave that line without a figure.

Norway is the case where a country paid on purpose and in advance. Its petroleum fund stood at 21,268 billion kroner at the end of 2025, according to the bank that manages it. That is about $2.0 trillion, set against roughly 55 billion barrels of oil and gas produced, with the gas counted at its equivalent in oil, or about $36 a barrel.

And at Prince William Sound, the company paid for the cleanup and paid the settlement, and the punitive award against it was cut to about a tenth of what the jury had set. Nobody has paid anything for the herring.

Put the receipts next to each other and one thing stands out. In some places nothing was paid at all. In one place the company that did the damage paid most of it, and it took years of litigation to make that happen. Everywhere else, where a bill is being paid at all, most of it is being paid by the public.

A few of these figures need their sources stated. The Norway price is the Energy Information Administration's range for crude oil. The Deepwater revenue is my own arithmetic, about 50 million barrels at about $80 a barrel, and not a published figure. The Libby death estimate multiplies the 694 deaths documented through 2011 in a 2017 study by Samantha Naik and colleagues by the value the EPA assigns to a statistical life, $7.4 million to $11.0 million. Because people are still dying, it is a floor. Baotou's documents support a cleanup floor and nothing more, so the line stops there.

Read down the last column. In every row what was owed is more than what was paid, and the direction never flips. It is the strongest thing the table says. It needs a caution attached, because the rows are not five independent experiments. The McDowell and Norway figures both rest on one price for carbon, the EPA's $190, and the replacement check counts the same tons of carbon the headline does. If that carbon price is wrong, those rows are wrong together. What the table shows is a pattern in the record. It is not a proof.

The table also raises the question a fair reader would ask next. Every row comes back with a gap. Can the method return a small one? Can it ever return none?

## Norway

If the method only ever reported disasters, it would be useless. So try it on the best-run extraction in the world.

Chapter 4 told how Norway came to manage its oil. The short version is that Norway passed a law creating a fund in 1990 and made the first deposit in 1996. The state's income from oil and gas goes into the fund, and the government may spend only the return the fund is expected to earn, which was set at 4 percent in 2001 and lowered to 3 percent in 2017. Norway has also taxed its own carbon since 1991. This is the nearest thing in the world to the rule John Hartwick wrote down in 1977: if you use up a resource that cannot be renewed, invest what it earned, so that the people who come later inherit the wealth even though they can't inherit the oil.

How well did Norway do by that rule? Very well. The state's net income from petroleum from 1971 through 2025, as the Ministry of Finance and Statistics Norway report it, comes to about $25 a barrel. The state takes 70 to 80 percent of the profit that comes from the resource itself, which economists call the rent, so the whole rent is roughly $32 to $36 a barrel. The fund holds about $36 a barrel. Norway has saved essentially all of the rent its oil produced, and because the money was invested, a little more.

Then why does the table show a gap? Because the rent was never the only cost. A barrel of oil, burned, releases about 0.37 tons of carbon dioxide, and at the EPA's $190 that is about $70 of damage. I have not run the full calculation for Norway, so the $70 is the carbon alone, and the true figure is higher. Subtract the $36 in the fund and at least $34 a barrel remains. Over everything Norway has produced, that is about $1.9 trillion.

Even that subtraction is generous to Norway. The fund was built to save oil wealth for later Norwegians. It was never meant to pay for carbon, and none of it goes to the people the carbon harms, most of whom are not Norwegian. A payment covers the cost it was set against and no other. Taken cost by cost, Norway has covered the rent in full and the carbon not at all.

So the fund doesn't put the barrel back in the ground, and it doesn't take the carbon out of the air. What it does is the thing Hartwick asked for. For Norwegians, who chose this through their own institutions and can see what they are choosing, the cost of drawing down their own oil is covered twice, by their consent and by the fund. For everyone else, the cost arrives uncovered.

The method told two cases apart. It found that McDowell left nearly everything unpaid and that Norway paid the whole of one cost and none of another. Norway is closer to honest pricing than anywhere else, and it is still short by a sum nearly as large as the fund itself, measured by the carbon alone.

That is a smaller gap. Is there such a thing as no gap?

## When the answer is zero

There is, and it matters that there is. A method that could never answer "nothing is owed here" would not be measuring anything.

Take a forest that is cut no faster than it grows, by an owner who replants and who has posted a bond against the damage that logging does to the streams. Later generations lose nothing, because the forest is still there. What damage there is has money standing behind it. The gap is zero.

Or take sand dug from a pit under a permit that requires a bond large enough to restore the land. Ordinary sand is so plentiful that nobody in any future will miss this load of it. The bond covers the hole, and the gap is zero again.

Or take a person who chooses a long commute knowing exactly what it will cost in hours and judging the lower rent worth it. That person saw the cost and was free to refuse it. That is consent, and nothing is owed.

These cases show where the method's reach stops. A cost is severed only when something is used up faster than it can restore itself, and the cost falls on someone who neither agreed to it nor was paid for it. A fishery harvested within what it can replace owes nothing for the fish. The method does not say that all extraction does harm, and it does not say that leaving things alone is always right.

They also give every line in the ledger three possible states, and Chapter 8 will use all three. A line can be Zero: the cost was looked for and isn't there, as with the sand. A line can be Filled: there is a figure, and the working is shown. Or a line can be Open: the cost is real and belongs in the account, and no figure has been entered, because nobody can honestly supply one yet. The herring of Prince William Sound are an Open line. So are the payments to the sick at Libby, and so is the price of coerced labor that Chapter 5 left to the scholars whose work it is. An Open line is not a zero. It means that the total above it is a floor.

Two fair objections belong here.

The first is that I picked my parameters. A different carbon price, a different guess about substitutes, a different weight on the future, and I could have had any number I liked. Part of that is true, and the next section shows exactly how far the number moves and why. But this objection would bite harder if the result could not fail, and it can. If the record anywhere showed a bond as large as the cost it stood against, that case would count against the pattern, and the forest and the sandpit describe what such a case looks like. Among the fossil fuel cases, I have not found one. The reason is not built into the arithmetic. No law anywhere yet requires anyone to post money against what a later generation loses.

The second objection is that two analysts using this method on the same mine will get two different numbers. They will. That is true of every method used to cost things that haven't finished happening, whether the thing is a nuclear plant to be taken apart or a pension to be funded or a regulation to be judged. Those fields don't treat a range as a failure. What they treat as a failure is a range whose causes are hidden. So the rule here is that every figure reports the value used and how it was reached, the checks that could be run, what was paid and by whom, and the price it is compared with. Two analysts who disagree can then see which input they disagree about and go and look it up.

Which input matters most?

## What moves the number

Carbon does. It is 92 percent of the McDowell figure, and the price put on it has changed with each American administration. Under President Obama the federal government valued a ton of carbon dioxide at about $42. The first Trump administration used $1 to $7. The Biden administration began at about $51, and in 2023 the EPA published $190. Nothing about the atmosphere changed between those numbers.

So here is what the McDowell figure does when the carbon price moves. At $51 a ton of carbon dioxide instead of $190, the ton of coal owes about $165 to $180. That is still more than was paid, but only 1.2 to 4.5 times the coal's price today. With no price on carbon at all, the ton owes about $30 to $45, which is less than the $88 to $100 I credited as paid. The whole gap for McDowell depends on carbon costing at least something like $20 to $25 a ton. A reader who believes carbon does no damage should reject most of my number, and I would rather say so here than have it found later.

Two things should be said about that. The first is that the $88 to $100 is the overstated figure, the national payments divided by local tons, so the comparison at a carbon price of zero is tilted against the gap. The second is that zero is not a neutral setting. To leave carbon out is to claim that it does exactly no harm, and no body of evidence supports that claim. The low figures and the high ones are all positions. I use $190 because it is the most recent official estimate from the agency whose job it is to make one, and I show what happens if you don't.

The second thing that moves the number is whether substitutes arrive. In the calculation I assumed that 85 percent of what coal does can eventually be done by something else. If nothing could ever replace it, the figure would rise from about $525 to about $573. The effect is small for coal, because coal's cost is mostly damage. For a resource whose cost is mostly lost access, it would be the whole story.

This is an old argument in economics, and the framework takes a side in it. In 1974 Robert Solow argued that when a resource runs down, other forms of capital can stand in for it almost without limit, so that a society which reinvests the proceeds leaves its descendants no poorer. Herman Daly spent a career arguing the opposite: some of what nature provides has no substitute at any price, and to treat it as replaceable is the central error of the standard accounts. The formula above sides with Daly on the principle and with Solow on the method. It allows that substitution can be nearly complete for some things and can stop well short for others, such as helium for the coldest laboratory work, water from a fossil aquifer or a stable climate. And it treats the question as one of fact, to be settled resource by resource.

That is also why I would rather argue about substitutes than about discount rates. How much weight the living owe the unborn has been disputed since Frank Ramsey raised it in 1928, and nobody has settled it. Stern and Nordhaus chose rates far apart, and Martin Weitzman showed in 2001 that when the right rate is uncertain, the rate that should be used declines over time toward the lowest of the candidates, which is the schedule I use. For McDowell the choice barely matters, as the three results in the second section showed. How fast a substitute for something can be built is a different kind of question. Engineers and hydrologists can work on it, and their answers get better.

All of this concerns the size of the costs once they are in the count. There is an earlier question. What is allowed into the count in the first place?

## The gates

Without a rule for what gets in, a method like this one is a way of adding up grievances. Anyone could propose any cost, and the total would say more about the analyst than about the mine. So a cost enters the count only if it passes four gates. Two of them decide whether it belongs. The other two check that it is stated in a form that can be added.

The first gate is a test, and this book calls it the Commons Inversion Test. It has three steps.

First, say at what level the claim is made: for whom, over how long, and counting which substitutes. The same activity can pass at one level and fail at another. One person's attention during one evening restores itself by morning. Whether the attention of a whole generation does is a different claim, and it has to be argued as one.

Second, check that the cost is attached to something shared and not to something privately owned. This step asks what would be different if the shared thing were not there at all.

Third, turn the scarcity around. Imagine the shared thing were unlimited, or restored itself at no cost. Does the cost disappear? If it does, the cost comes from using up something finite, and it belongs in the count. If the cost would remain even in a world of plenty, it is a cost of something else.

Try it on the coalfield. The claim is that black lung is a cost of McDowell coal, borne by the miners, their families and the public programs that treat them, across their lifetimes. The shared thing is breathable air underground and the health of the people who work there. Suppose the lungs of a mining workforce could take in any amount of dust without harm. Then the disease, and its cost, would vanish. The cost passes.

Now try it on something small. In Chapter 1 I signed a lease that put me two and a half hours from work in each direction. The claim is that the hours were a cost, to one person, over the length of the lease. The shared thing here is shared only between me and myself a year later: the time of one life. If a person's hours were unlimited, would five of them a day cost anything? No. The cost passes, even though there is one person in the case and nobody did anything wrong.

What does not pass? A claim that mining cost a town its sense of meaning fails, because nobody can describe a world with unlimited meaning, and the test needs that world in order to run. A claim also fails if the only way to make the cost vanish is to imagine someone else paying it. Every cost vanishes for its bearer when another person pays, and that tells us about the bond and nothing about the cost. A third kind of claim passes the second step and fails the third. If twenty boats share a fishery and quarrel over who may fish where, the quarrel would go on even if the fish were endless. That is a problem of governing a shared thing, not of using it up, and Elinor Ostrom's work is the place to take it. I come back to her at the end of the chapter.

The second gate asks that the cost be stated in dollars per unit, per year. A cost given only as a ranking or a score waits outside until someone converts it and shows the conversion. The third gate asks that the cost add up to a finite sum, since a claim of infinite damage would swallow every other line in the ledger. These two are checks of form, and most measured costs pass them without trouble.

The fourth gate is the other one that decides. A cost may not be counted twice. If the collapse of a town is entered once as lost community and again as lost habitability, one of the entries has to go, or the two have to be redrawn so that they don't overlap.

The gates leave two things out on purpose. They don't prove that a particular mine caused a particular harm. That has to be shown case by case, with the evidence and its source, as I tried to do for the herring. And they don't decide who bears a cost or what that person knew. That question belongs to the bond side of the ledger, and it is why the commute comes out differently depending on what the person signing could see. The cost of my hours passed the gates. Whether it was severed depends on whether I knew what I was agreeing to, and I didn't.

Running the test across the cases in this book has turned up ten kinds of shared thing that extraction draws on. They are the habitability of a place, space, time, institutions, the knowledge and culture passed down among kin, ecosystems, the political process, the trust between strangers that this book calls cohesion, what a society is able to know, and the autonomy of persons. The list is not complete, and it was never meant to be. These ten are examples: the kinds that happened to come up in the cases this book examines. A reader who runs the same test on other cases will find others, and may well divide these differently. Political traditions also disagree about which of them are truly shared. A classical liberal holds that autonomy belongs to the individual, and a civic republican holds that it depends on institutions held in common. The test doesn't settle that argument and doesn't need to. It takes whatever a tradition holds in common and asks what was drawn from it and who paid. The appendix lists the ten with the costs each one revealed, and it works seven cases through all four gates.

Every cost that passes those gates in this chapter falls, in large part, on people who have not been born. Why should anything be owed to them?

## People not yet born

Most of what the McDowell ton owes, it owes to people who weren't alive in 1960, and a good part of that to people who aren't alive now. Any account that sends a bill to the future has to answer a hard question about them, and the hardest form of it comes from the philosopher Derek Parfit.

In *Reasons and Persons*, published in 1984, Parfit pointed out that large choices change who gets born. A world that burns its coal quickly and a world that burns it slowly will not contain the same people. Different couples meet, and different children are conceived. So the people who inherit the hot, depleted world cannot say they would have been better off under the other policy. Under the other policy they would not exist. Whom, then, did the policy wrong? Parfit called this the non-identity problem, and it is the deepest objection to any claim that the present can owe the future.

His response was to stop asking about particular people and ask about outcomes. Whoever comes to live in the future, we can ask whether their world is worse than the world that some other set of people would have had. If it is, something of value has been lost, even though no one person can point to himself as the one who was robbed. That is the footing this book stands on. The residual commons value does not price what was taken from a particular miner's grandchild. It prices what will be missing from the world that whoever comes next has to live in.

Parfit did not claim to have solved his problem, and neither has anyone since. I have not solved it either. What his work gives this book is a serious, long-examined way to hold that the future has a claim, so that the claim does not rest on my asking you to feel it.

Parfit's answer says that future people count. It doesn't say how to measure what they lose. For that the book draws on Amartya Sen. Sen argued that a life should be judged by what a person is actually able to do and to be, which he called capabilities: to live to old age, to be healthy, to take part in the life of a community. In those terms, what extraction takes from the future is a narrowing of what people will be able to do. A county where a man lives thirteen years less than the national average, as the estimates for McDowell have it, has lost a capability that can be counted in years. The residual commons value is an attempt to put that narrowing into a figure.

A third philosopher supplies the piece that the calculation depends on most. John Broome, in *Counting the Cost of Global Warming* in 1992, in his 1994 paper "Discounting the Future" and in *Climate Matters* in 2012, argued three things. The rate at which a society discounts future harm is a moral choice, and it cannot be read off a market interest rate as though it were a fact about the world. Counting a person's wellbeing for less merely because that person lives later has no ethical defense. And a generation that knowingly imposes harm on those who follow owes them restitution. This book holds the first position and builds the third into its design. On the second, I show the results under several discount rates so that the reader can see what the choice does. Among the three of them, Parfit gives future people standing, Sen says what it is they stand to lose, and Broome says how that loss should be weighed across time.

For harm that has already been done, the book stands on different ground, because the people who were wronged in the past are particular people, with names and descendants. That ground belongs to the economics of reparations, and Chapter 5 set it out. William Darity Jr. and A. Kirsten Mullen built the method for pricing a harm that has already happened and been handed down. The method is theirs, and the framework adopts it. Wherever this book prices the legacy of a past harm, such as the years of life lost in McDowell County, it is borrowing their way of working and applying it to a different injury.

None of this will persuade someone who holds that the living owe the unborn nothing. I know of no argument that can force that conclusion on a person who refuses it. But the refusal has a price, and the price should be visible. It means that a child born in McDowell County this year, into a county that has lost four-fifths of its people, has no claim on anyone for coal that was sold for $4.71 a ton more than sixty years before she was born.

## Borrowed and added

Has all of this been done before? Much of it has, and a reader is owed an exact account of which parts.

The idea that a price can leave out a cost borne by third parties is Arthur Pigou's, from 1920, and so is the remedy of charging the cost back through a price. The economics of a resource that runs out is Harold Hotelling's, from 1931. He showed how the owner of a mine should value what remains in the ground, and in 1989 Salah El Serafy showed how an owner should keep the sale of that asset apart from true income. Both describe the owner's side of depletion. This framework prices the other side, which is the loss to people who are not the owner. The rule that a society should reinvest what a depleted resource earns is Hartwick's, and Partha Dasgupta carried that kind of accounting to the scale of whole nations, most fully in *The Economics of Biodiversity*, the review he wrote for the British Treasury in 2021. They work at the level of a country's books. The framework works at the level of a single ton or barrel.

The idea of a bond posted before the damage is not mine either. In 1990 Robert Costanza and Charles Perrings proposed that a company post a bond "equal to the current best estimate of the largest potential future environmental damages," to be returned only if the damage did not happen. That is the closest thing to what this book proposes, and it differs on one point. Their bond is sized to the worst case in total. The reserve here is sized to the cost each unit leaves unpaid, and it builds up as the units are taken out.

Counting the damage after the fact is established practice. Under American law, public trustees assess the injury a spill does to natural resources and recover the cost of restoring them from whoever was responsible. That is what happened at Prince William Sound and again, at far greater scale, after Deepwater Horizon. The method works where there is a responsible party that can be found, can pay and is within reach of the law. The international study known as The Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity, and the true cost accounting that grew from it, put prices on what nature provides to whole sectors and food systems. They share this book's aim, and they work at a different scale. They don't attach a figure to a unit of extraction or compare it with what was paid.

Then there is Elinor Ostrom, and here I want to be careful, because her work is often set against the kind of thing this book does and should not be. In *Governing the Commons*, published in 1990, Ostrom showed that people who share a resource are not doomed to ruin it. Fishing villages, irrigation districts and the users of a groundwater basin have governed what they share for generations, under rules they made themselves. Her cases have a common feature. The people who bear the costs of using the resource are, by and large, the same people who share its fate and help to make its rules. The cases in this book are the ones where that feature is missing. The company that mined McDowell did not live under the mountain. The people who will live with the carbon from Norway's oil had no vote in Norway. Some of those who bear the cost are absent or scattered, and others are present but overmatched. Scholars in Ostrom's own tradition are already working on that second situation. Studying watershed groups in India and the United States, Praneeta Mudliar and Tomas Koontz found in 2021 that her design principles "can conceal and perpetuate power asymmetries" even while they improve environmental outcomes. Ostrom asks how people who share a resource can govern it so that it lasts. This book asks what is owed when the people who bear the cost had no hand in the rules. A single place can need both answers.

The diagnosis that powerful parties shift costs onto those who can't refuse them is older than this book as well. Chapter 5 credited it to K. William Kapp and to the scholars of environmental justice who followed him. Karl Polanyi, and Nancy Fraser after him, saw the same thing and declined on principle to put a price on it, on the ground that treating nature and people as things with prices was the source of the trouble. I don't claim they would approve of what this chapter does. I count the cost so that it can be charged to the party that caused it. I am not proposing that anything be sold.

So what is added? Three things, and they are narrower than a new theory.

The first is a figure at the level of the single act: what one ton or one barrel owes, with the lost access and the continuing damage in the same sum.

The second is the subtraction. The damage totals were already there. What was missing was the comparison, unit by unit, between what was owed and what was paid, with the payers named. That comparison is where this chapter's main finding comes from. Where a bill is being paid at all, most of it is being paid by the public.

The third is a reserve posted in advance against that unpaid figure. The cases in this chapter all look backward. They price extraction that has already happened and read the receipts. A reserve sized ahead of time has not yet been tried on a real case, and I don't want to suggest otherwise.

One tool remains to be handed on. Much of what this chapter has priced can't be undone, and that changes how a decision should be made when the facts are uncertain. In 1951 the statistician Leonard Savage proposed a rule for choosing when the odds can't be known: look at how much you would regret each choice if it turned out wrong, and take the choice whose worst regret is smallest. Apply that rule to extraction. If we leave a resource in the ground and later learn we could safely have used it, we have lost some time, and the resource is still there. If we take it and later learn we should not have, nothing brings it back. The two regrets are not the same size. This book calls the result the Asymmetric Regret Rule: when you can't tell whether a loss can ever be made good, treat it as though it can't. The rule does not replace ordinary weighing of costs and benefits where mistakes can be corrected. It is for the cases where they can't. Chapter 7 leans on it, and Chapter 9 applies it to particular decisions.

Everything in this chapter was counted on Earth, where air is free and the oceans dilute what is poured into them, and where the people who pay usually live a long way from the people who gain. Would the same accounts hold in a place where none of that is true? Would a mining crew on an airless rock, who must make every breath they take, need a method like this one, or would the bill simply be in front of them? If the method works only where costs can be hidden, it describes this planet. If it works there too, it describes something more general. That is the next chapter.
