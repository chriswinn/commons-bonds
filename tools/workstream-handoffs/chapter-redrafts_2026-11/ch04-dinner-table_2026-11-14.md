# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 4: The Existence Proof

---

Three days before Christmas in 1969, a drilling crew in the North Sea found oil.

The crew was working a section of Norway's seabed that the maps called Block 2/4. Late December is a hard time to work in the North Sea. The light comes up late and gray, and by the middle of the afternoon it is gone. The water under the platform is cold water from the Atlantic, and it runs in long ridges, because the waves have had the whole width of the sea to build. The work above the water is steel and noise. A long string of pipe turns down through the seabed, one section at a time, and men handle that pipe in the wind, dressed for weather that does not care that it is almost Christmas. Crews had been drilling test wells on the Norwegian shelf for years by then. Three days before the holiday, the well on Block 2/4 came back with the news that there was oil. The field was named Ekofisk.

Ekofisk turned out to be one of the largest petroleum reservoirs the industrialized world had ever found. The country that owned the seabed above it was small. Norway had fewer than 4 million people in 1969, and Statistics Norway counts only about 5.6 million today. They lived along a cold Atlantic coast, and much of their economy was built on what they could pull from that water. Not one of them had ever produced a barrel of oil.

Every place that has made a find of that size has had to make the same decision afterward, usually within ten years. On the day the discovery is confirmed, one thing is already certain, which is that something very valuable is going to come up out of the ground. Two things are not certain. Who will get the value? And who will carry the costs of bringing it up? The rock does not answer either question. Institutions answer them, and people either build those institutions or fail to build them.

---

What usually happens next has happened so many times that it can be written down in advance. The country spends the windfall. Some places spend it fast and badly, on imports, on favors for political allies, and on showpiece projects that are left to rust. Other places spend it slowly and carefully, on schools and roads and clinics. But they still spend it. The seabed took a hundred million years to store that oil, and one generation uses up all of it.

Even the careful places tend to end the same way. The Canadian province of Alberta set up a savings fund, the Heritage Savings Trust Fund, and put a share of its oil royalties into it. Then the price of oil fell, and the province took money out of the fund to cover the shortfall. It did the same thing in one downturn after another, so the fund never grew to anything like the size of Norway's. A fund that is raided in every bad year is about the best that usually happens. What usually happens is that the windfall is simply spent and gone.

Norway chose not to spend most of its windfall, and almost no other place has made that choice. Norwegians argued the choice out in public and wrote it down. Then they enforced it for five decades. The rules they used were built for one purpose above all the others, which was to take away from every future government the power to undo the choice. Because all of it was done in the open, Norway is the place where the engineering can be inspected.

---

A child born in Norway this morning owns, from the first breath, a share of a fund worth more than $2 trillion. Take the value the fund itself reports (NBIM Annual Report 2025) and divide it by Statistics Norway's count of the population. The share comes to roughly $390,000 for every Norwegian alive, and that includes the newborns. The newborn did nothing to earn it. The child was simply born after a decision that other people made.

The fund was built in pieces, and each piece was built in public. In 1990 Norway's parliament, which is called the Storting, established what was then named the Government Petroleum Fund. The first deposit went in six years later, in 1996. In 2006 the fund was renamed the Government Pension Fund Global, and that is still its name.

Money reaches the fund from three sources. The first is a tax on the private companies that produce oil and gas on the shelf. The Norwegian Petroleum Directorate and the Finance Ministry put that tax near 78 percent at the margin, which means 78 cents of each additional dollar of profit. The second source is the production licenses the state holds for itself. The third is the state's ownership stake in Equinor, the national oil company, which was called Statoil until 2018. Add the three together, and the Norwegian state keeps roughly 70 to 80 cents of every dollar of net petroleum wealth that comes up from its seabed.

The fund invests every krone of that money outside Norway. It buys stocks, bonds and real estate spread across the world. There are two reasons. Invested abroad, the oil wealth grows along with the output of everyone else's economy. And kept out of Norway, it does not flood a small economy with more money than that economy can absorb. The fund's capital is never spent. What may be spent is set by a rule that was adopted in 2001. The rule capped each year's withdrawal at the return the fund was expected to earn after inflation, which in 2001 was put at 4 percent. In 2017 the rule was revised, and it was revised downward, to 3 percent. Spending rules almost never move in that direction. As the fund grew, the temptation to spend it grew too, and Norway answered by making the rule tighter.

All of the numbers behind this are large. Through 2024 the Norwegian shelf has produced roughly 55 billion barrels of oil equivalent, a measure that counts the gas along with the oil. The Norwegian Offshore Directorate estimates that this is about 57 percent of everything the shelf will ever produce. And in recent years the fund has earned more from its investments than the state has earned from the oil and gas themselves (NBIM). In those years Norway made more money from owning pieces of the world's economy than from the oil that had paid for those pieces.

A rule on paper is easy to break. So Norway surrounded the fund with arrangements whose only job is to make the rule hard to break. They are easy to skip past, and they are most of the reason the fund has lasted. The fund is run by Norges Bank Investment Management, which is kept at a distance from political direction. A public ethics council reviews what the fund owns, and when it decides the fund should sell something, that decision is made in the open. Four times a year the managers publish an accounting of everything. Any citizen, journalist or opposition politician can pick up that document, so if the rules were broken it would be visible within three months. Changing the structure itself takes a supermajority of the Storting, which is more than a simple majority of its members. A government that wanted to raid the fund would have to do it in public, slowly, and against its own parliament.

---

The arrangement has held since the first deposit in 1996. In that time it has come through crashes in the price of oil, changes of government, popular pressure to spend the money, and pressure from the industry to pump faster. Every administration since, from every party, has treated the fund as something close to untouchable. Parties of the center-left and the center-right agree on it, and in most countries those parties agree on very little. Each generation of Norwegian politicians has now done the same unusual thing. It has inherited the largest fortune in its country's history. The chance to spend that fortune has never been more than one election away. And each generation has handed the fortune on to the next one larger than it found it. Why?

It did not happen because of something in the Norwegian character. In the early 1970s, when the find was still new, Norwegian policymakers studied the countries that had struck oil before them. They saw two ways it had gone wrong. In the first, the windfall is used up within a generation. The boom buys imports and brings inflation, and when the price of oil turns, nothing is left. In the second, the wealth is captured by the country's own elites or by foreign oil companies, and the people who live over the resource are left with the costs. In 1971 the Storting wrote down a list of principles, and Norwegians have called them the ten oil commandments ever since. The principles put the resource under national management. They committed the country to developing it at a deliberate pace. And they said its benefits should go to the whole society and to the generations after it. The fund came two decades later. It was the instrument that could carry those principles out, and it was designed to prevent both failures at once. So far it has.

---

Follow one barrel of Norwegian crude oil out into the world, and it ends up in places that are easy to picture. Some of it becomes the clear tubing and molded fittings on a surgical tray, which are the medical-grade plastics no hospital can run without. Some of it becomes the kerosene in the wing tank of an airliner. Some becomes asphalt on a road, or lubricant in a gearbox, or one of the specialty chemicals that are needed long before carbon fiber and a thousand other industrial products can be made.

That barrel raises three questions. What did Norway keep from it? What would an honest price for it have counted? And how much of the difference between the two is still unpaid?

Start with what Norway kept, because no other country has kept as much. Divide the fund by every barrel the shelf has produced, and the fund holds about $36 for each barrel. Now compare that with the rent those barrels earned. Rent here means the income from the oil above what it cost to produce. That rent comes to roughly $32 to $36 a barrel. So once the fund's investment returns are counted, it holds about as much as all the rent the oil ever earned. It holds that money in permanent trust for the people who own the seabed and for the Norwegians who come after them. This book has a name for value that is set aside in that way, against the using up of something held in common. The name is an accountability bond. No community anywhere that lives on extraction has posted a bond this large.

An economist had described half of this before Norway built it. In 1977 John Hartwick showed that a society can keep its standard of living steady even as a resource runs out, if it takes the rents from the resource and invests them in capital that lasts (*American Economic Review* 67, 1977). The fund is Hartwick's rule carried out by a whole nation. But his result depends on an assumption. It assumes that capital can stand in for the resource. Where capital cannot stand in for it, no investment policy fully makes up for what is gone. The fund gives Norway the money to pay for repair, to pay for substitutes, and to compensate whoever the damage finally lands on. It does not bring back a barrel that has been burned.

So what would an honest price have counted? It would have counted the full value of what was used up, to everyone the oil would ever have served. That is more than any one generation receives. The value has three parts, and each part can be seen in the objects the barrel became. The surgical tray and the wing tank show the first part, which is the access that people in the future lose. Nobody can yet make a substitute for medical-grade plastics or jet kerosene at industrial volume. A future that needs them and cannot get them is paying for this barrel, and the harder a use is to replace, the more this part weighs. The asphalt, the lubricants and the specialty chemicals show the second part, which is the damage that continues after the platform is gone. The third part is the molded fitting that nobody has designed yet. It stands for every use this chemistry might have served that no one alive has imagined, and that use is lost for good the moment the barrel burns. The Technical Appendix builds this account formally, and it calls the total residual commons value. Subtract the bond from that total, and what remains is cost severance. In plain terms, take the full value that was used up and subtract what was actually set aside. The remainder was quietly passed to people who live somewhere else or who have not been born yet.

Only one piece of that honest price can be counted with confidence today, and it is the carbon. Burning a barrel of oil puts about 0.37 tons of carbon dioxide into the air. The United States Environmental Protection Agency puts the damage done by one ton at $190. Multiply the two, and the carbon cost is about $70 a barrel. Norway holds about $36 a barrel against it. So at least about $34 a barrel is unpaid. Across 55 billion barrels, that comes to about $1.9 trillion, which is about as large as the fund itself. This figure is a floor, meaning the true number can only be higher. It counts the carbon and nothing else. It leaves out the tray, the wing tank and the fitting nobody has designed.

The fund does not pay that bill, and it was never built to pay it. The fund is rent that Norway saved for later generations of Norwegians. The oil and gas from the shelf were burned all over the world for half a century, and the carbon from them is now in the one atmosphere that everyone shares. Most of the cost of that carbon falls on people outside Norway. No way of handling the rent would have paid them. Closer to Norway, the platforms have disturbed the North Sea's ecosystems where they stand. And the barrel is gone. It is true that substitutes get better over time, and that people who confidently predicted permanent scarcity have often been proved wrong. But a fund makes money grow. It does not rebuild a physical resource. If the future needs something that only that chemistry could provide, it does not get it back because the proceeds were invested well.

So Norway does not prove that extraction has been priced correctly anywhere. Its unpaid cost is large, and most of that cost lands on people who had no say in the matter. What Norway proves is narrower, and nobody had shown it before. The rent from something held in common can be kept whole for the people it belongs to, including the ones who are not yet born, and a country can do that with rules it writes for itself and then keeps. Oil is the non-renewable case, because every barrel that is taken is gone. A renewable commons is different. The oyster on my home water is one, and it severs a cost only when the harvest is larger than what can grow back.

---

McDowell County, West Virginia, is about as different from the Ekofisk field as a place can be, and the objection that starts there is a fair one. The objection says Norway was a special case. Norway had strong institutions before the oil came. It had low corruption and a public administration its citizens trusted. Its politics run on consensus, and its level of social trust is one of the highest ever measured. McDowell County had none of that. The Niger Delta had none of that. Most places where something is dug or pumped out of the ground have none of that.

Every word of the objection is true, and it supports the argument instead of defeating it. Cost severance is a mechanism. Value is taken out of a place, and the costs are passed to communities that have little power to refuse them. A mechanism like that works most easily exactly where the things Norway had are missing. It works where institutions are weak, where workers have nowhere else to go, and where a community's ability to demand an accounting was never built or was taken apart. It goes where the defenses are weakest. So it makes no sense to fault the one place with strong defenses for not showing the damage.

Think of the miner in Chapter 2. The people of McDowell County did not deserve less than Norwegians. The companies that mined their coal were not unusually malicious. They were ordinary firms working in a place where the usual means of holding a company to account were absent, and that absence did the damage. Every condition for severance was present. The value went one way and the costs stayed. The harm did not show itself for years. And a county with only one industry had no way to refuse. McDowell was also missing the fund. The money its coal brought in was spent as it arrived, so there is no reserve anywhere that could pay for repair, for substitutes or for compensation.

The communities most exposed to this mechanism are the ones with the weakest defenses. If such places have to wait until they grow Norway's advantages on their own, they will wait forever. But Norway's defenses did not grow on their own either. People whose names are known built them, in years that are known, against real pressure. So what does the same oil look like in a place where nobody built them?

---

On 10 November 1995, the Nigerian state hanged Ken Saro-Wiwa and eight other leaders of the Ogoni people. The Ogoni homeland is in the Niger Delta, and it sits above the oil. Nine men were hanged in one day. The next day, 11 November, Nigeria was suspended from the Commonwealth. The suspension lasted four years, and in 1999 Nigeria was let back in. The oil kept moving on the day of the hangings. It kept moving on the day of the suspension, through the four years that followed, and in every year since. The arrangement that the Ogoni Nine died inside went on after them without any change. Production continued, and the communities went on carrying what production left behind. The hangings cost Nigeria four years of Commonwealth membership; they cost the arrangement nothing.

Nigeria has been producing oil since the 1950s. Its first commercial find was at Oloibiri in 1958, which was eleven years before the strike on Block 2/4. Over roughly six decades, the oil under the Niger Delta has brought the Nigerian government hundreds of billions of dollars in revenue. NEITI, the Nigerian body that publishes what the oil and mining industries pay the government, puts the total near $583 billion since 1960. That figure is only the government's share. It is not the full value of the oil. Nigeria's resource was comparable to Norway's. The number of years was comparable, and the oil was sold into the same world market. But the architecture was the opposite, and so was everything that followed from it.

Norway built a fund. In Nigeria the value left the country. It moved out through international oil companies into financial systems far from Nigeria. Some of it left through a practice called transfer pricing. One part of a company sells to another part of the same company, and the company chooses the price so that the profit shows up wherever taxes are lowest. Taxes were never lowest in the Delta. Some of the value ended up in secrecy jurisdictions. These are places whose laws are written so that money can arrive and no outsider can find out who owns it. Economists have measured both of these. Gabriel Zucman's *The Hidden Wealth of Nations* (2015) counts the wealth that is held in tax havens, out of reach of the countries it came from. Léonce Ndikumana and James Boyce, in *Africa's Odious Debts* (2011), measure the capital that has fled African countries, and Nigeria is among them. <!-- Insertion M, cut to two sentences (map; style rule 24). The terms on which the oil was taken were older than the country itself. They came from concession agreements written before independence, when Nigeria was not yet a country and had no standing to negotiate them. After independence Nigeria inherited those terms largely unchanged. Some of the money did reach the Nigerian state, and much of that was captured by elites. The corruption prosecutions of the decades since are the record of it. This is not a story about Nigeria failing by itself. It is a story about an international architecture. Nigeria's weak institutions were one part of that architecture, and the other parts were designed, operated and defended in other countries.

The people who live in the Delta carry what is left, and it is in the land they live on. Oil has been spilling there for decades. It has moved through the mangrove creeks and into the farmland. Crude oil has worked into the sediment where the fish begin their lives and into the soil that people farm. The people there live with chronic illness from the spilled crude, from the gas that is burned off at the wellheads, and from heavy metals. Fishing and farming had supported these communities for generations. Both collapsed, and the communities came to depend on compensation payments. Those payments often went unpaid, or someone else captured them before they arrived. And the whole arrangement is surrounded by political violence that is tied to the oil.

The most careful outside accounting of any part of this is an environmental assessment that the United Nations Environment Programme published in 2011. It covers Ogoniland, which is one region of the Delta. UNEP found the contamination severe enough to recommend a restoration fund that would start at about $1 billion. It projected that full restoration would take 25 to 30 years of sustained work. The years since have not kept to that schedule, and monitoring has found the cleanup running well behind it. Ogoniland is also the only place where an assessment that careful was done at all. The contamination across the rest of the Delta covers hundreds of square kilometers of creeks and farmland, and it has never been measured even that closely.

Now do for the Delta the same accounting that was done for Norway. The value that was used up is enormous. It is six decades of production from one of the world's great oil regions. The amount that reached the communities who bore the costs rounds to zero. No bond was posted. Nothing was held in reserve anywhere that could pay for repair. So the severed cost is close to the whole of the value.

Two countries found comparable oil and sold it into the same market. In one of them, the rent stayed with the people who own the seabed. In the other, the rent left and the damage stayed. Geology did not decide that. The market did not decide it, and no law of economics did either. Cost severance is a policy choice. The architecture that stands between a resource and the people who live over it is something people engineer, and anything that has been engineered once can be engineered somewhere else.

Suppose an accounting treated all extraction as a disaster, always and everywhere. It would be useless for these two cases. It would describe Norway and Nigeria in the same words and prescribe the same thing for both. It would be wrong about each of them, in opposite directions. The accounting in this book can tell them apart. For Norway it shows the rent kept and a carbon bill unpaid. For Nigeria it shows almost nothing kept and almost everything unpaid. The geology is the same and the commodity is the same. So the difference comes from the one thing a society can actually change, which is the architecture of accountability between the resource and the people. That is why this accounting can be used to make policy, and is not only a moral complaint.

---

One objection is left, and it is the strongest one. It says that Norway is far away. Norway is small, rich and homogeneous. Its people trust each other, and its state works. Whatever Norway proves about Norwegians, the objection says, it proves nothing about anywhere else. It could never work here, wherever here happens to be.

Consider Alaska first. Alaska is a US state that is not known for its warmth toward collective ownership. In 1976 its voters ratified an amendment to the state constitution by roughly three votes to one. Governor Jay Hammond had championed the amendment, and it is now Article IX, Section 15 of the Alaska Constitution. It directs at least 25 percent of the state's oil and mineral royalties into a permanent fund. The constitution itself protects the fund's principal, so the principal cannot be spent. Since 1982 the fund has paid a dividend to every resident of Alaska, every year. It has done so for more than 40 years, through booms and busts and through administrations of both parties. The 2024 dividend was $1,702, and the fund stands near $89 to 90 billion in 2026 (Alaska Permanent Fund Corporation). That is resource rent captured for everyone, written into a constitution and ratified by voters, and it is inside the United States.

Chattanooga, Tennessee, is the second case. The city has owned its electric utility, EPB, since 1935, and it was EPB's crews who strung fiber-optic line across the city. By about 2010 the utility could offer gigabit internet service everywhere it served. That was ahead of Google Fiber, which lit its first city, Kansas City, in 2012. The network was paid for with roughly $111 million in 2009 federal stimulus money and with bonds the city issued. It serves on the order of 125,000 customers. It has run at a profit since about 2015, and today it sells service at speeds up to 10 gigabits (EPB; FCC 2015 preemption record). The private companies that already sold internet service in the area did not welcome any of this. Comcast sued in 2008, before the network launched. The fight over whether EPB could expand went all the way to a Federal Communications Commission preemption proceeding in 2015. The network was built anyway, and it was turning a profit while the lawsuits were still going on.

The third case is Mondragón. In 1956, in that Basque town, a priest named José María Arizmendiarrieta helped found a workshop. That workshop grew into the largest federation of worker cooperatives in the world. By the federation's own reporting it has roughly 80,000 worker-owners across some 95 cooperatives, and they compete in ordinary industrial markets. A rule caps pay inside the cooperatives. Historically the highest-paid person earned around 3 times what the lowest-paid person earned. Even in the large industrial cooperatives the ratio is roughly 6 to 9 to 1. At large American firms the ratio runs past 200 to 1. In 2013 Fagor, one of the federation's flagship cooperatives, went bankrupt. The federation did not turn Fagor's workers out. It took most of them into the other cooperatives. Some people believe worker ownership survives only in small specialty businesses, and they have seven decades at competitive scale to account for.

The fourth case is Vienna. In 1919, in the years remembered as Red Vienna, the city began building municipal apartment blocks called Gemeindebauten. People have been living around their courtyards for a century. Today about 62 percent of Vienna's roughly 1.9 million residents live in housing that is owned by the city or by non-profit cooperatives. That is well over 400,000 apartments, and the city itself owns some 220,000 of them (City of Vienna). Part of the money comes from a dedicated housing contribution of about 1 percent of payroll. The rest comes from progressive taxation and subsidized federal loans, and the system sustains itself. It has lasted through every change of government, through Austria joining the European Union in 1995, and through the crisis years of 2008 to 2013. Nothing about it looks like housing for the poor. It is where much of working Vienna lives, by choice, and has lived for a hundred years.

These cases work at four different scales: a nation, a US state, a municipality and a firm. They cover four different kinds of commons: a resource, an infrastructure, labor and housing. Whatever explains Norway now has to explain Alaska, Chattanooga, Mondragón and Vienna as well. An explanation that covers all of them can't be about what is special in Scandinavia. It is something plainer. It is architecture that people built on purpose and defended over time, in places that look nothing like each other.

---

The United States built one of these architectures too, and the year it was built explains nearly everything about its shape. Social Security was designed in 1935, in the middle of the Depression. It was designed for old people who were destitute at that moment, that winter, and not at some distant date on an actuary's chart. The design that could reach them right away was pay-as-you-go. Payroll taxes are collected from people who are working now, and the money goes almost at once to people who are retired now. Later the system collected more than it paid out. Those surpluses were lent to the Treasury, and in return the system received special bonds that cannot be sold on any market. So the trust fund is a record of promises the government has made to itself. Nothing in it grows along with any economy outside the United States, and the United States is the economy that will eventually have to make the promises good.

Now put that beside the Norwegian design. Norway takes a stream of oil rents that will one day end, and it compounds that money in capital invested outside its own economy. The United States takes a stream of payroll taxes that keeps coming, and it passes that money from one generation to another as it arrives. Neither design is dishonorable. They differ because of the circumstances each country was in, and not because one country was more virtuous. Norway did its designing in the 1970s and 80s. It had a windfall to invest, and there was a mature global financial system to invest it in. The United States did its designing in 1935. It had no windfall, and what it could do was set up a transfer program that put checks in old people's hands without delay. A design with a real fund behind it was not unimaginable in 1935. Germany's old-age and disability insurance, which Bismarck set up in 1889, was partly funded. It stayed partly funded until the hyperinflation of the early 1920s destroyed the fund, so funded designs carry risks of their own. None of this says the United States should adopt Norway's design. The claim is narrower than that. The architecture is a choice. No arrangement of who gets the value and who bears the cost across generations came about by itself. Someone engineered each one, under the constraints of its date, and what was engineered at one date can be engineered again at another. It is easiest to see that something was a choice when a second design is set beside it, and that is most of what Norway is for in this book.

Chapter 3 ended on a dock in Hampton, Virginia, with a question for Oslo. Could the instrument that was built for the oil be built, in some adapted form, for the oyster? My father grew up at Fox Hill among the sons of watermen; the men who taught him boats and weather and the meaning of the channel's smell in August were men whose working lives were the water that question is about. The family is one generation off the boats and still inside the community that worked them. That standing is the reason the question gets asked here with the oil's seriousness, and not as an analogy passing through. <!-- PROTECTED: the three sentences on the author's father and family are carried word for word from the current chapter. Only the author can change them. -->

Part of the answer now has a number. Chapter 3 put a price on what was taken from the Chesapeake reef. Restoring it would cost at least $20 to 37 million, and the central estimate is near $211 million. It is the one case in this book where a cost that has already been imposed is fully computed. So what was destroyed has a stated price. The question Norway adds is about the future. The oyster is the renewable case. Its cost was severed when the harvest became larger than what the reefs could rebuild, and the Bay's record shows that this went on for a century. When the harvest stays within what the reefs can rebuild, nothing is severed. A living commons makes more of itself as long as people take no more than grows back. So could the rents from a living commons be captured and held the way Norway holds the rents from its oil, so that the next century of the fishery is not priced at zero? That might be the restitution instrument running forward in time, or it might be a different instrument altogether. Chapter 3 raised the question, and this chapter leaves it open. Norway settles only one thing about it, which is that instruments of this kind can be built and kept.

---

Norway proves the thing can be built. It does not show every place how to build it, and there are four things it has not priced.

The first is the barrel itself. The fund saves the rent. Money in a fund can pay for repair, and it can pay for substitutes where substitutes exist. But suppose a future use turns out to need petroleum's particular chemistry, and nothing else will do. No amount of compounding brings the oil back. Norway has saved what the barrel earned. It has not replaced the barrel.

The second is the carbon, and it begins with people who will never receive a krone from the fund. They are the farmers of the Bangladesh delta, who work land that the sea claims more of each decade. They are the herders and farmers at the margin of the Sahel, where the rains have stopped keeping their old schedule. They are the people of the low-lying Pacific island states, whose entire national territories stand barely above the tide. The carbon from the shelf's oil and gas did not stay in the North Sea. It is in the one atmosphere, and its costs land hardest on these people. The fund holds more than $2 trillion in trust for 5.6 million people. The carbon falls on hundreds of millions who are not among them, and no part of Norway's architecture reaches them. Closer to the platforms, the North Sea's disturbed ecosystems recover slowly, where they recover at all.

The third is scale. The fund works because it compounds on the output of the rest of the world's economy. That means the solution needs an outside to invest in. If every country that extracts a resource built a fund like this tomorrow, there would not be enough outside economies to absorb all of them. What Norway built is a real solution for one country. The planet cannot adopt it by copying it. The global version of the problem is still unsolved, and it is the version that matters most.

The fourth is the hardest to see. The fund turns the oil's value today into money. It does not put a price on what the oil would have been worth if it had been left in the ground, for uses that nobody has imagined yet. Norway priced the present well. Pricing the future on purpose is a different problem, and nobody has solved it.

These four gaps are work that still has to be done. They are not a flaw in the proof. Norway proves that a partial solution exists. What remains is to close the gaps in two kinds of places. Some places already have Norway's foundations. Others have to build the foundations first, which is harder and more important, because the money to build them would come from the extraction economy, and that economy will resist.

---

There were two strikes, eleven years apart. One was at Oloibiri in 1958. The other was on Block 2/4, three days before Christmas, in 1969. The industry was the same, the hydrocarbons were the same, and the same world market was buying. Above one strike, a country wrote rules that keep the rent and limit how fast it can be spent. A fishing nation of fewer than 4 million people became the holder of a fund worth more than $2 trillion. Above the other strike there was no bond and no reserve. Six decades of production left the people of the Delta with the contamination, the flares, the broken fisheries, and a cleanup that is still running behind a schedule set in 2011. Neither outcome was inevitable. People engineered both of them, in years that can be named, under pressures that were real in both places.

So the choice has now been watched going both ways. The mechanism that takes value out of a place and leaves the costs behind can be interrupted. One country interrupted it, and has kept it interrupted for more than 30 years. Norway did not finish the job. Its carbon bill is unpaid, and the people that bill falls on live a long way from Oslo. What would it take to finish the job, in Norway and everywhere else? The remaining chapters take up that question. The next one starts much closer to home, with one family, in a house in Connecticut.

The drill came up three days before Christmas carrying the same news it carries everywhere. What happened next was a decision. It always is.
