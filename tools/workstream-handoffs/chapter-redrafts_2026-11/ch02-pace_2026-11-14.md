# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 2: The Miner

---

In May of 1960 a young senator drove through McDowell County, West Virginia. He had come for votes. A primary election was on, West Virginia was a poor state, and McDowell was the poorest part of it. He drove the hollows, where the road ran beside the creek because there was nowhere else to put a road.

He saw children standing in doorways with the swollen bellies of the underfed. He saw the surplus cheese and flour the government handed out, stacked in cabins, because there was no work and no money and nothing else coming in. He saw men sitting on porches in the middle of a working day. Some of those men had carried rifles in a war and come home to a country that had won it. Under their feet lay the richest coal on the continent, and they had cut it out of the ground with their own bodies. Now they sat.

McDowell's coal was metallurgical coal, the hard, hot-burning kind that steel mills cannot run without. It went into the rails, the ships, the bridges and the cities. At its peak the county shipped more of it than anywhere else in the United States. Measured by what came out of the ground, McDowell was rich. Measured by how the people who dug it lived, it was the poorest place in one of the poorest states in the country.

Four months later, in September, Senator John F. Kennedy stood at a podium in Canton, Ohio, and described what he had seen. He named the want, the idle men and the children, and he did not exaggerate any of it, because he had seen it with his own eyes. Then he laid the blame where a candidate lays it: on his opponent, on the administration in power, on a policy that had failed.

He had the suffering right. He had the wrong defendant.

No party and no policy had robbed the men on those porches. What emptied them out was an arrangement so ordinary that it had no name. It took the value of the coal they dug and sent it up and out of the county, and it left the cost of the digging behind, in their lungs, their creeks and their children. Kennedy was looking at the result of that arrangement. He had no way to point at the arrangement itself, and neither did anyone else. So the chair where a defendant should have been sitting stayed empty. What belongs in it?

---

The last chapter ended with a ledger and a set of empty columns. The first column belongs to McDowell, and the question that fills it is a small one. What did a ton of that coal actually cost?

The price is easy to find, because it is in the records. In 1960 the coal sold at the mouth of the mine for just under five dollars a ton. That is the number the buyer paid the seller, and it is the number most people mean when they say what something costs. But the price is not the cost. The cost is the price plus everything the price left out, the bill that never came.

In the last chapter I said I had options a coal miner does not have. This is the miner I meant. I had skills that several employers wanted and the freedom to move between them. He had a hole in the ground, a county with no other work in it, and a body the work would use up. A constrained choice is still a choice. It is not a free one. So I want to set down what his choice cost, one column at a time, the way you would read a bill you did not know you had run up.

---

The first column is written in a man's lungs.

Robert Bailey worked underground. Coal dust came off the coal face and off the machines that cut it, fine enough to ride the air into the deep tissue of the lung, where the body cannot clear it. The body walls the dust off with scar tissue, and the scarring spreads until the lung can no longer do the one thing a lung does. Doctors call it pneumoconiosis. Miners call it black lung. When Bailey tried to say what it was like, he said it was almost like drowning.

Black lung is not fast the way a wound is fast. It wears a man down the way water wears down stone. In the end Bailey needed a double lung transplant, two new lungs in place of the two the coal had filled. He got one, and it kept him alive on drugs that stop the body from rejecting what is not its own.

The company Bailey had worked for went bankrupt. When a company goes bankrupt its corporate shell dissolves, and the obligations inside the shell dissolve with it. One of them was the company's duty to the man whose lungs it had used. But a debt that real does not disappear. It moved onto a federal fund, which means it moved onto the public: people who never dug the coal, never bought the steel, and never sat in the room where someone decided to mine it cheaply. Bailey's body carried the disease, the public carried the bill, and the company carried neither, because by then it was a name on a closed file.

Bailey testified before Congress twice about the backlog of black lung claims, about the men who waited and died while their paperwork moved through the system. He said that most laws have been written with blood.

The history of the coalfields bears him out. When black lung was still being denied as a real disease, miners across the coalfields walked out in a wildcat strike in 1968 to force the question, and the Black Lung Association carried their demand into law. <!-- FACT FLAG 2026-11-14: "1968" is the current chapter's date. They won real things. What they did not have was the power to change the arrangement itself. A miner can win a strike and still have no way to put a price on a lung.

Bailey died in February of 2019, twenty-eight days after his last interview. The cancer that killed him was brought on, in part, by the drugs that had kept his transplanted lungs alive. Coal took his lungs, and then the medicine that replaced them helped take the rest of him. No invoice has a column for that.

Bailey is not a number, and I will not turn him into one. He is the reason the numbers matter.

Black lung is not a disease of the past. It is rising again, and rising fastest among younger miners, men with fewer than twenty years underground, because the newer machines cut faster and throw a finer and more dangerous dust. In the coalfields of central Appalachia, in Kentucky, Virginia and West Virginia, around one in five miners with twenty-five years or more underground has it. Each man who dies of it loses between eight and thirteen years of the life he would otherwise have had, and he loses them at the end, when there are fewest left.

The federal program that pays black lung benefits paid out around $44 billion through 2009. That is a floor, because no trustworthy total exists for the years since, and the payments have continued. The fund behind the program has run in debt almost every year since 1979, and in September of 2024 the debt stood at $5.1 billion. Between 2014 and 2016 three coal companies went bankrupt: Alpha Natural Resources, Patriot Coal and James River Coal. Together they carried $865 million in black lung obligations to sick and dying men. Those obligations came off the companies and onto the public, the same way Bailey's had.

That is the first column. A ton of coal cost part of a man's lungs. The man paid it with his body, the public paid the bill for the man, the company paid nothing, and none of it appeared in the price of just under five dollars.

---

The second column runs orange in the creeks.

Some of the rock that mining cuts into holds sulfur. When that sulfur meets air and water it makes acid, and the acid leaches metals out of the disturbed ground and carries them down into the streams. The creeks run the color of rust, or gray with sediment, and the things that lived in them stop living there. This is acid mine drainage, and it does not stop when the mining stops. It begins when the rock is opened, and it runs for decades after the last miner has left the last mine.

The law says mined land is to be reclaimed: the mines closed out, the drainage treated, the ground brought back toward what it was. The law is right, and it is underfunded. Across the coalfields more than 633,000 acres are waiting on reclamation. Doing the work would cost between $7.5 billion and $9.75 billion. The bonds the companies posted against that cost hold around $3.8 billion. That leaves a gap of $4 billion to $6 billion between what reclamation costs and what the companies set aside to pay for it. Either someone fills that gap or the creeks stay orange.

A ton of coal cost a measure of a watershed. The watershed paid it, and the price did not mention the water.

---

The third column is the town.

Welch had department stores, theaters, hotels and a daily paper. Keystone had a life of its own, and so did Kimball, and so did a town called War. They looked permanent, the way a place does when money is flowing and nobody yet imagines it will stop. The money was coal money.

In the coal camps up the hollows, some of a miner's pay did not come as cash. The camps belonged to the companies. Around Gary, south of Welch, a subsidiary of U.S. Steel built a dozen company towns and mines that were home to 15,000 people. In 1922 almost 80 percent of West Virginia's miners lived in company houses and shopped in company stores, and in Illinois only 10 percent did. In many of those towns a miner could draw against his wages in scrip, a private currency of paper or metal tokens that the coal company issued and then deducted from his pay on payday. Scrip was good for its full face value at the company store. The company would not buy it back for cash. Traders bought it at a discount of 25 percent or more, and nearby independent stores often accepted it at 10 to 30 percent below its face value. Lou Athey, writing in the *West Virginia Encyclopedia*, says scrip "became a means to cycle the value of a miner's labor back to the company." Some miners were handed a pay envelope with a curling line drawn across it. They called that line "the snake," and it meant no wages were due.

Historians disagree about how hard the company stores squeezed. The economist Price Fishback studied the early 1900s and found that prices at company stores were generally similar to prices at nearby independent stores, and that miners were generally not in debt to the store. Take his account as given. The employer still owned the house, the store and the currency in between. A wage paid that way does less to build a town than the same wage paid in cash and spent on a main street, because part of it goes straight back to the company that paid it.

Then the work thinned. The mines mechanized, and a machine cuts coal with a fraction of the men. The men who were no longer needed were no longer paid. The steel mills that had bought the coal began buying it elsewhere, or not at all. The big steel operation that had anchored the county wound down across the 1980s, and more than 1,200 jobs went with it. There was no single announced closing. A shift was cut here and a section closed there, until the work that had been the spine of a town was gone.

The county's income did not collapse all at once. In its worst single year it dropped around 13 percent. From its peak in the mid-1980s it lost around 15 percent over three years, and then it kept falling for forty years, and the people went with it. In 1950 nearly 100,000 people lived in McDowell County. By 2020 fewer than 20,000 did. Four out of five were gone. The stores closed because the people left, the people left because the work was gone, and the work was gone because the coal that remained no longer needed them to dig it.

What is left in Welch, Keystone and Kimball is mostly not ruins. It is quieter than that: empty lots where the stores stood, storefronts with names still faintly readable above doors that nobody opens, and a Main Street with Sunday's traffic on every day of the week.

A ton of coal cost a share of a town's future. The town paid it across forty years, and no single year on any ledger shows the size of it, because the cost was spread thin enough to look like ordinary decline.

---

The fourth column is the hardest to see, because the people who paid it are no longer in McDowell to be counted. They are the county's children, and they left.

Every family raises a child by spending on the child: breakfast cooked at seven in the dark before a shift, cleats for the team, lessons on a secondhand piano, and years of attention, money and worry. In the plainest sense that spending is an investment, a cost paid now against a return later. In most places the return comes back to the place. The child grows up, works there and raises children there, and what the family invested stays and circulates.

In McDowell the child grew up and left, because there was no work to stay for. Everything the family had invested walked out of the county on the child's own two feet and produced its return somewhere else, in a city that had paid nothing to raise that child. So the county spent itself raising the very people who had to leave it to live. That cost does not run orange, and it does not show in a lung. It shows in a county of old people and empty schools.

A ton of coal cost the county its own children, raised at the county's expense for the benefit of everywhere else.

Every column so far lands in McDowell. The next one does not.

---

Most of McDowell's coal was metallurgical coal, bound for a furnace. What goes into a furnace comes out as heat, and as the gas that warms the air for everyone who will live after the burning. That cost lands nowhere near McDowell and nowhere near 1960. It lands on people who are not yet born, and it does not come due in the lifetime of anyone who profited from the ton. The price of the coal said nothing about it. A price is set by the living, and people who have not been born cannot sit at the table where it is set or send a bill back through time.

That is the fifth column, and I am leaving it nearly empty on purpose. A later chapter does nothing but compute what these things cost, and the number belongs there. For now the column sits open on the page, which means the bill is already larger than this chapter can add up.

---

The county's own records show where the first four columns left it. A boy born in McDowell County could expect to live around 63.5 years, against around 76.5 for the United States as a whole. That is a difference of thirteen years. The county's drug-death rate ran around 141 per 100,000 people, against a national rate near 16. As of 2022 the poverty rate stood at 37.6 percent and the median household income at $28,235. When West Virginia's fifty-five counties are ranked by the health of the people in them, McDowell ranks fifty-fifth.

The first food stamps of the modern federal program were issued in Welch, in a 1961 pilot. The first recipient was a man named Alderson Muncy, on May 29. The county that had fed the furnaces of the United States became the place where the United States first tested how to feed the people those furnaces had used.

Numbers this bad need an explanation.

---

By now you have seen the same thing four times, in a lung, a creek, a town and a generation of children. Value is generated in one place, the cost of generating it lands in another, and the two are never reconciled: the cost is never billed back to the place the value went. I call that cost severance. The cost has been severed from the value.

The cost that gets left behind, the lung and the creek and the emptied town and the child who left, is the severed cost. It is real, it is owed, and there is no one to send the invoice to.

Taking the value while leaving the cost is value extraction. It is not value creation, which makes something and is paid for it. Extraction takes what was there and bears none of the cost of the taking. The line between making value and merely taking it is the economist Mariana Mazzucato's. The old pattern of wealth gathered by stripping it from a place that cannot defend its claim is what David Harvey called accumulation by dispossession. What this book adds is the application of that pattern to the physical extraction of a resource from the ground and, in later chapters, a method for putting a number on the severed cost.

Economics has had a name for part of this for a century. Arthur Pigou, writing before any of these mines had reached its peak, named the externality: a cost that a transaction imposes on someone outside it, like the smoke that falls on a neighbor's wash. The word is accurate. Black lung is an externality, and so is an orange creek. But to call a man's drowned lungs an externality is to file the worst thing in this chapter under a clerk's heading, as something off to the side of the transaction. It was never off to the side. Pigou's tradition saw the cost and lacked an instrument for doing anything with it.

Cost severance, severed cost and value extraction are the three terms the rest of this book is built on, and every chapter after this one uses them. I am coining them here, against this county, because this is the place where their meaning is plainest.

---

Why did nobody outside McDowell have to look at any of this?

In the last chapter the man on the rooftops climbed above each new city so he could see its shape: where the river bent, how the old roads ran, which neighborhoods the money flowed to and which it flowed past. You have to get above a place to see its shape. The people living inside it, on their own streets and at their own doors, never do.

McDowell's coal made the same shape in reverse. Its value went up and out, to the mills, the cities, and the accounts that bought the steel. The people on the receiving end rode the rails that steel built, crossed the bridges it framed and lived in the cities it raised, and not one of them ever had to look down on McDowell and see what their steel had cost. The cost stayed in the hollows while the value left them. The people who got the value lived far from the people who paid for it, far enough that they never had to climb above the arrangement and see it whole. Nobody feels the cost of a place they never have to look at.

I call that spatial cost severance. Geography keeps the bill out of sight. The value and the cost end up in different places, and no invoice is ever sent across the distance between them.

Was anyone to blame?

---

A chapter full of dead miners, orange creeks and emptied towns seems to be building toward a villain. The obvious candidate is the coal operator, and the obvious moral is that bad men did bad things.

There may have been bad men. There usually are, somewhere. But the argument does not need them and does not rest on them. Cost severance would have happened with saints in the boardroom, because it does not run on malice. It runs on one fact: the price of the coal had no line for the lung, the creek or the town. A transaction that has no line for a cost does not record that cost. A cost that is not recorded is not paid by the buyer or the seller. So it lands on whoever is standing where it falls. That is a property of the arrangement and not of the character of the men inside it, and it works without anyone intending it.

The opposite objection says the coal built the modern world, the world is worth what it cost, and adding up the bill is a way of wishing the rails and the bridges away. I am not wishing them away, and the question was never whether the coal should have been mined. The question is who should have borne the cost of mining it, and a person can believe the coal was worth mining and still ask why the bill went where it went.

So the indictment Kennedy delivered in Canton stands. The want was real, the suffering was real, and the case was true. Only the name of the defendant changes. It was never the party in power or the policy that failed. What belongs in the empty chair is an instrument that does not exist: a way of pricing the severed cost and sending it back to where the value went. There is no such instrument, and the argument of this book is that it has to be built.

---

None of this is finished, and it was never only about coal. The same county shows it, because the arrangement ran there a second time, in recent decades, with a different product.

When the work left McDowell it left behind what extraction leaves behind: people in pain and out of options, with bodies worn by labor, in a county with no economy to absorb them. The first extraction had taken the value of the coal and left the cost in the place. The second came for what the first had left, and it came as a medicine.

In the late 1990s a company called Purdue Pharma brought an opioid painkiller, OxyContin, to market and sold it hard into places like McDowell, and places like McDowell are where it did the most. It would be easy to say that the company drew a map of the broken places and aimed at them. The record supports something more careful, and more damning. There are two records. One is the record the companies wrote themselves: the internal documents, the settlements, and what was said under oath in litigation. The other is the record that researchers and prosecutors pieced together afterward, from prescriber data, public-health studies and the geography of distress, showing where the pills went and why. No single document confesses to a plan. Put together, the two records show a pattern. The drug found its largest markets where the wage economy had already collapsed, where physical work had left chronic physical pain, where there was little to lose and less to do, and where the medical and economic supports that might have caught the problem had thinned along with everything else. The places the first extraction had hollowed out were the places the second one filled.

The two extractions were different kinds of taking. A coal company at least employed the miner. It paid him a wage, took the value of his labor and left the cost in his lungs, but there was a wage contract in it, an exchange, however lopsided. Purdue did not employ McDowell at all. It found the wreckage the wage economy had left and made a market of it by selling into people's pain. Nancy Fraser calls this expropriation, which is accumulation by means other than the wage contract. By her term the second extraction was the purer case, because no wage contract disguised the taking.

Unlike the coal, this part of the story has guilty pleas in it, and the amounts are in the record. The Sackler family, which owned Purdue, drew something on the order of $11 billion out of the company. In 2007 Purdue pleaded guilty to a federal felony of misbranding and paid $600 million, and three of its executives, Friedman, Udell and Goldenheim, pleaded guilty to misdemeanor counts. In 2020 the company pleaded guilty again, to three federal felony counts. The largest drug distributors, McKesson, Cardinal Health and AmerisourceBergen, had moved pills into counties whose populations could not have consumed them honestly, and in 2022 they agreed to a settlement of around $21 billion. Set those figures beside McDowell's drug-death rate of around 141 per 100,000. The value left places like McDowell as money. The cost stayed in them as the dead. The two were never on the same ledger until courts forced them there, decades late.

The second cycle matters beyond its own facts. If the pattern in McDowell had been about coal, it would have ended when the coal did. Instead the same shape arrived in a different industry and ran the same way: value out, cost left behind, and no instrument to make the two meet. It will keep arriving, in whatever the next product is, for as long as that instrument does not exist. It is a fact about how things are priced, and it will run on anything priced the way the coal was.

---

The water I grew up on is a different place with the same defendant.

Chesapeake watermen go out before light and come back with what the bay gives, and what it gives has been thinning. The cause is costs the bay has been made to carry that nobody ever put on an invoice: the nutrients that run off the land, the sediment, the warming, the long slow loading of the water with the byproducts of everyone upstream. All of it lands on the oyster, the crab and the men who chase them, while the value of the upstream activity stays inland and out of their reach. The structure is the same as in the hollows, and so is the missing instrument.

One difference between the two places matters. Coal is finite. What McDowell lost, it lost for good, because a mountain does not refill. A bay can keep making what is taken from it, oyster after oyster and crab after crab, generation after generation, as long as the rate of taking stays under the rate of making. So the severance on the bay is conditional. It arrives only when the taking outruns the making, and it can be held off, even reversed, if the instrument McDowell never had is built in time. McDowell has been emptied. The bay still could be saved.

I have not said a word against the miners, and I am not going to. A man who cut metallurgical coal out of a mountain with his body did a hard, skilled and necessary thing, and he did it well. The pride he took in it was real, and he had earned it. Nothing in this chapter is a verdict on the men. The men were the best of it. The verdict is on an arithmetic: a way of pricing a ton of coal that had no line for the lung or the creek or the town or the child, that sent the value up and out and left the cost at home, and that could not, in its own terms, do otherwise.

Kennedy saw the suffering and named the wrong defendant. The right defendant is an instrument that does not exist. The rest of this book is the work of building it.
