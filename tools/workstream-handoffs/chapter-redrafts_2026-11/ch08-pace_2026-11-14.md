# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 8: What Things Actually Cost

## The rail car

In 1960 a rail car of coal left McDowell County, West Virginia. Someone bought the coal and someone was paid for it, and the price is still in the records. That year a ton of bituminous coal sold at the mouth of an American mine for an average of $4.71, according to the federal Energy Information Administration. Call it just under five dollars. As far as the market was concerned, the accounting for that ton was finished when the car pulled out.

Chapter 2 read the rest of the bill one column at a time: the miner's lungs, the creeks, the town, the children who left, and the air. It left every amount blank, because naming the columns was enough work for one chapter. So what does the bill come to?

This chapter writes it out for one ton of McDowell coal. Each line names who paid it. On every line I use the lowest figure I can defend, and where the sources give a range I take the bottom of the range. Some of the figures come from outside records and some are my own estimates built on those records, and I say which is which as we go. At the end we add the lines up and set the total beside the $4.71.

## The lungs

The miner paid the first line with his body. Chapter 2 told how, through Robert Bailey: coal dust scars the lungs until they can no longer do their work, and each miner who dies of black lung loses between eight and thirteen years of the life he would otherwise have had.

After the miner, the public paid. The federal black lung program had paid out about $44 billion in benefits through 2009, by the count of the Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Research Service, and there is no reliable total for the years since. The trust fund that pays those benefits has had to borrow to keep paying them, and as of September 2024 it owed $5.1 billion.

Spread that $44 billion across the coal mined in the United States since the program began in 1969, and the benefits alone come to between $1 and $1.50 for every ton. Black lung is only the best-documented part of the health cost. People who lived near the mines and never worked in one breathed the dust too, and they have higher rates of heart and lung disease. When a coal company went bankrupt before its obligations came due, its miners' medical bills moved to Medicare and Medicaid. A boy born in McDowell County can expect to live about thirteen years less than the average American boy.

When I count those costs together with the black lung benefits, I put the health line at between $2 and $4 a ton. That range is my own estimate. Only the black lung figure beneath it comes from an outside record. I take the bottom of the range, which is $2.

## The creeks

The second line is paid by whoever lives downstream. When mining opens rock that holds sulfur, the sulfur meets air and water and makes sulfuric acid, and the acid carries heavy metals out of the broken ground and into the streams. This is called acid mine drainage, and it is why the creeks below old mines run orange. It doesn't stop when the mine closes. It can run for a century or more, and the water has to be treated for as long as it runs.

Since 1977 a federal law, the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act, has required coal companies to post bonds that pay for cleaning up their mines. The bonds were set too low. In 2021 the group Appalachian Voices counted about 633,000 acres of mined land across Appalachia still waiting for cleanup. It estimated the cost of that cleanup at $7.5 billion to $9.8 billion, and it found that the bonds available to pay for it came to about $3.8 billion. That leaves between $4 billion and $6 billion that nobody has set aside.

The damage goes beyond the creeks. Where companies removed whole mountaintops to reach the coal, they pushed the rock into the valleys and buried the streams that ran there. A 2012 study led by Michael Hendryx surveyed people living near those mines and found that they were about twice as likely to report having had cancer as people in similar communities without them. And when the coal was burned, the mercury in it went into the air and came down in the oceans, where it has been measured in fish as far away as the North Atlantic.

For all of that I put the line at between $1 and $3 a ton, and again the range is my own estimate, built on the records above. I take $1. The bill so far is $3.

## The town

The third line was paid by the people who stayed, and by public programs that spend money on their behalf. In 1950 nearly a hundred thousand people lived in McDowell County. By 2020 just over nineteen thousand did. The mines had mechanized, and a machine cuts coal with a fraction of the men. U.S. Steel, the company that had anchored the county, wound its mines down across the 1980s, and about twelve hundred jobs went with them.

The people who remain live in one of the poorest places in the United States. In 2022 the county's median household income was $28,235, in a country where the median was about $67,500. Its poverty rate was 37.6 percent, and it ranked sixteenth-poorest among all the counties in the United States. In 2015 drug deaths there ran at 141 for every 100,000 people, when the national rate was roughly 16.

A county that falls to a fifth of its population still needs schools, a water system, fire protection and a hospital, and it has to keep them running with a fifth of the people to pay for them. Each store that closes takes away a reason for the next one to stay open. Food assistance, disability payments and Medicaid fill part of what is missing, and the state and federal governments pay for those. When the United States began the 1961 pilot that became its modern food stamp program, the very first stamps were handed out in this county, in the town of Welch.

No outside source puts a price per ton on a county's decline, so this figure is mine as well. I estimate it at between $5 and $15 a ton and take $5. Three lines in, the bill stands at $8, which is already more than the $4.71 on the 1960 invoice. The largest line is still to come.

## The air

The largest line will be paid by people who haven't been born yet. Coal is mined to be burned, and burning it puts carbon dioxide into the air, where it warms the climate for everyone who comes afterward. None of those people were at the table when the price was set.

The arithmetic has two steps. The first is how much carbon dioxide one ton of this coal releases. The Environmental Protection Agency's standard factor for bituminous coal is 93.28 kilograms of carbon dioxide for every million British thermal units of heat. The coal of the Pocahontas seam, which is what McDowell County mined, holds about 28 million of those units in every ton, by the figures of the United States Geological Survey and the Energy Information Administration. Multiply the two and a ton of McDowell coal releases about 2.61 metric tons of carbon dioxide. The average American bituminous coal releases less, about 2.32, because it holds less heat.

The second step is what each of those tons of carbon dioxide costs. That figure is not mine, and this book did not produce it. It belongs to the United States government. In 2023 the Environmental Protection Agency estimated the damage done by one metric ton of carbon dioxide at $190, drawing on a study that Kevin Rennert and his colleagues published in the journal *Nature* in 2022. Economists call this figure the social cost of carbon.

Now multiply: 2.61 tons at $190 each comes to $496 for one ton of McDowell coal.

That one line is more than all the others put together, many times over. It is also more than three times the highest price a ton of coal sells for today, which runs from about $40 to about $140 depending on the grade. With the three lines before it, the bill now stands at $504. If I had taken the top of each of my ranges and not the bottom, it would stand at $518.

The carbon figure still leaves something out. Coal that has been burned for heat can't be used for anything else: not for making steel, not as a raw material for chemicals, and not for whatever uses nobody has thought of yet. The economist Harold Hotelling described the root of this in 1931. A resource that can't be renewed carries a cost every time some of it is used, because what is used now is not there to be used later. Hotelling was describing the mine owner's own loss of future income. The loss I mean here falls on later people who never owned the coal. Nor does the carbon figure count most of what warming does to living systems, the kind of loss that Robert Costanza and his colleagues have worked to put a value on. I enter no figure for either one on this bill.

## The lines that are harder to count

Four more lines belong on the bill. They are harder to count than the first four, and no outside source prices any of them by the ton. I have entered each one at a dollar or two. I do that because the costs are real, and leaving them off would enter them at zero, which is the one figure I know to be wrong. Together they come to $6 of the total.

The first of the four is the children. Chapter 2 described how this cost works. A family spends years of money, attention and worry raising a child. In most places the grown child stays nearby, and what the family put in comes back to the place that raised them. In McDowell County the children grew up and left, because there was no work to stay for, and what their families had spent on them went to work in cities that had paid nothing toward it. A family that stayed often had one thing of value to hand down, which was a house in a place where houses had stopped selling. I estimate this line at between $1 and $5 a ton and enter it at $2.

A price of $4.71 shows the world one number and hides everything else on this bill behind it. The word I use for that is masking. I took it from computer security, the field I worked in later in my career, where masking means hiding a true value behind a presented one. For a long time I didn't connect the word to anything older, and it is much older.

In 1896 the poet Paul Laurence Dunbar published "We Wear the Mask." It begins: "We wear the mask that grins and lies, / It hides our cheeks and shades our eyes." Dunbar was writing about the face Black Americans had to show in order to survive, and about what it cost them to hold it in place. In 1903, in *The Souls of Black Folk*, W.E.B. Du Bois described the condition underneath that mask. He called it a veil, and he described "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity." In 1952 Frantz Fanon, in *Black Skin, White Masks*, carried the idea into the colonies and the economies built on them, which is the ground closest to this chapter's. In the same year Ralph Ellison's novel *Invisible Man* gave it a narrator who says, "I am invisible, understand, simply because people refuse to see me."

Those writers understood what a hidden cost does to the people who carry it, and they understood it long before I had a word for it. I want the idea credited to them. What this book adds to their work is a ledger: an amount for each cost, and the name of who paid it.

The second line is knowledge. A mining community knew things that were true only of its own ground. Its people knew how to read a particular seam of coal, how to keep one valley's water system working, and how the creek behaved in a hard winter. That knowledge existed because the same families stayed in the same place long enough to build it up. When they scattered, it went with them to places where it was no use to anyone. The churches, the civic clubs and the neighbors who could carry a family through a bad year went the same way. I enter this line at $1.

The third line is politics. The first lines of this bill went unwritten for decades, and the reason was not that nobody knew. The historian Alan Derickson has shown that doctors could identify black lung as a disease of the work early in the twentieth century. It did not become grounds for federal compensation until 1969, after the Farmington mine disaster of 1968, a strike by West Virginia miners, and the work of the Black Lung Association forced Congress to act.

The carbon figure on this bill has been fought over in the same way. Under President Obama a federal working group set the social cost of carbon at about $42 a ton, counting damage anywhere in the world. Under President Trump it was cut to between $1 and $7 by counting only damage inside the United States, a change that Gernot Wagner and other economists criticized in 2021 as having no grounding in the usual practice of weighing costs against benefits. Under President Biden it went back to about $51, and in 2023 the Environmental Protection Agency raised it to the $190 I have used. The physics of carbon did not change from one administration to the next. What changed was the answer to a political question, which is how much of the cost should be counted at all, and one side of that question has far more money to spend on it than the other. Oil and gas companies alone reported well over $100 million in federal lobbying in 2024, by the count of the research group OpenSecrets.

Money and public attention spent keeping a bill unwritten are a cost too. I enter it at $1, and of all the figures in this chapter it is the one most likely to be too low. Two bodies of work gave me the vocabulary for this line. The economists and writers who have studied the racial wealth gap and made the case for reparations, among them William Darity Jr., A. Kirsten Mullen, Darrick Hamilton, Dalton Conley and Ta-Nehisi Coates, showed how to name the people and the coalitions that shaped an arrangement. James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock, who founded the study of public choice, described the reasoning of a company that spends money to win favorable rules, which economists call rent-seeking. I use that vocabulary without taking on the rest of their political program, and Chapter 9 says more about both.

The fourth line is speed. This coal lay in the ground for hundreds of millions of years, and most of it came out within about a century, fastest in the decades after the machines arrived. The country did not need it at that rate. It came out that fast because the machines made it possible and because the companies earned more by mining faster. A slower pace would have left time for other kinds of work to grow up in the county, and time for other sources of energy to be developed. A higher price would have slowed it. After the oil shocks of the 1970s, when energy suddenly cost more, the United States spent the next two decades steadily using less energy for each dollar it produced, according to the Energy Information Administration. Coal's price never sent that signal, because the price left out most of the cost. I enter this line at $2.

## The sum

Here is the bill for one ton of McDowell coal, at the lowest figure on every line.

- The lungs: $2
- The creeks: $1
- The town: $5
- The air: $496
- The children: $2
- Knowledge: $1
- Politics: $1
- Speed: $2

**Total: $510**

In 1960 the ton sold for $4.71. Written out now, its bill comes to $510. Those are two prices from two different eras, and I won't divide one by the other, because that would compare a dollar of 1960 with a dollar of today. The fair comparison is with what a ton of coal sells for now, which is $40 to $140. Against that price the bill is about 3.6 to 13 times what the buyer pays.

Almost all of it is the air: $496 of the $510. That line rests on the government's figure and not on mine. The seven lines that rest partly or wholly on my estimates come to $14 together.

Chapter 6 gave a figure of $525 to $540 for this same ton, and the two figures are related. Chapter 6 starts from the first four lines of this bill and adds a term for what burning the coal takes away from the people who come later, the cost I left without a figure in the section on the air. So the two totals are not independent checks on each other. They share the carbon line.

## What was paid, and by whom

Some of this bill was paid. Most of what was paid came from the public.

The largest payment by far is the black lung benefits, about $44 billion through 2009. They were paid from a public trust fund, which is filled by a tax on coal as it is sold and by money borrowed from the federal treasury. Coal companies posted reclamation bonds worth about $3 billion to $5 billion, after allowing for the bonds that fell short. When three coal companies went bankrupt between 2014 and 2016, about $865 million of what they owed sick miners moved onto the public. And I estimate that another $5 billion to $10 billion in miners' compensation and medical costs was paid over the years, most of it by public programs.

Together that is about $53 billion to $60 billion. McDowell County and the counties around it produced about 600 million tons of coal, so the payments come to between $88 and $100 a ton from everyone who paid anything. That figure is too high, and I know it is too high, because the black lung benefits were paid to miners across the whole country and I have divided them by the tons of one coalfield. I leave it too high on purpose, because the error runs against my own argument. The more I count as paid, the less remains unpaid.

Of that $88 to $100, the coal industry paid between $8 and $13 a ton directly, through its bonds and its private settlements. Only the bonds were put up in advance, before the damage they were meant to cover, and they come to between $5 and $8 a ton.

Now set what was paid beside what was owed. Against Chapter 6's figure of $525 to $540 a ton, at least about $425 to $450 was never paid by anyone. Counting only what the industry paid, the unpaid part is about $512 to $532. Most of the unpaid part is the air, and the people it is owed to have not been born.

## Not only here

Is McDowell County a special case? The earlier chapters say it isn't. In Libby, Montana, the federal cleanup has cost more than $600 million, about six times the roughly $100 million the mine is estimated to have taken in, and the company repaid $250 million of it. In the Gulf of Mexico, BP paid about $61.6 billion after the Deepwater Horizon spill, the rare case in which the company that did the damage paid most of the bill, and it paid because lawsuits forced it to. At Baotou, in Inner Mongolia, nobody has published a count of the damage that could be set beside the revenue, so I can't give a figure at all. And Norway shows that money can be set aside against a bill like this while the resource is still being sold: the fund it built from its oil holds about $36 for every barrel of oil and gas the country has produced.

The method does not find a gap everywhere it looks. It returns zero in two situations. The first is when a perfect substitute exists for what was used up, because then nothing has been taken from the people who come later. The second is when a resource that renews itself is used no faster than it renews. A fishery that is harvested below the rate at which the fish replace themselves leaves no bill behind, and Chapter 3 showed what happens to a bay when the harvest runs faster than that. The bill appears only where use runs ahead of what can be restored, and with coal it always does, because coal does not come back.

## What honest pricing doesn't mean

It doesn't mean the coal should have stayed in the ground. Cheap energy lifted a great many people out of poverty. The steel made with this coal built railroads, ships and bridges, and the men who mined it did hard, skilled work that was worth doing. The bill doesn't ask whether the coal should have been mined. It asks who should have paid what the mining cost, and it shows that for the most part the buyer of the coal didn't.

It doesn't mean the market was fake, either. The coal was real, the money was real, and the $4.71 was a true price for what it covered. What it covered was the work of getting the coal out of the ground. Everything else in this chapter happened alongside that sale, to people who were never asked to agree to it, and none of it reached the invoice. The market was incomplete, and this chapter has filled in the part it left out. The idea that a price can leave a cost out is not new. The economist Arthur Pigou described it more than a century ago, and Chapter 6 says what this book borrows from him and what it adds.

So the rail car that left McDowell County in 1960 carried two bills. One of them was for $4.71, and it was paid on the spot. The other was for $510, and most of it is still owed. What would a country that decided to pay a bill like that actually do?
