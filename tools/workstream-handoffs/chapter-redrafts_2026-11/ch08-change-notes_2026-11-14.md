## 9. Strongest-critic read of draft B (run once)

Where a hostile but fair reader would stop, as questions for you.

1. **The four smaller lines.** The section now says in so many words that no outside source prices these lines and that each is entered at "a dollar or two." An economist will ask why any figure is better than a named line with none, and will note that $6 of unsourced entries buys nothing the $504 does not already show. You have decided to keep the $510. Is the reason the draft gives ("leaving them off would enter them at zero") the reason you want on the page?
2. **"Most of what was paid came from the public."** The black lung trust fund is filled mainly by an excise tax on coal. A reader from the industry will say that the tax is the industry paying, which would move most of the $44 billion to the industry's side and reverse the section's first sentence. The ledger counts only bonds and private settlements as industry-paid. Do you want a sentence that meets this head-on (who actually bears a tax on coal; the fund's borrowing from the Treasury), or a narrower claim?
3. **$2 a ton charged for health, $88 to $100 a ton counted as paid.** The draft explains that the paid figure divides national payments by one coalfield's tons. A careful reader may still stop at a bill whose health line is $2 when the payments against it look forty times larger. Should the health line say that its $1 to $1.50 is a national average per ton, so the two figures are visibly on different bases?
4. **Whose dollars?** Section 7 refuses to divide $510 by $4.71 because the two are from different eras. But section 4 says the $8 bill "is already more than the $4.71 on the 1960 invoice," which is the same comparison in words (the map asked for it). And the bill itself mixes payments summed over decades with a 2023 carbon figure. Keep the $8 remark, or cut it?
5. **A 2023 carbon price on a 1960 ton.** The EPA's $190 is its estimate for carbon released now. The ton left McDowell in 1960. A climate economist will ask why today's damage figure applies to an emission sixty-five years old. Does the Technical Appendix answer this, and should the chapter give the answer in a sentence?
6. **This coal made steel.** Chapter 2 says McDowell's coal was metallurgical coal, bound for the steel furnaces. The air section says burned coal "can't be used for making steel," and the chapter prices the ton as if it were burned for heat. A reader who remembers Chapter 2 will stop. Is the carbon released in steelmaking the same per ton, and should the section say that this coal went to the furnaces and released its carbon there?
7. **Where Dunbar sits.** The passage stands between the children's line and the knowledge line, in a section about four small dollar entries for a coal county. A reader may ask whether a tradition of Black American writing is being borrowed to lend weight to a different people's ledger, and whether the credit would land better where the book first uses the word "masking." Does it stay here, as the map has it, or move?
