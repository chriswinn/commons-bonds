# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 3: The Waterman

---

The harbor at Old Point Comfort wakes a long time before the city does. The first work boat eases out of its slip in the dark that comes just before gray, and a diesel coughs and settles. On the next pier a man says something to a man on the boat. Neither of them raises his voice, because at that hour the water carries a voice farther than it was meant to go. The Fort sits dark behind them. Ahead of them the mouth of the Bay opens gray on gray, and the channel marker blinks red. Somewhere on the far side of the channel a tug is moving supplies west toward Newport News, another load for the expansion of the Hampton Roads Bridge-Tunnel, which has dragged on for years.

I live aboard a boat now, but I did not grow up on the water the way a waterman's son would have. When I went out as a kid, it was for a day of fun, and maybe the fish bit and maybe they didn't. A waterman is deep into his work before dawn, and he stays out until there is enough aboard to feed a family and put money in a pocket. He comes back in the afternoon, tired. Some men come back with a hold full of crabs and some with a hold full of nothing, and the men who come home empty are quieter than the others, though neither group says much. They tie up, hose down and walk to a truck. At four the next morning they will be at the slip again, or they will not. The Bay will not notice the difference either way. The slip will notice, and the dock, and the man two slips over who has been doing this since before any of them were born. That is how the working waterfront keeps its books.

So what are those books not allowed to record?

---

I am writing from Hampton, Virginia. I grew up on this bay, and Hampton once held one of the largest oyster-packing operations in the United States. The firm of J.S. Darling and Son took that name in 1884, when Frank Darling joined his father's oyster business. At its peak, 160 shuckers handled 200,000 bushels of Chesapeake oysters every year. The meat went out in iced tubs to dinner tables and restaurants in Baltimore, New York and other cities, and the empty shells stayed behind. They left the plant on conveyor belts and went onto piles out back that rose more than four stories high, mountains a town could see and smell from the waterfront. The Darlings also ran a factory at Factory Point in Fox Hill that rendered thousands of tons of menhaden a year into fish meal for fertilizer and oil for industry. Up and down the coast Hampton was known as Crabtown. In the 1870s a man named James McMenamin had invented and patented a canning process there that put cooked Chesapeake crabmeat in airtight tins and shipped it to tables far from the Bay.

The meat left town, and the shells had to go somewhere. Most were sold and crushed into roads and driveways across the lower Bay, because shell was cheaper than rock for keeping a dirt lane out of the mud. My father's family's own driveway was paved with them. Some shells were hauled out to Back River and dumped, and new oysters anchored on them and grew. So anyone could see what shell did when it went back in the water. Most of it went into roads anyway, because the other choice was to pay more for gravel. My father worked every summer to pay his way through college, and he spent one of those summers pouring concrete for the boardwalk at Buckroe Beach. It was concrete and rock with no shell in it, and it still stands. The driveway is the part I keep returning to: the material a reef is rebuilt from, spread under a family's cars.

In the summer of 2020, at the Navy's own installation on the Hampton Roads shoreline, Navy personnel and volunteers stacked concrete blocks called oyster castles into the water. The blocks give young oysters something hard to cling to. The towns of the lower Bay paved their roads with the thing that builds reefs, and a generation later the Navy is casting concrete to do what the shell would have done for free.

The company shut down in 1979, and I was born in 1980. I never saw the shell mountains, and there are not enough oysters coming out of the water now to pile them that high. Seafood is still pulled from the Chesapeake today, but nothing like what came across these docks then. Nobody has ever had to explain to me how a renewable resource turns into one that isn't. I have watched some of it happen here.

---

The neighborhood of Hampton I am closest to is called Fox Hill. It is, or was, an island fishing community, a place where for most of the twentieth century almost every house had at least one person on the water. My grandfather was an exception. He worked just up the road at NASA Langley, building pieces of a different kind of America in a different kind of building. The neighbors on either side of him were on the water, and the boys my father grew up with were the sons of watermen. My father did not become a waterman himself. But the men who taught him how to handle a small boat, where the fish were running and what the smell of the channel meant in late August were men whose lives were the water. My family is one generation removed from the working life and no distance at all from the community.

Most days, at most hours, the Chesapeake looks like a postcard: flat water, soft light, a bay you would put a child on. Ask any waterman about the chop and you will hear about the other days. On the ocean a five-foot wave is barely worth a comment, because the tops of those waves run thirty feet apart or more, and the slow rocking can put a man to sleep. The Bay does not build waves in that shape. On the Bay a five-foot wave can come at you with something close to five feet between it and the next one, a wave very nearly square, and a square wave will turn the hardiest seaman green. The boat built for this water is the deadrise. It is the waterman's boat and nothing fancy, and the men who run them will tell you it rides the chop more steadily than almost anything else out here. There are no superyachts on this water. Those are somewhere else in the world, which says something about where the water's value went. The men who have worked the Bay for forty years know which days you do not go out, which days you go out but stay close to home, and which days the sky is lying about what the water will do in three hours. They know it without consulting anything outside themselves. The water gives them that knowledge on one condition, which is that they never mistake which day they are looking at.

It takes forty years of going out every day to know what the watermen know, and none of it is learned on weekends. It is learned on the sweltering summer days when the heat takes the air, and on the winter mornings when the gale comes off the cape and blows through whatever you are wearing and shakes your very soul. The men go out anyway, because the houses are not paid for and the families do not eat when nothing comes home. Their rain slicks keep the water out but hold no warmth, so under the slicks go thick base layers and sweaters against the brutal cold. Every man dressed that way knows what the clothes weigh. He knows that if he goes into the freezing water he will not get them off in time to reach the surface. He knows it, and he goes.

A community that grows up around work like that takes a particular shape. When the watermen of a place like Fox Hill say they look out for each other, they are describing how the work is built, not a feeling. A waterman whose nets need rebuilding before the next season cannot rebuild them alone in the time he has, and there is no industry of net-rebuilders to hire. So a morning is set. At three or four, when every man there would normally be on the water or on his way to it, the boats stay tied up and the pickups come to the place of whoever needs the nets. The men bring their own coffee, and they work until the nets are done. Nobody there thinks of the morning as a favor. Each of them knows, as surely as the sun rises and sets, that a morning is coming when the man who needs the help will be him, or his son, or his father. A community that fails to come on the day you need it failed long before that day. That is the bargain, and it holds for as long as the community holds.

The hardest thing to convey to anyone who has not been around it is what happens when one of the men who said he would be there is not. Everyone stops, and the nets go down. Somebody picks up a phone and calls somebody else, and within ten minutes the whole morning has reorganized itself around the question of where the missing man is. Everyone there has done this work long enough to know that only one kind of thing keeps a waterman from being where he said he would be, and it is something serious enough that he cannot tell anyone about it. It might be his truck, or his health. Or he was on the water at four-thirty and did not come back, so the water is the first place the other men go to check. The older men carry that possibility in their bodies the way a cook carries the possibility of burns.

This is the community Fox Hill was for most of my father's life. In my own life I have watched it slowly thin.

---

To understand what is happening on the Chesapeake now, start with what the Chesapeake was within living memory. I have lived near this water long enough to have known three rough generations of the men who work it. The Bay the grandfathers fished was not the Bay the fathers fished, and the Bay the fathers fished is not the one the sons would have fished, had there been a Bay left for them to fish. Across those three generations the resource has been visibly thinning, everywhere, in every season.

The oyster is the easiest place to begin, because the historical record carries it most clearly. In the late 1880s the Chesapeake produced something on the order of fifteen million bushels of oysters a year. By the 1990s the number had fallen to under two hundred thousand. That is a 99 percent decline in a century. Not many fisheries in the world have lost that much of themselves and still been recognizable as the same fishery, and there is a real argument that this one wasn't. The boats looked similar in 1990, and so did the tongs and the dock at the end of the day. But what the men were going out for was a different thing from what their grandfathers had gone out for in 1890.

A 99 percent decline is not luck or weather, and the causes are well documented now and were largely understood at the time. The first was overharvesting. Dredges scraped the reefs flat, season after season, faster than the reefs could rebuild. The second was disease: MSX in the late 1950s and Dermo in the years that followed, two parasites that the weakened, thinned-out populations could not withstand. The third was sediment. Agricultural runoff smothered the young oysters where they tried to set, and the nutrients in that runoff fed algal blooms that used up the dissolved oxygen the rest of the system depended on. Each wave made the next one worse. Reefs that had once been visible at low tide, reefs that ships in the colonial period had to chart around, were ground down to flat bottom and buried in silt. An oyster needs something hard to set on. By the late twentieth century, much of the bottom the oysters had built over centuries had been ground into the same mud the runoff was laying over it.

The last chapter ended on the difference between two kinds of drawdown, and the reef is where that difference matters. A coal seam is finite from the day it forms. You can dig it out faster or slower, but you cannot dig out more than was laid down. An oyster reef is another kind of thing entirely, because it produces more oysters every year for as long as the reef itself is left undamaged. Take some, leave the rest to spawn, and come back next year. Done correctly, the harvest does not shrink across the generations. You can do it, and your son after you, and his son after him, because the fishery makes more of itself.

What happened in the Chesapeake is what happens when the taking runs faster than the making. For a long while nobody can see the difference. The harvest is still good, the price still feels right, the boats come back fairly full most days, and there are good seasons and bad seasons the way there have always been. Then a season comes that cannot be explained away, and by then the damage is permanent, because the harvest had been taking the reef itself along with the oysters, and the reef was written off as a cost of doing business. The reef the oysters built was the bottom the next generation of oysters would have set on. Grind the reef down and you have taken more than this year's oysters. You have taken the bottom's ability to make any oysters at all. From that point on, leaving the system alone is no longer an answer. Either somebody puts something back, or what was lost stays lost.

Elinor Ostrom spent her career documenting commons that did not collapse, the fisheries, forests and irrigation systems that communities governed for generations without taking them past renewal. In those commons the people who took from the resource also lived with what happened to it, and on this reef the people who took the value had stopped sharing the reef's fate.

Nobody marks the day a reef crosses that line. Decline on this water moves the way a sunset moves: you would swear it is taking forever, and then you blink. The men going out the next morning go out the same way they went out the morning before. But something has been done that no decision of theirs can undo.

One of the oystermen my father knew, growing up on Fox Hill, was a man called Biggie. Biggie had the build of an oak. He was not tall, in the way some big men are not tall, but he was solid through the shoulders and the back, the build of a man who had done one specific kind of work for most of his adult life. He worked oysters, and he had worked them since he was old enough to be useful in a boat with whoever brought him into one. The bars he worked were bars he had worked his entire life, and his boat was suited to the work. He went out before the day began, and he came back when the bushels were what the bushels were going to be. You could watch him shift among his beds with one oar in the water, making the subtlest corrections with no wasted motion, and see at once that his family had been doing this for generations.

Every morning he went out, Biggie carried aboard a six-pack of Coca-Cola in a wire carrier. He brought no ice, no cooler and no lunch. The first time my father asked him about the lunch, my father was a small boy, still young enough to be honestly puzzled. Biggie smiled and told him his lunch was waiting for him in the water, as fresh as it could be. Once the sun was high and the work had found its rhythm, Biggie ate oysters as he worked. He kept a spot on the boat, near his body, where he set the oyster he was about to shuck, so that he never had to reach or lift his arm. He had spent a working life reducing the job to its smallest possible movement: a quarter turn of the wrist, the shell parting, the meat coming up clean. My father watched him more than once and tried to count how many oysters Biggie put away in a single day on the water. He told me, when I was old enough to ask, that he never managed to keep count. It was not that there were too many to count, though there may have been. It was that the eating was so woven into the work that there was never a moment when an oyster started or stopped being eaten. It was simply something Biggie was doing alongside everything else he was doing, the way breathing is. My father has told that story so many times that I finish it for him now whenever he tells it again.

Biggie has been dead for more than thirty years. He died before Virginia stopped its commercial shad fishery in 1994, a date the men still on the water sometimes use to mark when things were still working. He went out with what an earlier generation of men had gone out with, and he came back with what they had come back with, and the bars sounded under his tongs the way they had sounded under the tongs of the men before him.

The bars do not sound that way anymore. Biggie's body had worn the work down to a quarter turn of the wrist, and nobody is showing that motion to any younger man at Fox Hill, because no younger man at Fox Hill is going out to bars that would let him learn it.

---

The men who lived this do not use words like renewable or regeneration rate. If you sat with them on a dock, late in a season that had not gone the way a season is supposed to go, they would tell you what they remember. A grandfather went out of a place that does not exist as a working dock anymore. Their fathers ran boats named for wives and daughters, and some of those names are still on boats and some are not. When they were small they could stand in the bow on a calm summer day off the mouth of the Poquoson River and see the bottom, actually see it, the grass and the crabs walking through the grass. A child cannot do that anymore in most of the places on the Bay where it could once be done. The water is greener than it used to be, and the smell at the dock in late August has changed slightly. Their fathers came home with a kind of catch that does not come home anymore.

A man in his sixties who has worked the Lower Bay since he was twelve does not need a stock assessment to tell him something has changed. He has been making the comparison every morning for fifty years.

Nothing in the way these men talk is nostalgic. It is closer to the way you might describe a relative's slow illness, in small and exact observations from someone who has been paying attention for a long time. Around them the feeling is less anger than a patient, unsentimental clarity. They are not asking to be pitied, and most of them would refuse the offer. They are telling you what they have seen.

What they have seen is the margins disappearing. Seasons that used to carry a year now carry only part of one. Species that used to fill in for each other when one ran short do not fill in the same way. The crabs are smaller, on average, than the crabs they used to bring in. The runs of striped bass that their fathers timed to the calendar do not run on the calendar anymore, and the water itself seems to have a different temper. These observations run across forty and fifty years, which makes them one of the longest continuous records that any working person in the United States keeps. The record is kept in their bodies and their memories.

J.C. Hudgins runs the Virginia Watermen's Association and works the Piankatank River in the Middle Bay, and he has done the math out loud. *You're lucky if you can put $150 in your pocket per day,* he told *Maryland Matters*. *And it ain't no easy job. You're freezing your butt off. It's cold.* He has done it for the buyer, too: *People aren't going to spend $20 for a pint of oysters. It's pretty expensive eating. I think people are feeding their families in other ways.*

So the men on the water knew years ago. What did the people who manage the Bay do once they knew it too?

---

The striped bass, which people on the Bay call rockfish, is the species that showed Maryland and Virginia in the 1980s what stepping in can do. I am old enough to remember it. The fish I loved to eat as a boy were striped bass, red snapper and grouper, and what those years taught me first was how quietly such a thing goes. You notice you are catching one of them less often. Then it crosses your mind that you have not seen it on a menu in a couple of years, and you put the difference down to a bad season or two. I understand now that the bad season or two was the last of the seasons when the fish were there in any real number, and that those of us who fished the Bay went on taking as much as we could carry to shore. As the moratorium years came on, the watermen I knew as a boy, at the marinas where my family kept boats, were talking about something they had not had to talk about before. I did not understand the politics of it then. I understood, the way a child understands without being told, that the men whose work depended on this fish had agreed, however reluctantly, to stop the work for a long time so that the fish could come back.

By the late 1970s the population had collapsed so badly that Maryland and Virginia, working with the federal government, agreed to a moratorium. On the Maryland side, for five years beginning in 1985, no one fished for striped bass at all: not the watermen, not the charter fleet, not the recreational anglers. It was a hard thing to ask of the people whose income depended on the fishery. Many of them lost a livelihood for those years, and some never came back to the work. But the moratorium did what it was designed to do. The spawning population recovered, and by 1995 the fishery had been declared restored. The men who had given up five years saw a fishery come back from a place very few fisheries come back from.

Those men also disproved a famous argument. In "The Tragedy of the Commons," Garrett Hardin held that people who share a resource cannot restrain themselves, that each of them takes until the resource is gone, and that the only cures are private ownership or rule imposed from above. The watermen tied up their boats for five years, by agreement and at real cost to themselves, and the fish came back.

That recovery is part of why the present is hard for the men who remember it, because what is happening to the rockfish now is what was happening to the rockfish before the moratorium. Year after year the count of young fish has come in below the long-term average. In 2024 Maryland's young-of-year index was 2.0 fish per sample, against a long-term average of 11.0, and it was the sixth such season in a row. After six in a row it is no longer weather.

Allison Colden is the Chesapeake Bay Foundation's Maryland executive director, and she has watched that signal build across the years. *Maryland's striped bass surveys have made it clear,* she has said in public statements; *we're facing a looming disaster. Without action now, the Chesapeake Bay won't have a striped bass fishery in the future.* In a different interview she was blunter: *there are very few levers left to pull.* Many people in Maryland, she has said elsewhere, *rely on striped bass for their food, their businesses, and their futures. Without action, that will all be at risk.* Rising water temperatures and the low-oxygen periods of summer, both made worse by warming, are *pummeling* the species, in her word, in the months when it can least afford it.

Chris Moore, the foundation's Virginia executive director, said the harder thing in response to the 2024 stock assessment. *Despite recent management actions, striped bass continue to struggle. It is obvious that conditions have changed and striped bass will not have the same recovery they had in the 1980s. Warmer water from climate change, habitat loss, and invasive species are all making it very hard for striped bass to bounce back.*

The men on the water did not need to be told. They felt it before the survey numbers came in, the way miners know what the dust is doing to them years before the X-rays come back. What the surveys do is force the institutions to admit what the watermen already knew, and the admission always comes late: later than the data would have allowed, and later than the watermen warned. In 1985 it did not come late, because in 1985 there was a political will that has thinned in the decades since. The agencies that once could ask a fishery to take a generation off have lost some of their ability to ask.

So when the watermen watch the rockfish, they are really watching whether the system built to manage the Bay can act on what its own science is telling it. So far, in this round, the answer has been mostly no. Catch limits get tightened in smaller steps than the science has called for. Emergency actions get debated longer than the species can spare. The committees meet, substitute motions are offered, and the argument for erring on the side of conservation is answered with the argument that nobody is certain enough yet to act. When the proposal was to raise the harvest, the committees acted with confidence. Now that the proposal is to cut it, they have become cautious.

Bill Goldsborough was the fisheries director at the Chesapeake Bay Foundation through the rockfish moratorium years, and he told *Smithsonian Magazine* how the two clocks differ. *Our objective is to try to restore the health of bay and aquatic life so that it can support viable fisheries,* he has said. *We see that task taking place over a longer term [...]. But the watermen don't have the luxury of the long term. They are concerned about making their next boat payment.*

Some of the men still on the water lived through all of it. They gave up five years so that a fishery could come back, they watched it come back, and they are now watching it begin to go again, and no second moratorium has been called.

The rockfish moratorium was not the only one. In January 1994, a few years after it ended, Virginia closed its commercial shad fishery, the spring run that had been one of the Bay's reliable fisheries through most of the twentieth century. Shad live most of their lives in the open Atlantic and come into the Bay each spring to spawn, running up the tributary rivers to lay their eggs in fresh water. The Bay is the spawning ground for the shad of an entire region, so when its rivers fail the shad, the shad fail in the ocean too. The men whose fathers had timed the shad run to the calendar were still on the water when the runs stopped coming, and they could tell you the spring when the nets in late March began to come up empty. The closure was meant to give the shad a generation off, as the moratorium had given the rockfish. But the runs did not come back after 1994. Scientists have not agreed on a single explanation, and the one thing they do agree on is that there has been no recovery. In any working sense there are no shad in the Bay anymore. The measure that saved the rockfish was tried for the shad, and it came too late.

The blue crab is in a different kind of trouble. A crab does not build a reef, and it lives only a year and a half or sometimes two, so in principle the population can rebound quickly from a bad year if the next year is good. For a long time that made crabbing the most reliable seasonal income a working waterman on the Chesapeake had. The crabs came back every spring. They were tonged, trotlined and potted through the warm months and sold to picking houses up and down the Bay, where mostly women picked the meat by hand, and the meat ended up in restaurants in cities where most of the people eating it had never seen a crab pot in the water. The 2025 winter dredge survey put the Bay-wide population at around 238 million crabs. That was the second-lowest level since the survey began in 1990, and the sixth consecutive year of below-average numbers. *The red flags are flying for blue crabs,* Colden has said, and she has named the cause: *with more than five years of below average crab numbers, it is clear that changing conditions in the Bay are undermining the current management of this important species.* The rules are not so much wrong as written for a Bay that no longer exists. The committees that set the limits still meet on the schedule the earlier Bay kept, and the crabs are no longer on that schedule. The men on the water say it more simply. The crabs aren't where they used to be. They aren't running when they used to run. The bigger ones are gone.

---

There is a fish you have probably never eaten. It is small, oily and silver. It runs in schools so dense that their movement can flatten the surface of the water like a passing wind, and by weight it is one of the most important fish in the Atlantic. It is the menhaden. On the New Jersey side they call it bunker, and in New England, pogy. On the Bay it is just menhaden.

You do not eat it because it is not pleasant to eat. Almost everything else eats it instead. It is the forage fish at the base of the Bay's food web, the fish that the striped bass, the bluefish, the ospreys, the loons and the dolphins all come for. It is also the catch of the largest single fishery on the East Coast, an industrial operation out of Reedville, Virginia, which takes menhaden at scale and grinds them down into fish meal, fish oil, omega-3 supplements, pet food and feed for fish farms. The catch leaves the Bay in tank trucks, in railcars and on ships, and none of it goes to anyone's dinner table as fish.

That makes it an unusual case, because the menhaden is the food web. Take enough of it out and the rockfish do not have enough to eat in summer, when they are stressed by heat and low oxygen and trying to put on weight. In some of the Bay's tributaries the ospreys have begun to fail to fledge their young, and the people who watch ospreys suspect the parent birds are not bringing in enough small oily fish to feed a growing brood. Some of them call what they are seeing across the Bay *a hollowing*. The bigger species look fine until you ask whether they are reproducing and whether their young are surviving, and then the picture changes.

How much menhaden to take out of the Bay is the longest-running fight over fisheries policy in the region. There is a cap for the Bay of 51,000 metric tons. The Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission set it in 2017, and it was a cut of roughly forty percent from the limit before it. Conservation groups regard the cap as a floor rather than a ceiling, and the industry regards it as a hard-fought concession. In recent years the commission's scientific committee has recommended reductions along the whole coast and precautions for the Bay. The management board has adopted smaller reductions than the committee recommended, on slower schedules than the committee proposed. *Just as we were confident in increasing the total allowable catch when the science says we should,* Colden has said, *we need to be as willing to take reductions when the science indicates that that's warranted.*

Meanwhile nobody knows how many menhaden are in the Bay. Genny Nesslage, an associate research professor at the University of Maryland Center for Environmental Science, said it plainly to WHRO: *We don't know the abundance of menhaden in the bay at any given time relative to the coast.* Rob Latour, who studies menhaden at the Virginia Institute of Marine Science, said it more bluntly to the same outlet: *We have no data to really do this in a meaningful way, from a biological perspective.* In 2023 the institute drafted a $3 million proposal for a study of menhaden in the Bay itself. The Virginia General Assembly declined to fund it.

So increases are adopted as fast as people want them, and reductions only as fast as the resistance to them allows. The watermen know that, and the ospreys are living with it.

More than any other fight over managing the Bay, the menhaden cap shows who the system is built to serve. Reedville sits at the mouth of the Great Wicomico River in Northumberland County, and the plant there has employed several hundred people, in shifts that follow the seasons the menhaden run. The fishery does not feed people directly. It supplies ingredients to other industries, and the customers at the end of those chains have no idea that the small oily fish at the base of the Chesapeake food web passed through a Reedville plant on the way to them. They are the person taking an omega-3 capsule in Indianapolis, the owner of a salmon farm in Chile, and the shopper buying a forty-pound bag of dog food at a grocery in Phoenix. The value spreads out across a continent and across industries. The cost stays here, on the rockfish, the ospreys, and the watermen who depend on a Bay that depends on the menhaden.

On the last day of 2025 a Virginia resident named Tonya O'Connor filed a 349-page petition with the Virginia Marine Resources Commission. It asked the agency to declare a temporary moratorium on the Bay's reduction fishery until the agency produced science specific to the Bay. The petition's central finding was that the commission *did not have*, and *still does not have*, *a Bay-wide estimate of menhaden biomass, age structure, spatial distribution, recruitment trends, or localized depletion risk.* Its legal argument rested on Virginia's public-trust doctrine and cited three cases: *Commonwealth v. City of Newport News*, 158 Va. 521 (1932); *Taylor v. Commonwealth*, 102 Va. 759 (1904); and *Bradford v. Nature Conservancy*, 224 Va. 181 (1982). The commission held a hearing on April 21, 2026, and received 1,266 public comments. It voted six to two against the petition.

---

The fishing is only part of what reaches the watermen. The Chesapeake watershed covers some 64,000 square miles across six states and the District of Columbia. The water that makes the Bay starts as rain on a corn field in central Pennsylvania, a soybean field on the Eastern Shore, a parking lot in Northern Virginia, a chicken house on the Delmarva Peninsula, a wastewater plant in Baltimore, a suburban lawn in Loudoun County. By the time it reaches the Bay it is carrying everything those places have given it: nitrogen from fertilizer, phosphorus from manure, sediment from disturbed ground, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, whatever the economy upstream has not contained.

The science of this has been understood for forty years. The Chesapeake Bay Program was established in 1983, and ever since it has been measuring the nutrient loads, modeling them, dividing them among the states, and asking the parties upstream to reduce them. It has had partial successes and significant failures. Pollution from wastewater plants and factory pipes has come down in many places. But when the Program's own science advisory committee recently reviewed the forty-year effort, it said as plainly as such reports are allowed to that the cleanup is not on track to meet its goals. Agriculture has not been required to do what the science said agriculture needed to do, and the political will to require it has not formed.

The science was right. The targets were modeled, the schedule was set, and the states agreed to it. And where the loads were largest they did not come down at the rate the agreement called for, because the farm economy upstream carried more political weight than the fishing economy downstream. Year by year, the decisions went the way of the heavier weight.

From the deck of a sailboat in summer, in the years I have been on it, I have watched the green of the water change in a way that does not feel like life. The grass beds I could see through the water in the early 2000s are not visible the same way anymore. The watermen have been watching for decades longer than I have. They know that the runoff reaching the grass beds in Mobjack Bay started as fertilizer on a field they have never seen, owned by a farmer they will never meet, in a state none of their children have lived in. They know about the dead zones, the volumes of low-oxygen water that form in the deeper parts of the Bay every summer, suffocating the bottom-dwelling species that cannot move and stressing the swimming species that can. They know those dead zones come from farming and building upstream, and that they have never had any voice in either.

The watermen live at the bottom of a watershed whose accounting does not include them. Everything anyone does upstream has to come past their boats, and nothing they do reaches anyone upstream at all.

There is a man on Fox Hill I am leaving unnamed here. He is alive and still on the water, and the question of how he wants to appear in a book is not yet settled between us. He is a large man, not tall, and he has been on the Bay since he was a boy. He comes from the same Fox Hill world Biggie came from, and his working life picks up roughly where Biggie's left off.

He is a crabber and a fisherman, and he has been one all his working life. The water near Fox Hill and Poquoson is the water he has always known. Across his decades on it he has watched the math change for a man like him. The catches have come back thinner while the costs of fuel, gear and the slip have gone up, and not all of the species he came up working are still running. None of this is particular to him. Every man still working this water has had to absorb the same conditions in his own way. What is particular to him is how he has absorbed them.

He still gets up at two or three in the morning. He still goes out and pulls his crab pots, and he runs them the way he ran them when he was younger, but the math at the end of the day is not what it was then. So he comes back to the dock by mid-morning, hoses down the boat, and goes to the mechanic shop he owns on the Peninsula. His name is on the sign, and his crab pots are stacked behind the locked fence in the back lot, behind the cars he is working on for customers. He works the shop until five or six in the evening. What he calls this arrangement is between him and the people who know him. What it does is keep the family's math balancing, the boat in the slip, the license current and the man on the water.

He did not quit the water, because he could not. What he did instead was spend decades building, on his own time and his own dime, a second economy that lets him stay. The accounting that made the older men's lives possible, in which the crab and the boat and the long days were enough, does not work for him. He has filled the gap with the only thing he had left to fill it with, which is more of himself.

I have not asked him what he would say about all this, and I do not have his answer to put on this page. The accounting in this book is not necessarily the one he would apply to himself. It is an accounting of what the surrounding economy has been accepting from him, and from many men like him up and down this Bay, in exchange for a continued supply of the seafood it has not stopped wanting.

---

He has a second trade to fall back on. Whole communities on this Bay do not.

Tangier Island, in the middle of the Lower Bay, is a bit over a square mile of low marsh and sand, and people have lived on it continuously since the 1770s. By some accounts the accent spoken there is closer to a colonial-era English than to anything else still spoken in North America. The island's economy has been the water, crabs in the warm months and oysters in the cold ones, and its rhythms and vocabulary have been carried through eight generations of the island's families. The island is also losing shoreline to erosion, nearly two feet a year on the long-term average, and the projections suggest most of it will be uninhabitable within the lifetime of children now in its school. The mayor and the men on the docks have asked, repeatedly, for the engineering that would buy them another few decades. So far, most of what they have asked for has not come at the scale that would matter.

James "Ooker" Eskridge, the mayor of Tangier, has been a working waterman for more than fifty years. He told the Thomson Reuters Foundation what was at stake: *We're talking about saving a culture, the people, (a) way of life.* Cook Cannon, a former Tangier waterman who has spent the years since at the Chesapeake Bay Foundation, talked to *Smithsonian Magazine* in his island home on a cold January night. *I could cry to think about what's going to happen to Tangier.*

Smith Island, the Maryland counterpart twelve miles to the north, is in roughly the same shape. For generations the men on the boats brought in soft-shell crabs and mostly older women picked the meat, and the picking houses have been thinning. The young people are leaving for the mainland. There is a saying on Smith that you get mud between your toes when you spend time there, and that the mud means the island never leaves you. The women who say it are mostly past the age where leaving was an option. The children who hear it have been on the mainland for a decade.

Up the Bay on Tilghman Island in Maryland, the same work continues. Pat Whewell was photographed culling oysters aboard Lonnie Gowe's boat in Broad Creek in 2023, and Chumley Fisher and Chet Schwartz were photographed offloading oysters on Tilghman three years later. The pictures ran in *Maryland Matters*. The picking houses on Tilghman have been thinning like the ones on Smith.

Hampton, where I sit, is not Tangier or Smith. It is bigger and more diversified, and the larger economy of the city can absorb the slow shrinking of its working waterfront. The boats are still here and the slips are still rented, and the dockmasters still know who is going out tomorrow and who is not. But in the years I have kept my own boat at my marina, I have watched it tip slowly from a place where the working operators had most of the slips toward a place where the recreational owners do. Over a long enough time the direction is the same as on the islands. The number of working watermen on the Lower Bay has been falling for a generation. The picking houses are closing, and the boatbuilders who used to build wooden deadrises are mostly gone. A working waterfront depends on welders, engine shops, ice plants, buyers and haulers, and it loses them one at a time. In some places the community that used to come at three in the morning to help with somebody's nets no longer has the bodies to come. Eventually a young person who might have stayed cannot find the work that would have made staying possible, so that young person leaves, and the next one faces the same choice with less to stay for.

The broader economy has no name for what is being asked of these communities. In effect they are being asked to disperse. They are asked to absorb the cost of their own transition, to move their families on their own dime, to retrain their young for industries that do not exist locally, and to abandon places their ancestors have lived in for two and three centuries. They are asked to give up languages, skills and seasonal rhythms that took those generations to develop. And they are asked to do it without compensation, without acknowledgment, and largely without notice from the economy upstream, whose runoff, whose warming of the climate and whose harvest pressure have together made staying impossible. The people in these places are not asking for sympathy. Mostly they are asking to be allowed to keep doing what they know how to do, in the place where they know how to do it, for as long as the water, the regulation and the market allow.

What are these men telling their children? I have heard the answer in pieces, from men along this waterfront. The work is hard, the money is uncertain and the regulations are unpredictable. Nobody is encouraging a son into the work. Some sons go anyway, because the work is what they know and what their grandfathers did, and the choice is the son's. A daughter in nursing school in Norfolk has more options than her father had at her age, and he is glad of it, and there is something in the gain that he does not, in his bones, fully celebrate. The thinning of the family on the water, across the generations, is a kind of liberation that is also a kind of loss.

Add that up across the Lower Bay, the Eastern Shore and the small islands, and a generation is withdrawing the people who would have continued the fishery. The fathers are not giving their sons the years of patient teaching their own fathers gave them, because they will not teach a son into a trade whose floor they cannot promise. It took a community five generations to build the knowledge of where the crabs run in late October, and that community is not building a sixth generation that will know it the same way. The knowledge is dying with the men who hold it.

This is its own kind of cost, and it shows up on no stock assessment. An inheritance that took centuries to accumulate is draining away, because the generation that should receive it has been quietly told it cannot be passed on.

The children, for their part, are mostly going: to Norfolk, to Richmond, to the Hampton Roads tech corridor, to the medical centers and the military bases, to wherever a paycheck is more predictable than a season on the water. Some of them come back on weekends and ride out on the boat one more time to remember how it felt. Some of them never come back. The slip stays empty after the father retires, and the waterfront is one boat thinner, and the next father and the next son hear the news and run their own arithmetic.

Cameron Evans, a Tangier native then studying at Virginia Wesleyan, put the question to the Thomson Reuters Foundation in his own words. *If you can't save an island a mile and a half long, how are you going to save the East Coast...* Andy Harris, who represents Maryland's Eastern Shore in Congress, has put the same arithmetic from the other side of the aisle. *Our watermen are pretty resilient,* he has told *Maryland Matters*. *They're like our farmers. They have good years and bad years. But if you have a couple bad seasons in a row, then you're in trouble.*

---

And yet the oysters are coming back.

Maryland's oyster restoration program has succeeded by the measures it set for itself: five sanctuaries, each the size of a whole tributary, hundreds of acres of rebuilt reef, and tens of millions of oysters a year added to the rebuilt structures. The oyster population in Maryland has roughly tripled since 2005. *Large-scale oyster restoration is working,* Colden has said. *Our effort to bring oysters back from the brink in Chesapeake Bay is working,* she has said elsewhere, adding that *continued recovery is not guaranteed.*

Mitch Tarnowski, who leads the oyster reef surveys for Maryland's Department of Natural Resources, summed up the other half in twelve words. *There are plenty of oysters out there,* he told *Maryland Matters*. *They're just not being harvested.*

He meant that the wild bars outside the restoration sanctuaries are not being worked. The watermen with the licenses are not on the water, and the boats that used to take the oysters off those bars are mostly tied up.

In the same year the program was being celebrated as one of the largest successful ecosystem restorations in North America, the State of Maryland filed for a federal disaster declaration for its oyster fishery. The harvest had collapsed in volume and in value. The watermen who depend on the wild harvest were in financial crisis, because farm-raised oysters from the Gulf Coast had hollowed out the market for Chesapeake oysters and seasons of extreme weather had disrupted the harvest windows. *Despite a growing oyster population,* Colden said in support of the disaster request, *Maryland's watermen are feeling the pinch of extreme weather and market competition.*

Jason Ruth owns Harris Seafood Co., an oyster processor in Grasonville, Maryland, and he told the same paper how the longer arc looks from inside an oyster house. *While the demand for boutique aquaculture oysters has grown over the last decade,* he has said, *the shucked oyster sector,* which is the larger part of the industry, *has seen a 60-year nationwide decline.*

So the oyster population is recovering while the watermen are in a declared disaster, and both are true for the same reasons. The recovery is happening in sanctuaries the watermen are not allowed to harvest. The harvest from the wild bars outside the sanctuaries is being undercut in the market by aquaculture operations the watermen mostly do not own, and by Gulf Coast operations that never bore the costs the Bay's recovery is now carrying. The ecosystem is being asked to come back, and the watermen are being asked to absorb the cost of its coming back. The people eating the recovered oysters in restaurants are paying for an oyster. They are not paying for what the watermen absorbed to put it on the half-shell in front of them, and they could not if they wanted to, because the price was never built to carry it.

*One of the most important ways people connect with the Chesapeake Bay,* Colden has said, *is through eating seafood and fishing.* Without watermen, *there's certainly a lot fewer eyes on the water and a lot fewer people caring passionately about the Bay.* The people whose attention has held the Bay to account for centuries are being squeezed out by the same accounting that is beginning, species by species, to recover what was lost.

A recovery that never asks who paid leaves the people who paid still paying. The oysters can come back without the watermen coming back, and the reef can be rebuilt while the working waterfront is not. So who did pay for the reef, and who should have?

The last chapter added up what was left unpaid for the miner. The same can be done for the oyster, with an instrument I am going to name here and build properly in a later chapter. I call it the Restitution Bond. It looks backward at a commons that was drawn down, prices the physical cost of making it whole, and names who should hold that bill. It has a partner that looks forward, and that one can wait. Here I only want to run the backward one, once, on this reef.

I am going to price one thing only. I am not pricing the lost fishery, or the menhaden, or the livelihoods of the watermen, or all the oysters the bars would have grown if they had been left whole. Every one of those is a real cost. But the reef bottom itself, the hard foundation that was stripped out of the water and never put back, is physical and documented. What would it cost to rebuild it?

Darling's shell mountains are the picture of what was taken, but they cannot give the price. Raw shell is cheap, and no operation produces its peak year every year for a century. The price has to be built from acres.

Scientists who study the James River, the river system the Hampton bars belong to, have measured how much high-quality oyster reef bottom the river lost in the twentieth century. Between 1910 and 1981 the James lost about 2,700 acres of it. The careful historical acreage exists for the James and not for Hampton Bar alone, so the James stands in here for the Hampton bars. On lower-Bay bottom where oysters still set by themselves, the restoration record puts the cost of rebuilding an acre of reef between about $7,300 and $13,500. Multiply the acres by that cost and the result is roughly $20 million to $37 million. That is the floor: the smallest documented loss, priced at the cheapest documented rebuilding cost, for this one river. Price the same acres at the average cost of completed reef restoration across the whole Bay, about $77,000 an acre, and the figure comes to roughly $211 million, which is the center of the estimate.

Now set that against what the reef was worth while it stood. The watermen who brought the oysters up were paid by the bushel at the dock. At today's dockside rate of around $35 a bushel, a Darling peak year of two hundred thousand bushels comes to about $7 million. That is one peak year at today's prices, and it should not be multiplied across the decades any more than the shells should. But the dockside price was never what the oyster was worth. A bushel holds roughly two hundred oysters, and an oyster sells for a few dollars on the plate, so by the time that same harvest reached the tables it was sold to, it was worth on the order of $120 million a year. That value was realized in the dining rooms of Baltimore, New York and beyond. The watermen got the dockside price. The cities got the oyster, the restaurants, the jobs and the tax base, a whole economy built on the Bay's abundance and built everywhere except the Bay.

Put the three numbers side by side. The reef produced around $120 million a year in value, and that value went up and out to other regions' tables a century ago. The labor earned around $7 million in a peak year, and that stayed at the dock. The cost of rebuilding the lost reef, $20 million to $37 million for one river at the floor, stayed home and went unpaid. Mariana Mazzucato writes about the difference between creating value and capturing it, and David Harvey gave taking by foreclosure its name, accumulation by dispossession; this reef is one small instance of both, with a price on it.

The reef is already being rebuilt, and the public is paying for all of it. In Maryland alone, more than $92 million in state and federal money has gone into rebuilding roughly 1,300 acres of oyster reef in the sanctuaries. Across the whole Bay the public total comes to roughly $115 million. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's own itemized accounting of the completed ten-tributary restoration puts Maryland's share at $93.52 million and Virginia's at $21.77 million, paid by NOAA, the Army Corps of Engineers, and the Maryland and Virginia resource agencies. These two sets of numbers measure different things. The damage figure is for one river, and the public spending is for the whole Bay, so one cannot be subtracted from the other. What they show together is who has paid. At the Navy's Hampton Roads installation, the oyster castles went into the water on the Navy's account and the public agencies', with young oysters supplied by the Chesapeake Bay Program. No dollar figure for that one project has been published. The Navy paid, and the taxpayers paid. The industry that took the value of one of the country's largest oyster operations out of this water and shipped it to other people's cities has paid nothing toward the reef's return. The industry's line in this column is zero.

McDowell County had the same defendant. There, the value of the coal spread across a continent, the black lung and the orange creeks stayed in the hollows, and when the bill came due a public trust fund and public reclamation money paid it, because the companies that had collected the value were gone or shielded. Here the value of the oyster went to the cities, the cost of the lost reef stayed in the water, and public money and Navy labor are paying the bill. In both places the party that collected the value is not in the room. No instrument existed to put the cost into the price at the moment of the sale, so the cost came loose from the value and landed, decades later, on whoever was nearest. Usually that is the public.

The Restitution Bond does not invent a new bill, because the bill already exists and the public is already paying it. What the bond changes is who holds it. It names the parties who should have been holding the bill all along, and it moves the cost back onto the value. It does not pay any waterman a dollar, because the moment checks go to individuals the argument becomes which individuals and how much, and that argument swallows the idea. It pays into the reef. It rebuilds the commons that was drawn down, and it asks the parties who drew it down, and their successors, to fund the rebuilding in place of the public.

I did not invent the move of looking back at a commons that was foreclosed, pricing what it would cost to make it whole, and naming who owes the bill. The economics of reparations has been making that move for a different and a graver commons. William Darity and Kirsten Mullen built the most developed version, and Ta-Nehisi Coates made its architecture plain, which is to name the mechanism, the harm and the debt. I am borrowing their logic for a reef, and I am keeping the distance between a reef and what they price exactly as wide as it is.

The real figure for this reef is higher than the floor. The full cost to the people of the Bay, which would take in the fishery, the menhaden, the watermen's lost living and a hundred years of wealth sent elsewhere, is higher still, and I have left all of it out of the column on purpose.

---

What is happening on this Bay is happening in the Gulf of Maine, in Bristol Bay, on the coast of West Africa and in the Mekong Delta, wherever a fishery has been pushed past the rate at which it could replace itself.

The waterman is paid for the crab and not for the Bay. He is paid for the oyster that comes up in the tongs. He is not paid for the reef the tongs ground down across a century, or for the grass beds the runoff smothered, or for the menhaden the reduction fleet is taking out of the food web his life depends on, or for the Tangier shoreline going under, or for the daughter who has gone to nursing school in Norfolk because there was no boat for her to come back to. What does not come up in the tongs is left in the water, and something has to carry it. The Bay carries it while the Bay can. After that the watermen carry it, and the working waterfront, and the families, and the children who never had the chance to learn the late-October crab runs, because by the time they were old enough to learn, the crabs were no longer running on a schedule anyone could teach.

Put the miner and the waterman in a room together and they would not recognize each other right away. They live in different states and watch different news. They vote, when they vote, for different people, and they have been told different stories about who is to blame for what has happened to them. The waterman has been told that the people who would clean up the runoff are the same people who would shut down his harvest. The miner has been told that the people who would clean up the air are the same people who would close his mine. Each of them has been handed an enemy, and the enemy is the other man. Both are paid for what they produce and neither is paid for what producing it costs, and that arrangement lasts, in part, because the two of them are kept in separate rooms with separate stories.

What the Bay and the hollow share is not a resource. It is a position. Both sit at the bottom of a watershed of value that flows somewhere else.

One country has handled this question differently. In the late 1960s Norway, a country with fewer people than the state of Virginia, found a finite resource under its part of the North Sea. Its leaders had seen what happened to other countries rich in resources: the money scattered, the costs piled up at home, and the next generation was left with the bill. They built an instrument instead, a set of rules that holds the value coming out of the seabed in trust for the people of Norway and for generations not yet born, in a fund with limits on what can be drawn from it. Against more pressure than is usually acknowledged, they have kept that fund intact for more than thirty years. The next chapter asks whether what Norway built for its oil could be built for the Bay, so that the value that has been leaving this watershed for two centuries might begin to stay.

For now, the boats are out at the mouth. The light has crossed the channel and reached the top of the Fort. A man two slips over has come in early, empty, and is hosing down. A truck door closes somewhere on the parking lot. The day is beginning. Within my father's lifetime, on this same water, a man stepped aboard at this hour with six bottles of Coca-Cola in a wire carrier and nothing else, because his lunch was waiting for him in the water, as fresh as it could be. It is the day a waterman has, on the Bay he has, with the accounting the country has agreed to do. It is not yet the day he could have had.
