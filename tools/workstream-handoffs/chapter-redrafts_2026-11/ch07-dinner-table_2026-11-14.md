# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 7: On Other Worlds

## The habitat

I want you to imagine a place that doesn't exist. It is a habitat on Mars, and I have made it up, along with the woman who is in charge of its water. Picture her at her desk on an ordinary morning. Someone has sent her a request, and she has to answer it.

Ten thousand people live in this habitat. It has been there for several decades, which is long enough for children to have been born there, grown up, and had children of their own. All of the habitat's water comes from three deposits of ice under the ground nearby. People drink that water. The crops in the growing domes are watered with it. The habitat also makes its oxygen from it, by splitting the water apart. If the habitat keeps using water at the rate it does now, the three deposits will last about fifty years.

The request is from a trading consortium, which is a group of companies that have gone into business together. The consortium wants her permission to take the ice out of the ground faster than the habitat needs it and to sell the extra. The numbers in the request add up. The consortium would earn back the money it put in within five years, and the habitat would get a share of the profit. Further into the request, on a later page, it says what the faster pace would do to the supply. The ice would last about twenty years instead of fifty.

Her job is to manage the habitat's resources, so this is her decision to make. Her office is two corridors away from the kindergarten. Every morning she walks past the growing domes on her way to work, and this morning she will pass them again on her way to meet the consortium. Her own grandchildren are among the ten thousand people who live there.

So this is the trade in front of her. The consortium is offering a profit that arrives within five years. In exchange, it is asking for thirty years of the habitat's water. What should she say to them? And how does she know that her answer is the right one?

## Her answer

She doesn't need a theory in order to answer. She needs to think through four things. I believe any careful person sitting in her chair would think through the same four.

The first thing is whether anything else could do what the water does. Nothing can. People have to drink it, the crops have to take it up, and the oxygen plant has to split it, and there is no other substance on Mars that can do any of those three jobs. She has read proposals for getting water in other ways. One would pull it out of the planet's thin atmosphere, and another would bring ice in from comets. But none of those ideas is working today at the scale that ten thousand people need, and she can't be sure any of them will be working twenty years from now. If the ice runs out, she will have nothing to replace it with.

The second thing is what the remaining water is worth as the supply goes down. If nobody lived on Mars, the ice under the ground would be more than anyone could use. For ten thousand people who have only three deposits, it is a limited supply. Every ton that is left matters more than the ton before it did, because fewer tons now stand between her people and the day the water is gone. At first the value of each ton rises slowly. Later it rises steeply. And at some point ahead, which she can't locate exactly, running low stops being a growing problem and becomes a disaster.

The third thing is what faster drilling would do to the water she is trying to keep. The fluids used in drilling can contaminate the ice that sits next to the ice being removed. Taking one deposit out quickly can crack the rock that holds the next deposit in place. Drilling also releases gases from the ground, and the habitat's air machines, which are already working hard, would have to clean those gases out. There is one more risk under that ground, and she has no way to put a number on it. Nobody knows whether anything is alive in the ice of Mars. If something is, it is the only life anyone has ever found beyond Earth, and careless drilling could wipe it out.

The fourth thing is who will be living in the habitat thirty years from now. On Earth, economists disagree about how much a cost should count today if it won't arrive for decades. Many of their methods shrink a cost like that until it almost disappears. She can't treat the future that way, because she knows exactly who will be there in thirty years. The children who are in the kindergarten today will be adults by then, with children of their own, and her grandchildren will be among them. To her, the future is not a figure that can be marked down. It is the same people she sees every day, a little older. They will need water in thirty years exactly as much as she needs it today.

When she puts those four things together, her answer is clear. She turns the consortium down and keeps the deposits in reserve. She also raises the price of water as the supply falls. That way people stop wasting it long before it runs short. And she spends money on recycling, so that each ton of water gets used many times over. The consortium's offer was honest, and the profit would have been real and fairly shared. But five years of profit is small next to what the habitat would lose. And the people who would lose it are the very people the profit was being offered to.

I don't think her answer surprised you. My guess is that you reached it before she had finished reading the request. That is the reason I put her on Mars. This book has been arguing two things. One is that when something shared gets used up, the price leaves out most of the cost. The other is that the cost ends up with people who had no say in the matter. A fair-minded reader could agree with every case so far and still have a doubt. Perhaps all of that is only a fact about politics on Earth. Perhaps it comes from lobbyists, from regulators who serve the industries they oversee, and from the particular deals struck over the last two hundred years. And perhaps a careful planner who started fresh, with no lobbyist anywhere near her, would count costs the same way the market does.

The administrator is that planner. Nobody is lobbying her, and she holds no ideology. Yet she counts the way this book counts. She asks what can't be replaced, what the remaining supply is worth, what the taking damages, and who will be there afterward. In her habitat, counting that way is so obviously right that it hardly seems like a method at all. So why isn't it obvious on Earth?

## One number at a time

To answer that, start with her situation exactly as I described it, and then change one thing about it.

Give her more ice. Suppose the habitat had three thousand deposits and not just three. In that case, taking a ton this year would make no real difference to how much was left. The pressure that made every remaining ton so valuable would be gone, and she could agree to more of what the consortium wants.

Now put her situation back the way it was and change something different. Suppose some other substance could do the work that water does, so that a habitat running short of ice could switch over to it. Then her decision would no longer trap the people living there thirty years from now, because they would have another option. Once again she could agree to more.

Next, suppose the drilling were clean, so that taking one ton did no damage to the next. Or suppose the people of thirty years from now really were going to be better supplied than she is, so that a ton would matter less to them than it does to her. Each of those changes would loosen her answer a bit more.

If you make all four changes at once, her careful way of counting and the consortium's simple way of counting give the same result. The reason is that the resource is now plentiful, something else can replace it, taking it does no harm, and the future is well supplied. Under those conditions the market's price is close to the whole cost. Nothing is left over to fall on anyone else, and the right answer is to go ahead.

Now make the changes in the opposite direction. Suppose the resource is scarce and nothing is sure to replace it. Suppose the damage from taking it lasts long after the taking has stopped, and the people of the future depend on it. Then the full cost rises far above the price the market sets, and the right answer becomes "only with great care," or simply "no." Notice that the four questions never changed through any of this. Only the facts changed, and as the facts changed, the answers changed with them.

Earth is one of those settings. It is not a different kind of place from her habitat. It is the same kind of place, and its numbers are milder. Coal shows this well. Earth has far more coal than her habitat has ice, but the coal is being used up and no new coal is being made. Other things can do coal's main job, which is to make electricity, and that counts in coal's favor. The damage from coal lasts for generations after a mine has closed. Miners get black lung. Acid water drains out of the old tunnels. The carbon from burning the coal stays in the air. And then there is the fourth question, about how much the people of the future should count. Economists on Earth have been fighting over that one for decades.

Their fight has two clear sides. In 2006 the British government published a review of the economics of climate change, led by Nicholas Stern. The review argued that a person's welfare should not count for less because that person is born later. It marked down future costs at a rate of about 1.4 percent a year. William Nordhaus, who built one of the most widely used economic models of the climate, argued for a higher rate, around 4 to 5 percent a year. He took that rate from what money actually earns when it is invested. The difference between the two rates looks small. But a rate like this compounds year after year, and over a century the difference decides whether a ton of carbon burned today comes with a large bill or a small one. There is an older idea behind both positions. In 1977 the economist John Hartwick showed what it would take to leave the people who come after us no worse off. A society that earns money from a resource that will run out has to reinvest that money. The bonds in this book are built on Hartwick's idea.

I am not going to settle the argument between Stern and Nordhaus in this chapter. I only want you to see why the administrator never has to have that argument. The people of the future in her habitat have faces, and she walks past them in the corridor. The people of the future on Earth are strangers to us, and it is easy to decide that a stranger counts for less.

So each of her four questions has an answer on Earth as well. The numbers here are milder than hers. They have been mild enough that for a hundred years nobody was forced to ask the questions at all. Still, if this way of counting only ever said "no," you would be right to distrust it. Does it ever say yes?

## Other worlds

It does say yes. The fastest way to show you is to take the four questions to a few more places. The places themselves are real. What I am imagining is that someone wants to mine them.

Start with an asteroid that is made mostly of iron, where nobody lives on it or anywhere near it. Iron is one of the most plentiful metals there is. Thousands of other asteroids contain it, and so does Earth. Mining this asteroid would not pollute any air or water, because the asteroid has no air or water. It would not force anyone out of a home. And nobody thirty years from now, or three hundred years from now, would run short of iron because this one rock had been mined. So all four questions come back with nothing in them. That means the full cost of the iron is about the same as whatever a buyer is willing to pay for it. The answer is to go ahead and mine it.

Next, go to the Moon, and ask about two different things that sit in the same ground. The first is the gray dust that covers the Moon's surface. A settlement there could pile that dust up as shielding, or bake it into bricks. There is more of it than anyone could ever use, and digging it up does very little harm apart from stirring up more dust. So the answer for the dust is to go ahead. The second thing is a rare form of helium called helium-3. The Sun has been driving it into the Moon's dust for billions of years. In 1986 a team of fusion engineers at the University of Wisconsin estimated that the Moon's surface holds about a million tons of it. Today almost nothing uses helium-3. But there is one design for a fusion reactor that would run on it. If that design is ever made to work, nothing else could take helium-3's place as its fuel. Nobody knows yet whether it will work.

Chapter 6 gave a rule for a case like this one, called the Asymmetric Regret Rule. The rule is for decisions where you can't know the odds. It says to compare the two ways you could turn out to be wrong, and to avoid the one that can't be undone. Apply it to the helium. Suppose we leave the helium where it is and fusion never arrives. Then we have lost nothing, because leaving it there cost nothing. Suppose instead we mine it and use it up on something minor, and then fusion does arrive. Then the fuel is gone for good. So the answer for helium-3 is to wait. That gives the Moon two answers. For its dust the answer is to go ahead, and for its helium the answer is the opposite. Both answers came from asking the same four questions about two different things.

A comet makes the same rule point in the other direction. Suppose a comet comes in from the far edge of the solar system and passes close enough to reach for a few months. It is carrying ice and other frozen material. After those few months it leaves, and it will not return for thousands of years. Taking material from it would harm no one and nothing. In this case, the mistake that can't be undone is waiting, because once the comet has gone, the chance has gone with it. So the answer is to take what is useful now, while it can be reached.

Europa is a harder case. Europa is one of Jupiter's moons. Under its shell of ice there is an ocean of liquid water, and that ocean is one of the few places in the solar system where scientists think life might exist. Suppose someone proposed to mine Europa's ice. Three of the four questions could be answered. The third one, about what the taking damages, could not. If something is living in that ocean and the mining destroyed it, what would that loss be worth? Any figure I gave you would be meaningless, so this book does not give one. It leaves that line of the account open, and it says plainly that the line is open. Leaving a line open does not mean the counting has to stop. One way of being wrong here could never be undone. The other way would cost nothing but time. So the answer is to wait, and to study the ocean first.

That is five answers from four places. For the asteroid's iron and the Moon's dust the answer was to go ahead. For the helium it was to wait. For the comet it was to take it now. For Europa it was to wait, because one of the costs can't be priced. If this way of counting were a creed, it would have said the same thing every time. It gave different answers because the places are different.

There is something else to notice about those places. Every chapter of this book so far has had a bill in it that one group gained from and another group was left to pay. On the asteroid and the comet there was no bill like that, because nobody was there to be left with it. So where does that kind of bill come from? And what makes it disappear from the accounts?

## The crew that breathes its own dust

To see where it comes from, go back to the habitat and give its people a second decision to make. Near the habitat, under the ground, there are rare earths, which are the metals that electronics depend on. The people of the habitat have two choices. They can go on importing their electronics from Earth. Or they can mine the rare earths and make their own.

Mining rare earths is dirty work, on Mars as it is on Earth. It produces fine dust and chemical waste. But think about where the dust would go. It would go into the one air system that everyone in the habitat breathes from. The waste would have to be stored inside the same sealed walls where everyone lives, because Mars has no river to carry it off and nowhere downstream for it to end up. A miner who got sick would be treated in the habitat's one small hospital, by the same doctors everyone else depends on. So the people who would gain from the mine are the same ten thousand people who would pay for it. And every one of them can see the whole of what it costs.

That means nothing in the habitat is severed. In Chapter 5 I gave the name severed cost to a cost that has been cut loose from the party that got the value and left with whoever couldn't refuse it. In the habitat there is nobody else to leave a cost with. The cost of the mine might be large. The people of the habitat might decide the metals are worth that cost, or they might decide they aren't. In both cases, the people making the decision are the people who will pay, and they are making it with the cost in plain view. When the same people gain and pay, and they know it, the unpaid part of the bill is zero.

Both parts of that last sentence are needed. To see why, suppose the dust carried a danger that nobody in the habitat was able to detect. They would then be paying a cost they had never agreed to pay. The unpaid part of the bill would no longer be zero, even though everyone was still living inside the same walls.

Now separate the people who gain from the people who pay, and the bill shows up. Imagine a different venture, which I am also making up. A group of investors on Earth pays for a mine on an asteroid. A crew of twelve people travels out to run the mine for ten years. The investors will receive the metal. The crew carries all the rest. They carry the chance of dying out there. They carry the chance that the company goes bankrupt and can't pay to bring them home. They carry whatever ten years in that place does to their health. And they carry whatever ten years away does to them and to the families they left behind.

Go through that list the way the administrator would go through it. The crew can see that the work is dangerous, and their contracts pay them extra because of it. So they have accepted that first cost with their eyes open, and it has been covered. The other three costs have not been covered. Nobody has set money aside to bring twelve people home if the mine goes bankrupt. Nobody has paid for the medical care they will need after they get back. As for the long absence, nobody can know what it will cost until the crew and their families have lived through it.

This is the point where the instrument this book proposes does its work, and it does that work before the first load of metal is shipped. Say that the mine expects to ship a thousand tons over the ten years. Estimate what the rescue and the medical care would cost, and divide that amount by a thousand. The result is what each ton owes. Every time a ton is shipped, that amount is paid into a reserve. The company can't touch the reserve, because a trustee holds it on the crew's behalf. If one of the costs turns out never to happen, its share of the reserve goes back to the investors. The fourth cost, the long absence, stays on the books as an open line. Because that line is open, the reserve is a minimum and not the full amount, and everyone involved knows that.

What I have just described is the new thing this book proposes, in a small example. It is a price for each act of extraction, with a reserve posted against that price in advance. The example also shows what has to be true before a cost can be severed. First, there has to be a second party to carry the cost. Second, if that party would object to carrying it, there has to be some way of keeping the cost out of sight. On Earth, that way of keeping a cost out of sight has a name.

## Distance

Its name is distance. There are four kinds of distance, and you have already seen each one of them in this book.

The first kind is distance in space. The coal of McDowell County was dug in West Virginia, and it was burned in steel mills and power plants far from the county. The people who got the steel and the electricity never saw the creeks in McDowell run orange. And the people who lived beside those creeks had no way to send them the bill.

The second kind is distance in time. Much of the cost of burning that coal will fall on people who have not been born yet. Those people can't object to it, and they can't bargain over it. By the time they are old enough to add up what it cost them, everyone who gained from it will be gone.

The third kind is social distance. This is the gap between people who live in the same place at the same time but do not have the same standing. In 2008 the banks and the families who lost their homes were in the same towns and lived under the same laws. But they were not equally able to make themselves heard, and they were not equally able to walk away.

The fourth kind is distance in what people know, and the closest example I have is my own. In Chapter 1 I told you about an apartment I rented because it cost three hundred dollars a month less. Before I signed, I tested the drive once, late at night, and it took forty-eight minutes. During working hours the trip took two and a half hours each way, and over a year the lease cost me thirteen hundred hours. Nobody hid those hours from me. I could have discovered them by making the drive once on a weekday morning. I didn't make that drive, because nothing on the listing or in the lease prompted me to ask the question. The person who gained from that lease was me, and the person who paid for it was also me. The only thing that separated the two was what I didn't know when I signed.

Two habits help keep that fourth kind of distance in place. The first is routine. Once a cost has been paid for long enough, people start calling it the cost of doing business, and after that they no longer see it as a cost at all. The second is plenty. When something seems to have no end, nobody bothers to count it. The air absorbed our smoke for two hundred years, and the oceans kept giving up their fish. For a long time it looked as though both could go on doing that forever.

You may have noticed that one thing is not on this list. I left it off on purpose. Force is not a kind of distance. Where people can simply be made to bear a cost, there is no need to hide the cost from anyone. So force is a way of assigning a cost, and not a way of concealing one. I come back to it at the end of this chapter.

Now look at the habitat again with those four kinds in mind. Nobody there is far away, because everyone lives inside one set of walls. The people of thirty years from now are in the kindergarten, two corridors from the administrator's office. Her own family breathes the air that she is responsible for. And nobody there can believe the water will last forever, because every ton of it has been counted. It is not that the four kinds of distance are weak in the habitat. They are not available there at all. A cost in the habitat can still be large, and the people there can still make a bad choice. But nothing can keep the cost where they can't see it.

Now turn that around. Imagine someone who grew up in that habitat and is visiting Earth for the first time. Our resources are not what she would notice first. She would notice that the air is free, and that nobody manufactures it or measures out how much each person uses. She would notice that water falls out of the sky. She would notice that the temperature stays within a livable range without any machine holding it there. She would notice that something overhead, which nobody has to maintain, blocks the worst of the Sun's radiation. Her habitat has to budget for every one of those things, down to each breath. Earth gives all of them away without anyone asking. It gives away so much that we have never had to learn what any of it costs. Seen that way, Earth's plenty is the widest distance we have.

So what separates her world from ours is not that her people are wiser than we are. Her world has no distance in it, and ours is full of distance. Distance is the shield this book sets out to take down. But nobody can take a shield down everywhere at the same time. Where should the work begin?

## What to hold back

The work should begin with the things that can't be replaced. The four questions do not treat every resource alike. The first question asks whether anything else can do the job, and it sorts resources more quickly than the other three do.

That first question has to be asked in two ways. One way is: can something else do this job today? The other is: could something else do every job that people might need this resource for over the next several hundred years, including jobs that nobody has thought of yet? For some resources the answer is the same both times. For other resources the two answers are far apart. I call the difference between the two answers the substitutability gap. Where that gap is wide, a society that intends to last should hold some of the resource back.

Coal is at the narrow end of the gap. Electricity can be made from sunlight, wind, moving water, nuclear power and the heat inside the Earth, and nobody has described a future that needs coal and can't use anything else. Sand, stone and ordinary iron ore are at the narrow end too. There are good reasons to leave coal in the ground, and they are the damage it does and the carbon it releases. The risk of running out of it is not one of those reasons.

Oil, copper and zinc are next. Most of the jobs they do today could be done by something else. And no need that we can foresee in the distant future depends on them alone.

Uranium, lithium and cobalt are in the middle of the range. Each of them has a possible replacement that researchers can already see. For uranium it is thorium. For lithium it is sodium. For cobalt it is batteries based on iron. But none of those replacements has been proven at full scale. And some uses in the future may turn out to need exactly these metals.

The rare earths and the platinum family of metals are at the wide end. They have particular magnetic and chemical properties. Those properties are what make it possible to build small powerful motors, fuel cells and advanced electronics. For some of the ways these metals are used today, substitutes exist. Nobody knows how to find substitutes for all of the ways they are used. And the technologies of the next few hundred years are more likely than not to need the same properties.

Helium-3 doesn't fit anywhere on the range. If fusion reactors ever run on it, it belongs at the very top of the list. If they never do, it is only a curiosity.

This ranking answers only the first of the four questions. For each resource, someone still has to ask how much of it is left and what taking it damages. But the ranking does tell a society where to look first. And the metals at the wide end of the list turn out to be the same metals that go into the motors, magnets and batteries the world needs in order to move away from coal and oil. Chapter 9 takes up what follows from that. The world is about to dig up very large amounts of the things it can least afford to waste. It has a chance to price them correctly starting with the first ton.

## Two fair objections

By this point a careful reader will have two objections. I think both of them are fair.

The first objection is that Mars is too simple to teach us anything. A sealed habitat has ten thousand people, three deposits of ice and one administrator. If she gets the water wrong, people die within ten years, and everybody knows the reason. Earth is nothing like that. It has eight billion people. Its resources have been only partly mapped. New deposits and new inventions keep changing which things are scarce. And when harm comes, it comes slowly and shows up far from whatever caused it. So this reader says that a lesson learned in the simple place proves nothing about the complicated one.

I agree that Earth is more complicated. But consider what the complication actually changes. It changes how well we know the answers to the four questions. On Earth, the amount of a resource that is left is an estimate. People dispute what could replace it. The damage is hard to trace back to its source. And as I said, economists argue over how much the future should count. None of that changes the questions themselves. In both places something is being used up faster than it comes back. In both places some people gain from that and other people pay for it. And in both places the price either includes the cost or leaves it out.

Physics teachers do the same thing when they use a frictionless plane, which is a surface with no friction at all. No surface like that exists. But when a block slides across one, a student can see the law of motion working with nothing getting in its way. Once she has seen the law there, she can put the friction back and understand a real block on a real ramp. Every working science keeps a few impossible objects like that one. It uses them to check whether its reasoning gives the right answer in a case where nobody doubts what the right answer is. The habitat is this book's frictionless plane. Suppose the four questions had told the administrator to sell the water. You would have known immediately that something was wrong with the questions.

The second objection comes from the opposite direction. On the asteroid, this way of counting agreed with the market. So this reader asks two things. Where the market is already right, doesn't the framework add nothing? And where the framework disagrees with the market, why should anyone believe the framework?

It is true that on the asteroid the framework adds nothing, and that is what it should do there. The market's price for that iron is correct, because mining it damages nothing, forces nobody out, and leaves no one in the future poorer. Think of a measuring tool. If it disagreed with a reading that was correct, it would be broken. If it agreed with every reading it was ever compared to, it would be useless. This tool agrees with the market on the asteroid's iron and on the Moon's dust. It departs from the market on the habitat's water, on helium-3, and on a ton of coal. And in each of those three cases you can point to the particular question that made the difference.

That leaves one more objection, and it is the oldest of them. Perhaps distance and unpaid bills are products of modern industry, or of modern markets. Did they begin there?

## Older than any of our systems

They did not begin there. The same arrangement shows up in two civilizations that never knew about each other, and it shows up thousands of years before there was anything we would call an industry or a market.

The pyramids at Giza were built around 2550 BCE. The whole complex took about seventy years to build. The largest pyramid, which was built for the king Khufu, took about twenty. For a long time people imagined that slaves had built them under the whip. Then Mark Lehner led excavations in the town where the workers lived, and Zahi Hawass led excavations in the cemetery where they were buried, and what they found was different. The builders were skilled craftsmen, working alongside farmers who were called up in rotation. They were fed from the king's stores. When they were injured, their injuries were treated. When they died, they were buried with care, close to the monument they had worked on.

But they were not free to say no. The king's economy was the only economy that existed. There was no other employer to work for and no other place to go. The king received what the pyramid was built to give him, which was his standing among the living and his provision for the afterlife. The laborers gave their years and their bodies.

Twenty-three centuries later, on a different continent, Qin Shi Huang conquered the warring states of China and became its first emperor. He ordered two things built. One was a wall along the northern frontier, which ran about five thousand kilometers. The other was a tomb for himself, near what is now the city of Xi'an. In 1974 farmers there found the buried clay soldiers that are now famous, and those soldiers are one small part of that tomb. The historian Sima Qian wrote about a century after the Qin dynasty fell. He reported that three hundred thousand to five hundred thousand conscripts worked on the wall at one time, and that more than seven hundred thousand worked on the tomb. They were farmers, prisoners and convicts, and the histories record that great numbers of them died. The Qin state made no effort to disguise its philosophy. It set quotas, and it punished anyone who failed to meet them. Within four years of the emperor's death, rebellions had brought the dynasty down. The wall and the clay soldiers are still there.

I am not going to put a price on what those laborers were owed, in Egypt or in China. Nowhere in this book do I write down a dollar figure for forced labor.

But look at what the two cases have in common. Egypt and Qin China had different gods and different systems of writing. They had different ideas about what a ruler was. Neither one knew the other existed. Yet in both, one group of people gained and another group paid, and the cost went to whoever could not refuse it. And in both, almost nothing was hidden. The laborers knew what the work was costing them. The work was done out in the open, at the foot of the monument. There was no distance in space or in knowledge to hide behind. None was needed, because the people who bore the cost had no power to refuse it. That is what I meant earlier by force. Force doesn't hide a cost. It assigns one, in plain view.

So this arrangement was not produced by capitalism, or by any other system that people argue about today. It is older than all of them. Every age has run it through whatever system that age happened to have. The pyramids and the wall have lasted longer than every one of those systems.

This means the administrator's question was never hers alone. She asked what a thing costs when everything is counted, and who will be there to pay for it. She was able to answer in a single morning, because in her habitat nothing stood between the people who would gain and the people who would pay. On Earth a great deal stands between them. Here the only way to answer her question is to do the counting by hand, one cost at a time.
