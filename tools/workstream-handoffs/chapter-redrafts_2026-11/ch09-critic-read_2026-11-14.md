# Ch 9 draft B: one strongest-critic read (2026-11-14)

**Status:** PROPOSED (internal). Run once, on draft B, by the session that wrote it. A hostile but fair reader: an
economist or a trade lawyer who has read Chapters 1 to 8 and wants the book to fail on its own terms. Each item is
where that reader would stop, put as a question for the author. Nothing here has been changed in the drafts.

1. **"I take no position," and then the chapter leans.** Section 1 says the book takes no position on the seabed
   ruling. Then Classify puts the seabed "about as high in the high bin as anything on Earth," Reserve notes it
   already has a reserve, and Reassess says a decision to mine "cannot" be revisited. A fair reader will say the
   chapter argues for waiting while claiming not to. *Do you want the lean kept and the disclaimer dropped, the
   disclaimer kept and the four returns to the seabed made even-handed, or the returns cut to one?*

2. **Who holds the bond, and who says the cost is "made good"?** Section 6 says the bond is "held for whoever
   bears the cost" and "released when the cost has been made good." An economist will ask at once who holds the
   money, who certifies the repair, and what stops the holder from spending it. The chapter's own diamond test
   (section 8) asks the same thing of everyone else. The Chapter 7 draft answers the first for its invented mine ("a trustee"), and section 6 now points to it. *Should section 6 answer those three for a real mine, or say plainly that
   the book leaves them to each country, or point to the appendix?* (The five features need checking against the
   appendix's definitions either way.)

3. **Sweden in 1992 is not about households.** The 2008 paragraph is about families who lost homes. The 1992
   paragraph is about shareholders taking the loss. The link between them is the reading "that is the bond's logic
   applied after the fact," which is the session's, and the "close to zero" cost is one economist's judgment
   against other estimates near two percent of a year's output. *Is that reading yours? If not, does Sweden shrink
   to the 1930s sentence that Chapter 5 already has?*

4. **Chapter 5 promises more than this chapter gives on Public Choice.** Chapter 5 says Chapter 9 "develops this
   pairing at length" and addresses readers at four named institutions. The map cut the pairing to one paragraph.
   *Does Chapter 5's promise change, or does this paragraph grow?*

5. **The answer to the ladder is a design nobody has built.** The European charge gives no longer timetable to
   poorer exporters as a matter of principle and sends no money the other way. The draft says an honest charge
   "does two things," and a trade lawyer will ask where one exists. *Should the paragraph say outright that no
   border charge yet does either, and that this is the part still missing?*

6. **Sections 10 and 11 are a run of names.** Coase, Ostrom, Nozick, Locke, Polanyi, Fraser, Christophers twice,
   Harvey, Ostrom again, Susskind, Gardiner: a dozen names in the last two sections, after nine sections with few.
   You wrote "how should we fix this?" beside the same pattern in Chapter 5. Susskind gets one sentence that says
   little. *Which of these does the chapter need at this length, which could be a sentence, and should the two
   objections of right come before the pieces that exist, so the chapter ends on what is real?*

7. **The opening will date.** "Set to resume in March 2027," "by January 2026," "87… in its 2026 report." Each is
   true today and each will read as old news in the printed book; one ruling in Kingston or Washington changes the
   last two paragraphs of section 1. *Do you accept a section that has to be updated at copyedit, or would you
   keep the 1979 strip and the 2023 return (which will not change) and cut the account of the talks to two
   sentences?*
