# Chapter 10. Common Bonds

It is a long time before sunrise, and the first thing that arrives is not light. It is the sound of men going to work.

Somewhere down the line of slips a diesel coughs, catches and settles into its idle. A line that has slapped a mast all night goes quiet. The sound crosses the water to me as if the water were carrying it, and in a way it is. Behind the slips the Fort is dark. The Bay is one shade of gray, opening very slowly into another. The channel marker blinks red on its own schedule whether or not anyone is watching. North of Newport News a tug is working, and I can hear it long before I will see it.

I am on the deck of a sailboat, in a slip on the harbor side of Old Point Comfort. I am awake because the work boats are starting and nobody sleeps through them. I wouldn't want to.

This is the last chapter, and I came out here to write it, the way I have come out here for years to do the things that matter most to me. The chapter is what I have to say. The morning is what I came here to listen to.

## The sentence

Nine chapters stand in front of this one, and all nine say one thing.

Value leaves the place it comes from, and the costs of its leaving stay behind.

That sentence is the framework. The chapters are the unfolding of it. I unfolded it against the coal of West Virginia, and against the oysters of the Chesapeake, half a mile from where I am sitting. I unfolded it against the rare earths of Inner Mongolia, the oil of the North Sea and the Niger Delta, the water of the Ogallala, and the water ice of Mars. The resources are different, and so are the centuries and the oceans, and the same sentence runs under all of them.

What I wanted was to make the sentence count, which meant building it well enough to hold weight. Everything else in the book serves that. So why did I need to write it at all?

## Why I came here to write it

I didn't set out to write a book. I set out, in middle age, to understand a few things that would not leave me alone.

The first was the ledgers. For years I sat in rooms where the numbers were honest about everything except the one thing that mattered. A cost that landed on someone out of sight was simply not on the page. The page balanced anyway, and everyone in the room agreed that it balanced. I came to distrust a balanced page.

The second was my parents, in Hampton, getting older the way parents do, all at once and then steadily. Caring for them is work. It appears on no ledger I know of, and it is real labor all the same.

## The room

If I let myself, I can put them all in one room.

A miner in southern West Virginia is pulling on his boots in the dark. A waterman from the lower Bay has been crabbing since he was twelve, and he runs a mechanic shop in the afternoons because the water alone will no longer carry a family. A woman in Baotou has a kitchen window that looks out on a tailings pond. A fisherman in the Niger Delta has a son who will not fish the creek. A farmer in the Texas Panhandle is watching the irrigation pivots come off his field for the last time. There is a rural nurse, a warehouse worker on the third shift, and a long-haul driver thirty nights from his own bed.

They don't know each other, and most of them would have nothing to say if they met. The miner has never heard of Baotou. The waterman has no quarrel with the farmer.

But the same mechanism took from every one of them. The value each of them helped produce went somewhere else, and the cost of producing it stayed where they are. Nobody has told them that this is something they share. It is a kinship that has never been spoken in that room, and this book exists to speak it.

I add one more person, because I spent a chapter on Mars and I am not going to be coy about it now. She is a habitat administrator with ten thousand people in her charge, and she has been asked to speed up the extraction of subsurface water to keep the settlement growing. Cost severance is not impossible on Mars. It is impossible to hide. In a sealed dome there is no away. There is no downwind. There is no later generation in another country who will absorb the loss out of sight. Everyone who would bear the cost is in the room, breathing the same recycled air. Everyone breathes the same air; everyone drinks the same drawn-down water; the cost-bearer and the value-capturer are in the same room by physical necessity.

On Earth the mechanism is the same. The room is larger, and the lighting in it is poor. So what is it, exactly, that keeps the people in the room from seeing one another?

## What keeps it out of sight

What keeps them apart is distance, and the cases in this book turned up four kinds of it.

The first kind is geographic. The value goes to one place and the cost stays in another, far enough apart that nobody sees the two together. McDowell County's coal left the county that way.

The second kind is distance in time. People alive now make the decision, and people born later absorb what it costs, long after the ones who decided are beyond anyone's reach.

The third kind is social. The person who gains and the person who pays can work in the same building, as my colleague in Chapter 1 and his employers did, and one of them still has no seat in the room where the decision is made.

The fourth kind is informational. One side knew what was happening, and the other side did not know in time. The company measured the asbestos, and the family breathed the dust. The collapse of 2008 ran on this kind above all. Two things keep informational distance in place for a very long time. One is routine: a cost becomes normal so slowly that people stop pricing it as a cost and start calling it the cost of doing business. The other is apparent abundance, the working belief that a resource is endless, which holds until the math turns over. The atmosphere absorbed two centuries of carbon under that belief, and it is the longest-running illusion of them all.

Power is different from all four. It doesn't hide a cost. It decides who receives one. Capital can move and a poisoned town can't, so the party with no ability to refuse is the party that pays. The pyramids were built that way.

Our instruments answer these one at a time. A border adjustment closes part of the geographic distance. A sovereign wealth fund closes part of the distance in time. Disclosure requirements close part of the informational distance, and so does a price on carbon, because it ends part of the pretense that the atmosphere is endless. Each of these is real, and none of them reaches all of it.

The whole strategy is to bring the person who pays and the person who gains closer to standing in the same room, because the closer they stand, the harder the severance is to keep up. That strategy depends on an instrument that measures the distance truly. I built one, and a reader is entitled to ask why it should be trusted.

## The model maker

My grandfather, the man my family called Pooh, worked twelve miles up the road from this slip, at NASA Langley. He was a model maker in the wind tunnels.

He built scale aircraft and spacecraft out of wood and metal and resin, so that the tunnel could tell the truth about a full-size design that did not yet exist. His patented work extended the discipline past the tunnel itself. He did small repairs in the workshop behind his house, and I spent time there as a boy, watching a man make a small true thing stand in for a large one.

His whole craft rests on a single demand: build the model faithfully enough that reality will not lie when it is run against your work. A model that flatters its designer is worse than no model at all, because it fails in the air. Reality does not negotiate there, and the cost of the error falls on people who can't recover from it.

The framework in this book is a model in that lineage. I am the grandson of a model maker, working in a different shop with different materials and at a different scale, and what I inherited from him is what I used to build it. I built it to his demand. A model built to that demand also has to say what it can't do.

## What the model can't do, and what it can

It can't settle the discount rate. How much should a cost that falls on someone not yet born weigh against a benefit that lands today? Economists have argued over that for decades without agreeing. Nordhaus discounts the future at a higher rate and gets a smaller number. Stern discounts it at a lower rate and gets a larger one.

The model doesn't need to settle that argument, and for too long I left this part out. Its two results are that the severance is real and greater than zero, and that a fraction of a hard-to-substitute resource is worth reserving. Both hold across the whole range from Nordhaus to Stern. A reader can discount the future at the steepest rate anyone defends, and the conclusion still stands. For the longest horizons Weitzman proposed a rate that declines over time, and the framework adopts his approach directly, which bends its own estimates downward. So the model takes the most conservative discounting it can defend, and it still finds the cost.

It can't price what is not denominated in any currency: the ocean under the ice of Europa, or a sacred landscape. The model prices what can be priced, says plainly what can't be, and never invents a number so that it will look complete.

It can't predict when a substitute will arrive. It can say that a resource is hard to substitute, and that the gap is therefore costly to foreclose. It can't name the year a laboratory closes that gap.

And it can't resolve the tension between a present need and a future one. A community that needs the income now is making a real claim. So is a future that needs the resource intact. The model can show what each claim costs. It can't say which claim is right, because that is a moral choice, and it stays one.

What the model can do is make the second transaction visible. Every extraction is two transactions. One is on the page: the price paid for the coal or the fish or the ore. The other is off the page: the cost severed onto whoever could not refuse it. The model names that second transaction and gives it a number, in a vocabulary a person can use.

Once the second transaction is visible, the arithmetic of prevention is not frightening. Reserving a fraction of a hard-to-substitute resource costs only the revenue given up on that fraction. Capturing the rents in a sovereign fund costs the discipline to maintain the fund, and Norway has shown that a country can do it. A border adjustment costs administrative complexity. None of these is large when it is set beside the foreclosure it prevents. The insurance is cheap compared with having none, and that is most of the argument.

The framework's job is to make the work conceivable. The rest belongs to whoever picks up the instrument. And the instrument does not only point forward.

## Backward, and the account it can't reach

The same model that prices what will be extracted in 2027 can price what was extracted from a community in 1860, or 1960, or 1995. The accounting doesn't care which direction time runs. It asks only whether the cost was severed.

Should that pricing lead to compensation, or restitution, or structural reform, or some other form of acknowledgment? The framework does not answer. It insists only that the accounting be honest, because no answer that rests on a dishonest accounting can be honest. The framework names the column and does not fill it. There are harms I have no standing to price, and I leave the figure to those who do.

And there is one account the framework does not reach at all.

In 1868 the Treaty of Fort Laramie guaranteed the Black Hills, Ȟe Sápa, to the Lakota Sioux. The Great Sioux Reservation named the Hills inside its boundary. Six years later the Custer expedition entered the Hills and found gold. Three years after that, in February of 1877, an Act of Congress took the Hills away. That Act was the formal severance of a sacred landscape.

In 1980 the Supreme Court ruled, in *United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians*, that the 1877 taking had been unconstitutional. The Court awarded roughly $17.5 million, which was the land's value in 1877, plus interest since 1877 at five percent. By 1980 the award had grown to something like $106 million. The Lakota refused it. The money has sat in escrow ever since, drawing interest, and the trust account now holds more than $1.5 billion. It has been refused for more than four decades.

The framework can't reach this. Money does not substitute for that land, at any amount or for any purpose. The Black Hills sit in the category the model can't price. The framework assigns no number to that taking, and I do not, and I do not say who else may.

The framework operates inside a settlement whose original wound it can't heal, and it does not pretend to. The miner pulling on his boots and the waterman in his shop are not in the same position as the descendants of the people who held Ȟe Sápa under treaty before the gold was found. Saying so does not rank the harms. It is accurate about them.

A reader who wants to understand what the framework can't reach should read the people whose question it is: Robin Wall Kimmerer, of the Citizen Potawatomi; Leanne Betasamosake Simpson, of the Michi Saagiig Nishnaabeg; Glen Coulthard, of the Yellowknives Dene; Roxanne Dunbar-Ortiz; and Vine Deloria Jr., of the Standing Rock Sioux. They are asking a different shape of the question this book asks, and a deeper one.

There is a fair objection to what I have just done. Setting Indigenous dispossession beside a fishery's collapse and a mine's tailings, as cases of one mechanism, risks flattening a thing that should not be flattened. I take that risk seriously. The framework's claim is about structure. It is not a claim that the harms are morally equal. What was done to Indigenous peoples in North America was not an aberration of the American system. It was that system operating as it was designed to operate, visible at its most severe. Naming how general the mechanism is does not shrink the particular wound. It makes clear what kind of wound it is. The Black Hills account is the one that holds the framework honest, and it is not the only test the framework has to meet.

## Three objections

Out here before light, with no one to perform for, I can hear the objections more clearly than I can at a desk. Three of them are real attacks on what I have built.

The first comes from my allies, and that is what makes it sharp. A reader on the left, who sees what I see in these cases, will tell me the book does not go far enough. Honest pricing, strategic reserves and accountability bonds will not save us, this reader says, because a system whose logic drives it to extract until nothing is left will absorb the reform and keep extracting, a little more slowly and with a cleaner conscience. The framework is exactly the kind of thing a carbon-neutral multinational adopts to justify carrying on. What is needed is a different relationship to the resource, and a better price is not that.

The people who say this are right about what they see. The framework can be captured. It can be softened into an ESG line in a quarterly report. Carbon pricing has been captured. So have sovereign wealth funds, and so have reclamation bonds in almost every place they exist. The conflict-free certificate that delivers a premium to the retailer and nothing to the miner is the pattern, and I have watched it run.

But this book is not an argument for capitalism, and it is not an argument against it. The model measures what extraction costs. A state-owned mine that severs costs is still severing costs, and so is a worker cooperative that does it. Feudal lords severed costs. The pharaohs severed costs to raise the pyramids, and the Qin emperors severed them too. The mechanism belongs to no single form of ownership, and it is older than the one my critic wants to indict. Capitalism is the vehicle that delivers it now, and it is the most efficient one yet built. It is not the cause. Treating it as the cause does the same damage as treating it as the cure, because both pull the conversation toward political vocabulary while the mechanism keeps running underneath the argument.

The second objection goes deeper than capture, and I have turned it over more than any other. It says the instrument is the disease. By pricing the commons and writing the obligation down as a bond, I am extending the market into the last things it had not reached. A reader with Polanyi in hand will say I have manufactured one more fictitious commodity. A reader with Pistor in hand will say that a bond is one of the legal modules capital is assembled from, so coding the commons as a bond is coding it as capital. I met Pistor earlier in the book as a friend, a diagnostician on my side. The honest thing is to admit that the same diagnosis can be turned around and aimed at me. Christophers would add that a bonded commons hands the rentier one more asset to capture. And Michael Sandel, who is my ally everywhere else in this book, warns that putting a number on the priceless already concedes the extractor's frame.

Chapter 5 answered Polanyi's readers and Sandel in full, and Chapter 9 answered Christophers, so here I give only the center of the answer. Accounting for a cost and coding an asset are opposite acts that happen to share the word "price." The bond creates nothing to own or to trade. It records a debt and keeps that debt visible until it is paid. And the choice was never between a sacred thing left unpriced and a sacred thing reduced to a number. It is a choice between two numbers, because the thing already has a number, and that number is zero. Zero is what made the extraction profitable. Where a thing can't be given a number at all, as with the ocean under Europa's ice and the Black Hills, the model says so and invents none. I concede what I conceded to the first reader: an institution that wanted to could rebrand a commons bond into something tradable, the way carbon credits were. The design guards against that, and so does the tradition the design comes from, but keeping it from happening is political work.

The third objection comes from the other direction. It comes from my parents' world, from the conservative friends they have in Hampton. I respect it because they are real people and not a strawman. The framework, this reader says, is a Trojan horse for the state. Accountability bonds, strategic reserves, sovereign wealth funds and border adjustments add up to a vast new apparatus of government control over private extraction, a central planner's wish list dressed as accounting. Price every externality and you have written a license for unlimited government reach. "Honest accounting" is the thin edge of a wedge that ends with the state owning the commons it claimed only to be measuring. And the agencies will be captured, as the book itself documents.

My answer starts where the first one ended. The framework legislates nothing. It measures what extraction costs. A society can take that measurement and build a tax, or a bond posted with a private trustee, or a market in tradable reserve credits, or a contract between private parties, or a sovereign fund, or nothing at all. That is a political question, and the framework does not decide it. A conservative can take the same figure and argue for a market instrument, and a progressive can take it and argue for a public fund.

Honest accounting is also a conservative value before it is anyone else's. A sound balance sheet requires every cost to be priced honestly, and a hidden liability is a fraud whether a government hides it or a company does. The framework hands the center-right reader a demand for honest books, which is the discipline conservatives ask of the state and of companies alike.

On capture I concede the point in both directions. The apparatus can be captured by the regulatory state, just as it can be softened into an ESG slogan. The left's example is the conflict-free certificate. The right's example is the minerals agency owned by the industry it was built to watch. The answer to both is the same. Every instrument runs the risk of capture. The guard is the design, which accounts for costs and does not code assets, and after the design the guard is politics.

So I have friends on the left who will find me too modest about institutional reform, and my parents have friends who will find me too generous to it. Both are asking the framework to take a political side it was not built to take. The model measures, and taking sides is the reader's work. That work has a deadline.

## The hinge

Step back far enough, and something becomes clear about the moment that you and I are alive in.

We are the first generation to know, with the numbers in hand, that the resources are finite. We are the first to have the technology for substitutes, however partial. We are the first with the institutional capacity to do something, scattered and incomplete as that capacity is. And we are very likely the last generation with meaningful leverage, before the costs harden into facts that no instrument can soften.

The carbon clock is the easiest of the clocks to read, and it is not the only one. The aquifer drawdown is a clock. So are the fishery collapse and the loss of soil, and so is the depletion of the hard-to-substitute resources that the renewable transition itself depends on. The decision points are not out in the next century. They are in this one, and several of them are in this decade.

That is the hinge. I mean it as a description of where the leverage is and of how briefly it will last.

## Two readings

The title of this book can be read two ways.

*Commons Bonds.* These are the obligations a participant owes to the commons. The bond is the account that keeps the debt visible until it is paid, and it is the instrument this book has built.

*Common bonds.* These are the bonds among the people the value was taken from. The miner, the waterman, the woman in Baotou and the farmer watching the pivots come off his field share a kinship that none of them was told about. They did not choose the position the mechanism put them in, and the bonds of a position you did not choose are bonds all the same.

The two readings are one reading. The obligation owed to the commons and the kinship among the people who paid are the same fact, named once from the side of what is owed and once from the side of who was made to pay.

## The morning

The first gray is in the sky now. The channel marker goes on blinking, and the dawn has caught up to it.

Below, the dog stirs at the foot of the companionway. She lifts her head, decides it is not yet time, and puts it back down.

There is a kettle to put on, and after that a day made of small things. There is nursing coursework, taken up late in a working life, that I will sit with at the table. There are hours at the hospital, learning the bedside work that the framework has nothing to teach me about. There is desk work on the book. There is the call to my parents, who pick up on the first ring or the second. There is the tutoring office, and the first-generation college students who come through it and remind me what a window opening looks like from the inside. And if the day allows, there is an hour on the foredeck with a book.

The line between the work and the life has grown shorter over the years. I have tried to keep it short enough that my attention stays attached to the people it was meant for. That is most of what I have learned about how to live, and the framework taught me none of it.

## The compass

Camus closed *Le Mythe de Sisyphe*, in 1942, with a sentence I have carried for a long time. *Il faut imaginer Sisyphe heureux.* One must imagine Sisyphus happy.

Sisyphus is not happy because the work is finished. It is never finished, and the stone always rolls back down. He is happy because the work is his and he has chosen it. He walks back down the hill seeing the stones and knowing the weight, and he puts his shoulder to the boulder again.

The framework is not the boulder, and it is not the hill. It does not flatten the slope, lighten the load or grant rest. It tells the walker where north is. A compass is not the journey. It is how a person who has to keep walking can know whether the direction is right. The framework's honesty does not lift the work. It tells the walker that the work, when it is done, will have been done in the right direction.

The men out in the slips already hold compasses. They do not hold the framework, which they have never needed and never read. They hold something older. They know honest weather from weather that lies, and they carry that knowledge in their hands when they need it and in their bodies when they don't. They did not need the framework before it existed, and they will not need it after it is gone. What it offers the rest of us is the chance to see what the men on the dock have seen all along, and the chance to do something with it.

## Let us begin

Naming a thing is the beginning of the work.

The work that follows is long. It is institutional and it is generational, and most of it will be done by people I will never meet, with instruments I will never see, after those instruments have replaced the ones I have described. This book does not finish the work. It insists that the accounting be honest, so that whatever follows can be honest too.

The light is full enough now to see the far shore. The work boats are out past the marker. Somewhere down the line a man I will never meet is hauling pots in water my grandfather might have known.

The kettle is on. The dog is up. The day is made of small attentions, and they are waiting.

Let us begin.
