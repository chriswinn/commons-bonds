# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 2: The Miner

---

In May of 1960 a young senator drove through McDowell County, West Virginia. He was running in a primary election, and he had come to ask people for their votes. West Virginia was a poor state, and McDowell was the poorest part of it. The county is made of hollows, which are narrow valleys between steep hills. The road he drove ran right beside the creek, because the bottom of a hollow is the only place flat enough to put a road.

Along that road he saw children standing in doorways, and their bellies were swollen the way children's bellies swell when they don't get enough to eat. He saw cabins stacked with the surplus cheese and flour that the government handed out. Families lived on that food because there was no work, no money, and nothing else coming in. He saw men sitting on porches in the middle of a working day. Some of those men had carried rifles in a war and come home to a country that had won it. The richest coal on the continent lay under their feet, and they were the ones who had cut it out of the ground with their bodies. Now they had nothing to do but sit.

The coal under McDowell was metallurgical coal. That is the hard, hot-burning kind that steel mills cannot run without, so this coal became the steel in rails, ships, bridges and cities. At its peak the county shipped more of it than anywhere else in the United States. If you measured McDowell by what came out of its ground, it was rich. If you measured it by how the people who dug that coal lived, it was the poorest place in one of the poorest states in the country.

Four months later, in September, the senator gave a speech in Canton, Ohio. He was John F. Kennedy, and he told the crowd what he had seen in West Virginia. He described the want, the men with no work and the children, and he did not exaggerate any of it, because he had seen it with his own eyes. Then he said who was to blame. He blamed the people a candidate usually blames: his opponent, the administration in power, and a policy that had failed.

He had the suffering right. He had the wrong defendant.

The men on those porches had not been robbed by a political party or by a policy. They had been emptied out by an arrangement so ordinary that nobody had a name for it. The arrangement took the value of the coal they dug and sent that value up and out of the county. It left the cost of digging the coal behind, in their lungs, their creeks and their children. Kennedy was looking at what that arrangement had done. He could not point at the arrangement itself, and nobody else could either, because it had no name. So imagine a courtroom with an empty chair where the defendant should be sitting. What belongs in that chair?

---

The last chapter ended with a ledger and a set of empty columns. McDowell fills in the first of those columns, and it does that by answering one small question. What did a ton of that coal actually cost?

I don't mean the price. The price is easy to find, because it is in the records. In 1960 the coal sold at the mouth of the mine for just under five dollars a ton. That is what the buyer paid the seller, and it is the number most people mean when they say what something costs. But the price and the cost are two different things. The cost is the price plus everything the price left out. You can think of what was left out as a bill that never came.

In the last chapter I said I had options that a coal miner does not have. This is the miner I meant. I had skills that several employers wanted, and I was free to move between those employers. The miner had a hole in the ground and a county with no other work in it, and he had a body that the work would use up. A constrained choice is still a choice. It is not a free one. So I want to write down what his choice cost. I will take it one column at a time, the way you would read a bill you did not know you had run up.

---

The first column is written in a man's lungs.

Robert Bailey worked underground. When coal is cut, dust comes off the coal face and off the machines doing the cutting. The dust is fine enough to float in the air, and a miner breathes it into the deepest tissue of his lungs. The body has no way to clear dust from that deep, so it builds scar tissue around it. The scarring keeps spreading, and eventually the lung can no longer do the one thing a lung does. Doctors call this disease pneumoconiosis, and miners call it black lung. When Bailey tried to say what it felt like, he said it was almost like drowning.

Black lung is a slow disease. A wound hurts a man quickly, but black lung wears him down over years, the way water wears down stone. By the end Bailey needed a double lung transplant, which meant two new lungs to replace the two the coal had filled. He got the transplant, and it kept him alive. To stay alive he had to take drugs that stop the body from rejecting an organ that is not its own.

The company Bailey had worked for went bankrupt. A company exists as a legal shell, and when it goes bankrupt that shell dissolves. The obligations the company held inside the shell dissolve along with it. One of those obligations was the company's duty to the man whose lungs it had used. But a debt that real does not just disappear. It moved onto a federal fund, and that means it moved onto the public. The public is made up of people who never dug the coal, never bought the steel, and never sat in the room where somebody chose to mine it cheaply. So Bailey's body carried the disease and the public carried the bill. The company carried neither one, because by then it was only a name on a closed file.

Bailey testified before Congress twice. He went to talk about the backlog of black lung claims, which meant men were waiting and dying while their paperwork moved through the system. He told Congress that most laws have been written with blood.

The history of the coalfields shows what he meant. For years black lung was denied as a real disease. In 1968 miners across the coalfields walked out in a wildcat strike, which is a strike the union has not approved, to force the question, and the Black Lung Association carried their demand into law. <!-- FACT FLAG 2026-11-14: "1968" is the current chapter's date. When that union stopped fighting for them, the movement that became Miners for Democracy fought to take it back. These miners won real things. What they did not have was the power to change the arrangement itself, because a miner can win a strike and still have no way to put a price on a lung.

Bailey died in February of 2019, twenty-eight days after he gave his last interview. He died of cancer, and the cancer was brought on, in part, by the drugs that had kept his transplanted lungs alive. So coal took his lungs, and then the medicine that replaced them helped take the rest of him. No invoice anywhere has a column for that.

I am about to give you numbers, and Bailey is not one of them. I will not turn him into one. He is the reason the numbers matter.

Black lung is not a disease of the past. It is rising again, and it is rising fastest among younger miners, the men with fewer than twenty years underground. The reason is that the newer machines cut faster, and they throw a finer and more dangerous dust. Central Appalachia is the coal country of Kentucky, Virginia and West Virginia, and there around one in five miners with twenty-five years or more underground has the disease. Each man who dies of it loses between eight and thirteen years of the life he would otherwise have had. Those are the years at the end of his life, when he has the fewest left.

A federal program pays benefits to miners with black lung, and through 2009 it had paid out around $44 billion. The real total is higher than that. Nobody has published a trustworthy figure for the years since 2009, and the payments have continued. The fund behind the program has run in debt almost every year since 1979, and in September of 2024 that debt stood at $5.1 billion. Between 2014 and 2016 three coal companies went bankrupt. They were Alpha Natural Resources, Patriot Coal and James River Coal, and together they owed $865 million in black lung benefits to sick and dying men. When they went bankrupt, what they owed came off the companies and landed on the public, the same way Bailey's company's debt had.

That is the first column. A ton of coal cost part of a man's lungs. The man paid that cost with his body, and the public paid the bill for the man. The company paid nothing. None of it appeared in the price of just under five dollars.

---

The second column runs orange in the creeks.

Some of the rock that mining cuts into has sulfur in it. Once that rock is opened, the sulfur meets air and water and turns into acid. The acid dissolves metals out of the disturbed ground and carries them down into the streams. A creek that gets this drainage runs the color of rust, or it runs gray with sediment, and the things that lived in it stop living there. The name for this is acid mine drainage. It does not stop when the mining stops. It begins when the rock is first opened, and it keeps running for decades after the last miner has left the last mine.

The law says that land which has been mined is to be reclaimed. That means the mines are closed out, the drainage is treated, and the ground is brought back toward what it was. The law is right, but the money behind it falls far short. Across the coalfields more than 633,000 acres are still waiting on reclamation. Reclaiming them would cost between $7.5 billion and $9.75 billion. Coal companies are required to post bonds to cover that cost, and the bonds they posted hold around $3.8 billion. So there is a gap of $4 billion to $6 billion between what the work costs and what the companies set aside to pay for it. If nobody fills that gap, the creeks stay orange.

A ton of coal cost a measure of a watershed. The watershed paid it, and the price did not mention the water.

---

The third column is the town.

Welch had department stores, theaters, hotels and a daily newspaper. Keystone had a life of its own, and so did Kimball, and so did a town called War. These towns looked permanent. A place looks that way when money is flowing through it and nobody yet imagines the money will stop. In McDowell the money was coal money.

Not all of a miner's pay came to him as cash. Up the hollows from towns like Welch were the coal camps, and the coal camps belonged to the companies. Around Gary, south of Welch, a subsidiary of U.S. Steel built a dozen company towns and mines, and 15,000 people lived in them. In 1922 almost 80 percent of West Virginia's miners lived in company houses and shopped in company stores. In Illinois only 10 percent did. In many of those West Virginia towns a miner could draw against his wages before payday, and what he drew was scrip. Scrip was a private currency of paper or metal tokens. The coal company issued it, and on payday the company deducted it from the miner's pay. At the company store scrip was good for its full face value. But the company would not buy it back for cash. A miner who needed cash could sell his scrip to a trader at a discount of 25 percent or more, or spend it at a nearby independent store, which often accepted it at 10 to 30 percent below its face value. Lou Athey, writing in the *West Virginia Encyclopedia*, says scrip "became a means to cycle the value of a miner's labor back to the company." Some miners were handed a pay envelope with a curling line drawn across it. The miners called that line "the snake." It meant the company owed them no wages.

Historians do not agree about how much the company stores took advantage of miners. The economist Price Fishback studied the early 1900s. He found that prices at company stores were generally similar to prices at nearby independent stores, and that miners were generally not in debt to the store. Suppose he is right about all of that. The employer still owned the house the miner lived in, the store he shopped in, and the currency he used between the two. A wage paid that way does less to build a town than the same wage paid in cash and spent on a main street, because part of it goes straight back to the company that paid it.

Then the work thinned. The mines brought in machines, and a machine cuts coal with a fraction of the men. The men who were no longer needed were no longer paid. The steel mills that had bought McDowell's coal began buying it elsewhere, or stopped buying it at all. The big steel operation that had anchored the county wound down across the 1980s, and more than 1,200 jobs went with it. Nobody announced one big closing. A shift was cut in one place and a section was closed in another, and this went on until the work that had been the spine of a town was gone.

The county's income did not collapse all at once. In its worst single year it dropped around 13 percent. From its peak in the mid-1980s it lost around 15 percent over three years. After that it kept falling for forty years, and the people left as it fell. In 1950 nearly 100,000 people lived in McDowell County. By 2020 fewer than 20,000 did, which means four out of every five were gone. The stores closed because the people left. The people left because the work was gone. And the work was gone because the coal that remained could be dug without them.

What is left in Welch, Keystone and Kimball is mostly not ruins. It is quieter than ruins. There are empty lots where the stores used to stand. There are storefronts with names you can still faintly read above doors that nobody opens. And there is a Main Street that has Sunday's traffic on every day of the week.

A ton of coal cost a share of a town's future. The town paid that cost across forty years. No single year on any ledger shows the size of it, because the cost was spread thin enough to look like ordinary decline.

---

The fourth column is the hardest to see, because the people who paid it are no longer in McDowell to be counted. They are the county's children, and they left.

Every family raises a child by spending on that child. The spending is breakfast cooked at seven in the dark before a shift. It is cleats for the team and lessons on a secondhand piano, and it is years of attention, money and worry. In the plainest sense all of that is an investment, which means a cost paid now in the hope of a return later. In most places the return comes back to the place where the child was raised. The child grows up, works there and raises children there, so what the family invested stays and circulates.

In McDowell the child grew up and left, because there was no work to stay for. Everything the family had invested in that child walked out of the county on the child's own two feet. It produced its return somewhere else, in a city that had paid nothing to raise that child. So the county spent itself raising the very people who had to leave it to live. This cost does not run orange in a creek, and it does not show up in a lung. It shows up as a county of old people and empty schools.

A ton of coal cost the county its own children. The county paid to raise them, and everywhere else got the benefit.

Every column so far is a cost that landed in McDowell. The next one is a cost that landed somewhere else.

---

Most of McDowell's coal was metallurgical coal, and it was bound for a furnace. What goes into a furnace comes out as heat. It also comes out as gas, and that gas warms the air for everyone who will live after the coal is burned. So this cost does not land anywhere near McDowell, and it does not land anywhere near 1960. It lands on people who are not yet born. It does not come due during the lifetime of anyone who profited from that ton of coal. The price of the coal said nothing about it, and there is a reason for that. A price is set by people who are alive. People who have not been born cannot sit at the table where the price is set, and they cannot send a bill back through time.

That is the fifth column, and I am leaving it nearly empty on purpose. A later chapter does nothing but compute what these things cost, and the number for this column belongs there. For now the column sits open on the page. That alone tells you the bill is larger than this chapter can add up.

---

The county's own records show where the first four columns left it. A boy born in McDowell County could expect to live around 63.5 years. For the United States as a whole the figure was around 76.5 years, so the boy from McDowell could expect thirteen fewer. The county's drug-death rate ran around 141 per 100,000 people, and the national rate was near 16. As of 2022 the county's poverty rate stood at 37.6 percent, and its median household income was $28,235. West Virginia has fifty-five counties. When they are ranked by the health of the people in them, McDowell ranks fifty-fifth.

The modern federal food stamp program began with a pilot in 1961, and its first food stamps were issued in Welch. The first person to receive them was a man named Alderson Muncy, on May 29. McDowell had fed the furnaces of the United States. Now it became the place where the United States first tested how to feed the people those furnaces had used.

Numbers this bad need an explanation.

---

By now you have seen the same thing happen four times. You saw it in a lung, in a creek, in a town and in a generation of children. Each time, value was generated in one place and the cost of generating it landed in another. And each time the two were never reconciled, which means the cost was never billed back to the place the value went. I call that cost severance, because the cost has been severed from the value.

The cost that gets left behind is the severed cost. In McDowell it is the lung, the creek, the emptied town and the child who left. It is real and it is owed, and there is no one to send the invoice to.

The act of taking the value while leaving the cost behind is value extraction. It is different from value creation. Someone who creates value makes something and is paid for it. Someone who extracts value takes what was already there and bears none of the cost of taking it. The economist Mariana Mazzucato drew that line between making value and merely taking it. David Harvey gave a name to the old pattern in which wealth is gathered by stripping it from a place that cannot defend its claim. He called it accumulation by dispossession. This book adds two things to their work. It applies the pattern to the physical extraction of a resource from the ground, and in later chapters it gives a method for putting a number on the severed cost.

Economics has had a name for part of this for a century. Arthur Pigou named it before any of these mines had reached its peak. His word was externality, which means a cost that a transaction imposes on someone who is outside the transaction. His kind of example was the smoke that falls on a neighbor's wash. The word is accurate. Black lung is an externality, and an orange creek is one too. But think about what the word does. To call a man's drowned lungs an externality is to file the worst thing in this chapter under a clerk's heading, as though it were off to the side of the transaction. It was never off to the side. Pigou and the economists who followed him saw the cost, but they lacked an instrument for doing anything with it.

Cost severance, severed cost and value extraction are the three terms the rest of this book is built on. Every chapter after this one uses them. I am coining them here, against this county, because this is the place where their meaning is plainest.

---

Why did nobody outside McDowell have to look at any of this?

Think back to the man on the rooftops in the last chapter. He climbed above each new city so that he could see its shape. From up there he could see where the river bent, how the old roads ran, and which neighborhoods the money flowed to and which it flowed past. You have to get above a place to see its shape. The people who live inside it, on their own streets and at their own doors, never do.

McDowell's coal made that same shape, but in reverse. The value of the coal went up and out of the county. It went to the mills, to the cities, and to the accounts that bought the steel. The people who received that value rode the rails the steel built. They crossed the bridges it framed and lived in the cities it raised. Not one of them ever had to look down on McDowell and see what their steel had cost. The cost stayed in the hollows while the value left them. And the people who got the value lived far away from the people who paid for it. They lived far enough away that they never had to climb above the arrangement and see the whole of it. Nobody feels the cost of a place they never have to look at.

I call that spatial cost severance. It means that geography keeps the bill out of sight. The value ends up in one place and the cost ends up in another, and no invoice is ever sent across the distance between them.

Was anyone to blame?

---

A chapter full of dead miners, orange creeks and emptied towns seems to be building toward a villain. The obvious villain is the coal operator. And the obvious moral is that bad men did bad things.

There may have been bad men. There usually are, somewhere. But my argument does not need them, and it does not rest on them. Cost severance would have happened even with saints in the boardroom, because it does not run on malice. It runs on one fact, which is that the price of the coal had no line for the lung, the creek or the town. When a transaction has no line for a cost, the transaction does not record that cost. When a cost is not recorded, neither the buyer nor the seller pays it. So the cost lands on whoever happens to be standing where it falls. That outcome comes from the way the arrangement is built. It does not come from the character of the men inside it, and nobody has to intend it for it to happen.

Some people raise the opposite objection. They say the coal built the modern world and the world is worth what it cost, so adding up the bill is just a way of wishing the rails and the bridges away. I am not wishing them away. I am also not asking whether the coal should have been mined. I am asking who should have borne the cost of mining it. A person can believe the coal was worth mining and still ask why the bill went where it went.

So the indictment Kennedy delivered in Canton stands. The want was real, the suffering was real, and the case he made was true. Only the name of the defendant changes. The defendant was never the party in power, and it was never the policy that failed. What belongs in the empty chair is an instrument that does not exist. By an instrument I mean a way of pricing the severed cost and sending it back to where the value went. There is no such instrument today. The argument of this book is that it has to be built.

---

None of this is finished, and it was never only about coal. McDowell itself shows that, because the same arrangement ran there a second time, in recent decades, with a different product.

When the work left McDowell, it left behind what extraction always leaves behind. It left people who were in pain and out of options. Their bodies were worn by labor, and their county had no economy to absorb them. The first extraction had taken the value of the coal and left the cost in the place. The second extraction came for what the first had left, and it came in the form of a medicine.

In the late 1990s a company called Purdue Pharma brought an opioid painkiller to market. The drug was OxyContin. Purdue sold it hard into places like McDowell, and places like McDowell are where it did the most. It would be easy to say that the company drew a map of the broken places and aimed at them. What the record supports is more careful than that, and more damning. There are really two records. The first is the one the companies wrote themselves. It is made up of internal documents, settlements, and what was said under oath in litigation. The second is the one that researchers and prosecutors pieced together afterward. They used prescriber data, public-health studies and the geography of distress to show where the pills went and why. No single document in either record confesses to a plan. But when you put the two records together, they show a pattern. The drug found its largest markets in places where the wage economy had already collapsed. Those were places where physical work had left people in chronic physical pain, where there was little to lose and less to do, and where the medical and economic supports that might have caught the problem had thinned along with everything else. The places the first extraction had hollowed out were the places the second one filled.

The two extractions were different kinds of taking. A coal company at least employed the miner. It paid him a wage, took the value of his labor and left the cost in his lungs. That was a lopsided exchange, but it was an exchange, with a wage contract in it. Purdue did not employ McDowell at all. It found the wreckage that the wage economy had left behind, and it made a market of that wreckage by selling into people's pain. Nancy Fraser has a word for this. She calls it expropriation, which is accumulation by means other than the wage contract. In her terms the second extraction was the purer case of the two, because there was no wage contract to disguise the taking.

The coal story had no guilty pleas in it. This part of the story does, and the amounts are in the record. The Sackler family owned Purdue, and the family drew something on the order of $11 billion out of the company. In 2007 Purdue pleaded guilty to a federal felony of misbranding and paid $600 million. Three of its executives, Friedman, Udell and Goldenheim, pleaded guilty to misdemeanor counts. In 2020 the company pleaded guilty again, this time to three federal felony counts. The largest drug distributors were McKesson, Cardinal Health and AmerisourceBergen. They had moved pills into counties whose populations could not have consumed them honestly, and in 2022 they agreed to a settlement of around $21 billion. Now set those figures beside McDowell's drug-death rate of around 141 per 100,000. The value left places like McDowell as money. The cost stayed in them as the dead. The two were never on the same ledger until courts forced them onto it, decades late.

The second cycle matters for more than its own facts. Suppose the pattern in McDowell had really been about coal. Then it would have ended when the coal did. It did not end. The same shape arrived in a different industry and ran the same way, with the value going out, the cost left behind, and no instrument to make the two meet. It will keep arriving, in whatever the next product turns out to be, for as long as that instrument does not exist. The pattern is a fact about how things are priced. It will run on anything that is priced the way the coal was.

---

The water I grew up on is a different place with the same defendant.

Watermen on the Chesapeake Bay go out before light and come back with what the bay gives them. What it gives has been thinning. The cause is a set of costs that the bay has been made to carry and that nobody ever put on an invoice. Nutrients run off the land and into the water. Sediment washes in. The water is warming. Over many years the bay has been slowly loaded with the byproducts of everyone upstream. All of that lands on the oyster, the crab and the men who chase them. Meanwhile the value of the upstream activity stays inland, where the watermen cannot reach it. The structure is the same as in the hollows, and the same instrument is missing.

There is one difference between the two places, and it matters. Coal is finite. What McDowell lost, it lost for good, because a mountain does not refill. A bay is different, because a bay can keep making what is taken from it. It can make oyster after oyster and crab after crab, generation after generation, as long as the rate of taking stays under the rate of making. That makes the severance on the bay conditional. It arrives only when people take faster than the water can make. It can also be held off, and even reversed, if the instrument McDowell never had is built in time. McDowell has been emptied. The bay still could be saved.

I have not said a word against the miners, and I am not going to. A man who cut metallurgical coal out of a mountain with his body did a hard, skilled and necessary thing, and he did it well. He took pride in that work. The pride was real, and he had earned it. Nothing in this chapter is a verdict on the men. The men were the best of it. The verdict is on an arithmetic. I mean a way of pricing a ton of coal that had no line for the lung or the creek or the town or the child. It sent the value up and out and left the cost at home, and in its own terms it could not do otherwise.

Kennedy saw the suffering and named the wrong defendant. The right defendant is an instrument that does not exist. The rest of this book is the work of building it.
