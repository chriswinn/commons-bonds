## The Highest Point

The most credentialed person in the building made about half of what I made. I was a junior developer then, two years out of nothing, no certifications, no degree. He held the qualifications the firm needed to keep its contracts, and he had been to more places than I could name and spoke more languages than I knew existed. Every morning he greeted me in the native dialect of one of my fifteen nationalities, rotating through them, because he had learned somewhere that hearing your mother's tongue even once a week is a small mercy to anyone living far from home. He learned to say hello in languages he would otherwise never speak, for friends he had made all over the world. And in every city he ever visited, he climbed to its highest point, so he could see the place from a vantage most of the people who had lived there their whole lives had never had.

He could not leave. His right to remain in the country was tied to the job. His family was here. To press for fair pay, or to walk out, was to risk all of it. So the firm paid him pennies on the dollar of what he was worth, and he said good morning to me in a language I did not know I carried, and we both understood, without either of us saying so, that something was wrong. I had no word for it. I had it the way you have a chipped tooth: a thing the tongue keeps finding.

Here is the word, in plain language, finally. Value created by an activity is captured by one party while the costs are absorbed by another party who cannot bill them back. That is the whole of it. No equation is needed to feel it, only the nerve to look at who ended up holding what.

Mariana Mazzucato got most of the way there. In *The Value of Everything* (2018) she shows how modern economies lost the ability to tell value creation apart from value extraction, until the act of moving money could be dressed in the costume of the act of making something. She names the extraction precisely. What I want to add is the accounting: a ledger, kept arrangement by arrangement, of who captured the value, who absorbed the cost, and whether the one absorbing it had any way to send the bill back. The extraction is the diagnosis. The ledger is what you do with it.

Once you are keeping that ledger, you see it everywhere. Turing Pharmaceuticals took a sixty-two-year-old drug already in use and repriced it from $13.50 a pill to $750 in 2015. Nothing was invented; the molecule was unchanged. A pacemaker implant returns something like $6,000 to the device manufacturer, while the Medicare reimbursement for the conversation about whether to implant it at all, the advance-care-planning code, runs about $86. The market values the hardware at seventy times the deliberation. In Libby, Montana, a vermiculite mine run by W. R. Grace captured on the order of a hundred million dollars in revenue across its decades; the downstream costs of the tremolite asbestos that came up with the ore, the lung disease that worked through the town for a generation, run into the billions, and the federal government eventually declared a public-health emergency there. Roughly forty dollars of cost for every dollar of revenue, landing on people who never saw the books. In 2008 the origination fees and the bundling fees were captured during the boom; the foreclosures and the wiped-out retirements and the household wealth that does not come back fell on the people who bought the product, and there was no slot on any invoice for the bill to travel back up.

Ronald Coase argued, in "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960), that where the parties to a harm can bargain, they will sort the cost out between themselves. It is an elegant argument, and it presumes the parties can find each other and that the transaction has somewhere to happen. The architectures I am describing are built so that it does not. The man in Libby cannot bargain with a corporate structure dissolved across decades. The foreclosed family cannot sit across the table from a tranche. The bill-back has no slot, and the missing slot is not an accident. It is the design.

## The Quiet Math

The math is older than the word for it, and it is the most boring story I have. For a stretch of years I lived an hour and a half from the office because the apartment was three hundred dollars a month cheaper, thirty-six hundred dollars a year, real money on a young salary. What the cheaper rent did not put on any statement was the commute: about five hours a day, five days a week, fifty-two weeks, which is roughly thirteen hundred hours a year. Thirteen hundred hours. A third of a working year, spent stationary, to save thirty-six hundred dollars. There was no villain in it. I did it to myself, because I did not have the real numbers, because the cost that mattered most was the one nobody had put on the invoice. The quiet math is just the discipline of counting that cost anyway.

I came to it honestly. My grandfather spent his entire working life at the NASA Langley laboratory in Virginia, hired under its older name when it was still the NACA, staying through the 1958 renaming, building the physical models that went into the wind tunnels. A wind-tunnel model has one job: to be true enough that the air tells you the truth in time, before the real thing is built and the error becomes expensive in lives. He built them true. His name is in the patent record. The patents, like most patents from salaried inventors, were assigned to the institution that employed him; he made the thing and others held the right to it, which is not a theft but the ordinary architecture of paid invention, and it is the first place I learned to recognize the pattern.

We called him Pooh, after the children's story. Years later a small nephew, reaching for the Greek *Pappou*, grandfather, got only as far as *Pou*, which is the same sound, and my father smiled without a word and something moved behind his eyes, because the child had handed back to him a name he had not known he was missing. I did not invent the resemblance. The sounds simply lined up, the way the truest things sometimes do.

He fixed everything, and he mentioned none of it. A man down the road once asked to borrow a drill bit. My grandfather found the bits worn, so he sharpened them, all of them, the way you would notice a dull knife. Somewhere in there he remarked, the way you might remark on the weather, that the man's air compressor had given out, so over about two weeks he machined new pistons and new rings and rebuilt the engine, and when the bits came back he said only that those were good to go now. The rebuilt engine he never circled back to. It was simply done, and the doing was its own quiet ledger, kept for no one.

My father was a naval architect, a civil servant for the Navy, with a high clearance and a diplomatic passport, which I mention only because naming the credentials gives nothing away. When I was a boy he woke me before dawn to drive into the Appalachians on what he called hunting trips, though we rarely brought anything down. It took me years to understand that the hunting was never the point. The best part of any trip, he would say, is the journey, and he meant the hours in the truck with me. He taught me a question, too, without ever saying it as a rule. Before he walked into a room or looked at a thing he had made, he carried himself as though someone he answered to would ask, *would you be proud of what I am about to see?* He spoke a hair below the volume of any room he was in, and the room would quiet itself down to reach him. Three generations of us pulled long hours and made things of value, and in each generation the larger share of the fame and the fortune those things produced settled somewhere else. That is not a grievance. It is the ordinary structure of salaried making, and it is the charge running under everything I notice.

I did my own version. I came to the National Institutes of Health at twenty-two on a three-month contract, near the turn of the century, while the Human Genome Project was racing toward completion, and stayed into the work that translates a finding into the dozen-odd forms it has to take to reach the people who need it. The baseline was fifteen derivative works for fifteen distinct reader audiences from a single result, sometimes into the mid-twenties. One project I inherited had been planned for a team of around forty people, a year, about a million dollars. I am deliberately aiming low with those numbers; that is how I remember writing them, and aiming low is the only honest way to remember a thing twenty years gone. By the time I arrived it was running about a million and a half and a year past due, with a proposal on the table to add another year, another million, more people. I cut it back to its original scope and had a working proof of concept in three months. The renewals followed.

Then the hours got strange. I billed more than a hundred and twenty hours in a week for stretches that ran into months; three weeks back to back I came close to a hundred and thirty-eight of the week's hundred and sixty-eight. Paper paychecks stacked up unopened on a counter, because opening them would have cost time I was spending. I shaved my own head on mute during conference calls. Once I fell asleep mid-email and woke cursing at myself, at no one else, for not having clicked send before sleep took me. I have been to fifty-eight countries as of this writing, most of them seen from the inside of a working week. Thomas Piketty, in *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2014), spent two centuries of data tracking where the value finally settles, in the hands that already hold the assets. I was keeping the other column the whole time without knowing it, the one that tracks where the costs settle. They are two halves of one accounting, and the second half is the one nobody totals.

## A Century Apart

Two facts, from opposite ends of a century, make the same point: the architecture is engineered, and it can be engineered differently.

Norway struck oil at Ekofisk, in its North Sea waters, in 1969. It captured the rents through licensing, through taxation, through direct state ownership of the operator, and then it did the unusual thing: it established a petroleum fund in 1990 and made the first deposit in 1996, and it built a wall between the windfall and the budget. The fund now holds more than two trillion United States dollars, roughly $390,000 for every citizen. A fiscal rule adopted in 2001 lets the government spend only the fund's expected real return each year, four percent at first, lowered to three percent in 2017, and the principal has never been spent. Set that beside the petro-states where the same geology produced capture without the architecture, where the oil came up and the wealth went sideways and the people got the costs. The difference between Norway and Venezuela is not the geology. It is the ledger.

And notice what the Norway case is not. It is not an argument against extraction. Norway pumped the oil, and the country is the richer and the fairer for it. The framework does not indict extraction. It indicts the mispricing of it, the cost quietly moved onto someone who never saw the books. Where an activity severs no real cost onto anyone, there is nothing to price and nothing to answer for. The accounting bites only where a cost-bearer exists and has been handed the bill with no way to send it back.

At the other end of the century, the cost side. In *From Here to Equality* (2020), William Darity Jr. and A. Kirsten Mullen assembled the most rigorous cost-side accounting I know of, read across centuries of American life. Their updated reckoning, in the second edition and in their 2022 statement in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, puts the Black-white wealth deficit on the order of fourteen trillion dollars. Years earlier, in *Umbrellas Don't Make It Rain* (2015), Darity with Darrick Hamilton and their colleagues had already shown that Black households at the highest strata of education and income hold less wealth than white households several strata below them, which is the signature of a cost severed by structure rather than by choice. I credit the field with that number. I will not write a coercion figure in my own hand; that is a column I decline to total, and I take no position on who else may. The point of an honest ledger is partly to be honest about which entries are not yours to fill.

Karl Polanyi saw the mechanism in 1944. In *The Great Transformation* he called land and labor and money fictitious commodities, things the market treats as though they were produced for sale when they were not, and then bills as free inputs. Nancy Fraser carries the charge into the present in *Cannibal Capitalism* (2022): the economy consumes the whole background of care and social reproduction and ecological repair without ever replacing what it eats. Cost severance is that insight wearing an accountant's eyeshade. Here is where I break with the tradition I most belong to, and I do it in the open, because the break is the contribution. The lineage's instinct is to pull the commons back out of the price system, to re-embed it, to keep certain things sacred by keeping them off the ledger entirely. But the unpriced commons has not been protected by its pricelessness. The cost landed on someone regardless: on the town, on the household, on the man who could not leave. Keeping the water off the page did not keep it safe. It only kept the bill hidden. The honest move is not to hold the commons above accounting. It is to put both sides of the ledger on the same page.

There are places where it is built that way already. The Mondragon cooperatives, founded in the Basque country in 1956 by a priest, José María Arizmendiarrieta, now run to roughly ninety-five cooperatives and some seventy thousand workers. Where a Fortune 500 chief executive might out-earn the median worker by something near three hundred to one, Mondragon's ratios run closer to three to one, six or nine to one at its largest industrial co-ops. They founded their own bank, Caja Laboral, in 1959. When the appliance maker Fagor failed in 2013, the federation absorbed the displaced workers instead of severing the cost onto them. It is not a utopia and it has not escaped the market. It is an architecture in which the value and the costs are kept, as much as possible, in the same set of hands. The point of the existence proofs is not that Norway or Mondragon is the answer. It is that the arrangement is a choice, and a choice can be made another way.

A market economist will say everyone chose. The colleague chose the job. I chose the apartment farther out. The miner in McDowell County, West Virginia, a county now home to fewer than twenty thousand people, chose the mine and chose the black lung that the mine, often enough, eventually delivered. The choice is real, and I will not pretend it away. But choosing happens inside an architecture that sets the price of every option on the menu before you ever reach for one. When the alternatives are the mine or leaving the only place your family has ever known, the mine is a choice the way my colleague's silence was a choice. The architecture does not abolish the choosing. It prices it, and then it hands you the menu with the prices torn off.

## Three in the Morning

I grew up on the Chesapeake, and the Chesapeake is dying in slow, well-documented motion.

Allison Colden, a fisheries scientist and the Maryland director of the Chesapeake Bay Foundation, has said plainly that the striped bass "will not have the same recovery they had in the 1980s. Warmer water from climate change, habitat loss, and invasive species are all making it very hard for striped bass to bounce back." In Virginia, Chris Moore, who studied the Bay's ecosystem for the Foundation for the better part of two decades, testified before the legislature early in 2026 that "our surveys in the Bay all point to a looming disaster, and our fishery in Virginia has all but dried up because there are so few striped bass around." He named the deeper problem too: "We don't have base science to make good decisions. We have a Bay cap that's based on historic landings."

The numbers underneath the testimony are stark. Maryland's 2024 survey of juvenile striped bass came back at 2.0 fish per sample against a long-term average of 11.0. The cap on menhaden, the small oily fish the whole food web leans on, sits at 51,000 metric tons for the Bay, set in 2017 by the interstate commission that manages it. In February 2026 Maryland asked the federal government to declare a disaster for its oyster fishery. The shad, which fed the regional commercial fishery for generations, have been effectively gone from it for decades.

This is where the watermen come in, because the watermen are the part of the story the institutions cannot see. In Fox Hill, an old neck of watermen's families, when a man's gear failed before a run, the others would be at his place in the dark, three in the morning, reworking the nets by worklight so he would not lose the tide. No one was keeping score. No invoice changed hands. Biggie ran oysters there; my father knew him from boyhood, and he is gone now thirty years and more. Another man, still living, fixed engines and ran his crab pots for decades on that same water; I will leave his name off the page, though everyone on the neck could supply it. What they did for each other in the dark has a name in no system of accounts. It is what a commons bond looks like when no institution is taking notes.

Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel Prize for documenting exactly this. In *Governing the Commons* (1990) she showed that communities have managed shared fisheries and forests and irrigation systems for centuries, below and before any formal institution, without either privatizing the resource or handing it to the state. The Fox Hill arrangement is an Ostrom case operating in the wild, and the men reworking the nets had never read her and did not need to, because the practice does not run on scholarly recognition; it runs on knowing whose tide is at risk. But Ostrom's design principles assume something the Bay no longer has. They assume a community of appropriators bound to the resource and to one another, who share the commons's fate, one community that captures the resource's value and bears the cost of its decline. Cost severance prices the commons where that shared fate breaks. The menhaden are not drawn down by the watermen who would carry the cost of the Bay's collapse; they are drawn down by an industrial reduction fishery that captures the value and carries none of it, and by a warming the watermen did nothing to cause. Ostrom governs a commons whose appropriators share its fate. The framework prices the commons where one party holds both the power and the incentive to draw the resource down against a cost-bearer who has neither.

Michael Sandel will object, and the objection is the serious one. In *What Money Can't Buy* (2012) he argues that pricing corrupts the goods it touches, that some things should be kept out of the market because the act of pricing them degrades them. He is right that market reasoning crowds out moral reasoning; I have watched it happen in rooms I was sitting in. But refusing to price the Bay did not keep the market out of the Bay. The watermen's catch was priced out from under them whether or not anyone ever put a dollar value on the water. The commons entered the ledger the moment the reduction boats arrived; the only question left was whether it would enter honestly, with the cost shown, or dishonestly, with the cost hidden on the side of the people who cannot send the bill. Sandel keeps the commons sacred by keeping it silent. Silence has not protected it. It has only protected the people drawing it down.

## Pulled North

I was thirty-eight when my son was born, and for a short while I got the mornings.

I would hold him with my left hand on top of him and my right hand underneath, supporting his weight and the small of his back. At first the feel of him was unfamiliar, and then it was not, and then I understood that my chest was not just a different kind of bed but a warm one, warm in a way the plastic bed could never be. I counted his breaths against mine, seven and seven, sometimes eleven and eleven. I got those mornings two or three times in a week. The other mornings I was already gone.

I took every day of paternity leave the company offered, and when that ran out I took the full leave the Family and Medical Leave Act allows. My boss let me work remotely and routed every piece of work he could to other people, and he kept doing it for as long as he was able. Then a few deals came back to life at huge clients where I had worked before and made my name, the kind a company calls must-win in a lean year. Word of them climbed high enough that even the chief executive was tracking them himself, and at that point there was no one my boss could hand them to. In the terms every company runs on, it was simply time for me to step up and add value. My employer was not cruel and my colleagues were not indifferent. The protocol did what it was built to do: paternity leave ends, family leave exhausts, the work only you can do sits waiting. No one thing made me go back. Everything did.

So I flew north, out of Savannah, on a working morning. As the city tilted away beneath the wing I thought of the colleague on his high points, looking down on Washington and Paris and Taipei from a vantage the residents never climb to, and I understood that I had finally reached the view he was after, and that what it showed me was the cost I had been living inside the whole time. In my best years, salary and commission together, my earnings reached something near four hundred thousand dollars at the very peak, and even that ceiling could not have covered the year and more of surgery and rehabilitation that life eventually asked for. The system fails the high earner too; it just fails him later and more politely.

The marriage did not make it through the distance and the trauma. There were other reasons, there always are, and the work alone did not end it; I will not load the whole weight onto the architecture, because the no-villain honesty has to run all the way to my own door. But the house, the hours, the absence at the worst possible time, those were on the ledger, and they were mine. We had built something with a beautiful roof, and I have learned this much from losing it: the house is not the thing I miss.

## An Old Move

None of this is a new move. It only wears new clothes.

Paul Laurence Dunbar published "We Wear the Mask" in 1895: *We wear the mask that grins and lies, / It hides our cheeks and shades our eyes.* Eight years later, in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), W. E. B. Du Bois named the veil and the question that hangs unasked in every encounter across it, the one nobody says out loud and everybody is asking: *How does it feel to be a problem?* Ralph Ellison, in *Invisible Man* (1952), gave the condition its plainest name, a man unseen because the people around him decline to see him. Frantz Fanon traced, in his account of the colonized world, how a whole order can be built so that some people absorb its costs as the simple price of its running, and are taught to carry that absorption as though it were their own nature rather than the architecture's design.

None of them were pricing a commons, and I would not flatten the distance between a poet's mask and a wealth deficit measured in trillions; the structural rhyme is real and the moral weights are not the same, and an honest ledger has to hold both of those truths at once. But the move is the same one I have been making across a whole book and the length of this essay: naming the thing that hides behind a polite and orderly surface, and insisting that it be seen and counted. The pricing of commons is new. The move is old.

The years did not give me the power to set right what my body had known was wrong since that first morning in my mother's language. They gave me something narrower, and in the end more useful: a way to make the wrong visible, to put the arithmetic in another person's hands and let the page carry what I had carried without words for as long as I can remember. This is the ledger. I can point to the empty columns. The numbers are yours to enter.

It is an honest accounting, and it does not undo the damage. The son I once held those mornings is somewhere in the world now, grown, and I do not know where, and we have no contact. I am writing for the ones still ahead of us, for the costs of the decisions not yet made, so that we might see the true price of a thing while there is still time to use the knowing.
