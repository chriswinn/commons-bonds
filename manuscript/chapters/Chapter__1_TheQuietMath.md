# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*



## Chapter 1: The Quiet Math

---

The first thing he did in any new city was find the highest place in it and climb.

A rooftop, a tower, a hill at the edge of town where the road switched back on itself until the whole city lay open below. He went up to see a place the way the people who had lived in it all their lives never had. Our first in-depth conversation was on the roof of a building in Washington, D.C., and between drags on his cigarette he kept looking wistfully off toward the Washington Monument. He told me that day that you do not really see a city from inside it. Most people sense it. You might even vaguely feel it. Sure, you see your street, your bus or metro stop, the door you walk through every morning. But you don't really see the shape of it. Because you can't see it from the inside. You have to get above it before the shape of the thing shows itself, before you can see where the river bends and why the old roads run the way they do and which neighborhoods the money flowed to and which it flowed past.

He had done this in more countries than I had ever set foot in, and that is still true now that I have been to fifty-eight of them. He was better at languages than I will ever be. When he learned that I carry fifteen nationalities in my blood, he began greeting me each morning in the native dialect of one of them, rotating through, so I never knew which tongue would meet me at the door when I arrived to work. He did it for everyone he cared about. He learned hello and goodbye, good morning and good evening, in the language of every person he befriended. He understood better than anyone I have known that a person living far from home will smile at a greeting in their mother's tongue, even if it comes only once a week. He had been far from home himself. He knew the ache, and he had decided to spend his life easing it in others, one word at a time.

You would probably never guess that he was the most credentialed person in the building.

At that time I was a junior developer about two years into the work, twenty-something, with no certifications and no college degree, learning on the job. He held degrees and standing the company could not have replaced if it had tried. The contracts that kept the lights on and the rest of us employed leaned on his name the way a roof leans on a load-bearing wall. Take him out and the structure would not sag. It would come down. Everyone there understood that without ever saying it.

He made about half of what I made.

The most credentialed person in the building made about half of what the least credentialed person in the building made, and there was nothing he could do about it. I do not mean he chose to do nothing. I mean there was nothing he could do. He was not a citizen. His right to remain in the United States was tied to his job. His family was here, his children were here, and the whole life he had built was here. If the company decided it no longer wanted him, he could be sent out of the country, away from all of it. So he came to work, with a smile on his face, and he did the work beautifully. There was a large difference between what his work was worth and what he was paid to do it. That difference went somewhere, but it did not go to him.

I had no word for what I was looking at. Nobody around me had one either. We could all see him. We all knew, more or less, what his situation was. It was not a secret. But in our ordinary way of talking about work and pay and fairness and the market, there was no place where the thing I was looking at could be set down and named. The language we had described a free employee, freely working, freely paid, free to quit whenever he liked. That language was a lie. Nobody was lying. It was a lie because it had no room in it for a man who could not leave.

What I felt standing near him was older than language. I felt a wrongness deep in my bones. I felt that something was being taken. Not stolen the way a thief takes, with a hand in a pocket. Taken in a quieter way, a way built to run smoothly and leave no fingerprints. The value he generated went up and out and settled into accounts that were not his. The cost of the arrangement, the gap between his worth and his pay, the constrained years, the daily knowledge that he could not go, all of that settled down onto him and stayed. He carried the full weight of it with his head held high. He could not bill it back to anyone. There was no invoice he could send and no court that recognized the debt. The whole thing was legal and ordinary, and it sat in my chest like a stone.

---

I would learn to quantify it. But the learning came in pieces, and the first piece had nothing to do with him. It had to do with a lease.

When I moved to the region around Washington, the one the people who live there call the DMV, I did what every new arrival does. I looked for a place to live and ran the math everyone runs: rent against square footage against distance from work. Farther out was cheaper, and everyone knew it. So I found a place farther out, with more room and an extra bedroom, for three hundred dollars a month less than anything I could find near the office. I drove the route once before I signed, late at night on empty roads. It took forty-eight minutes end to end, and I believed I understood what I was agreeing to.

I did not.

The first day I drove it during working hours it took two and a half hours each way. Not the driving alone. The drive to the train, the parking, the ride into the city, the walk. Two and a half hours in the morning and two and a half hours at night, plus forty dollars to park, at least until I learned that parking in the alleys and eating the occasional ticket came out cheaper than the lot. I got home that first night emptied of whatever optimism I had carried into the lease. I worked five days a week, six when we were busy.

Run the arithmetic with me, because the arithmetic is the thing. Five hours a day. Five days a week. Fifty-two weeks in a year. Thirteen hundred hours. In one year, in exchange for the cheaper apartment, I spent thirteen hundred hours in a car and a train and a station, going nowhere, generating nothing, becoming no one. Thirteen hundred hours is enough time to learn a language. It is enough time to learn to play a musical instrument. It is enough time to fall in love. If you are not careful, it is enough time to fall out of love.

I had saved three hundred dollars a month. Thirty-six hundred dollars across the year. And against that thirty-six hundred dollars I had spent thirteen hundred hours of my one life, and not one of those hours appeared anywhere when I signed. They were not on the listing. They were not in the rent. They were not in the calculation everyone runs, the calculation I had run, the one that told me, correctly, that the apartment was cheaper. The apartment was cheaper. It was also the most expensive place I have ever lived, and it would be years before I realized both of those things are true.

Nobody did this to me. There was no villain at the leasing office and no scheme. I did it to myself, freely, with full information about everything except the one thing that mattered most: the cost no one had put on the invoice. Once I had felt that cost in my own body, I could not unsee its shape. I started looking for the missing number everywhere, not as a theory but as a flinch, the way you favor a joint you have hurt.

Without my deciding it, seeing became a method. I had started running a kind of math nobody taught me, the math of what a thing actually costs. It included the part nobody writes down. It included the part that lands on someone who never agreed to carry it. I didn't name it. I just ran it, under everything, all the time.

---

And then I saw it everywhere.

I saw it in the watermen of the water I grew up on, the men who work the Chesapeake. They go out before light and come back with what the water gives, and more and more with less of what it used to give. The water has been made to carry costs no one ever put on an invoice. Those costs settle onto the oyster and the crab and the men who chase them, while the value floats off upstream and inland and out of reach. I will tell you about them properly later. They deserve a chapter, not a sentence.

I saw it in the worker, any worker, who generates value that does not stay near the worker. It flows past and settles as someone else's fortune. The hand that builds the thing is paid for an hour of building, and the thing goes on earning for years into accounts that are not the builder's.

And I saw it in the deepest version, the one that lies under all the others. I cannot speak honestly about any of this without naming it. There were people whose labor was taken from them entirely, by force, for another's fortune. It was not only their labor. It was their freedom, and beneath their freedom, the thing harder to name and worse to lose, the freedom to choose how to live a life at all. I am the child of a place that sits on that history. I think of Harriet Tubman, who escaped slavery and reached her own freedom and then went back, again and again, into the country that would have re-enslaved her, to carry others north on the Underground Railroad. The wrongness there is not a missing line on a ledger. It is the ledger torn up and the person who kept it sold. The shape repeats throughout this book. It is the same shape, value generated by one person and captured by another. But the weights are not the same and will never be the same. A commute and a stolen life do not belong on the same line, and they will not share one here.

Once the lens had formed, the examples did not arrive one at a time, politely, in order. They arrived all at once, each one calling up the next, until I could not walk through an ordinary day without three or four of them surfacing on their own. I suspect that as you read, your own will eventually start occurring to you if they have not already. The job you took or left. The person you watched carry something that was never theirs. The thing you bought cheap that someone down a chain you could not see paid the real price for. You have witnessed this your whole life. So had I. But just like you can listen to someone without hearing them, you can look past something without actually seeing it. Now I can't unsee it. What was new was that I had finally been handed a way to see it on purpose, instead of feeling it as a stone in the chest and reaching, every time, for a word that was not there.

---

I had been handed the feeling, it turns out, long before I was handed any of the words.

My son would never meet my grandfather. The family called him Pooh, after the bear in the children's story, though my father didn't tell me that until I was grown. Pooh spent his entire working life at NASA's Langley Research Center in Hampton, Virginia. He started there when the agency was still called NACA, stayed through the change to NASA in 1958 and retired not long before he died. He was a model maker in the wind tunnels. He was an inventor, and his name is in the patent record.

A wind tunnel tells the truth about an aircraft that does not yet exist. You cannot fly the real thing to find out whether it will fly, because if the answer is no, it is paid for by people who cannot recover from it. So you build the world in miniature. You make a scale model in wood and metal and resin of a craft that exists nowhere yet, set it in the moving air, and let the air tell you the truth. But the air tells the truth only if the model was built faithfully enough for the truth to come through. A careless model gives back a confident, beautifully measured, completely false answer, and the false answer kills people. So the discipline is specific and unforgiving. Build it true, true enough that the truth can be seen while there is still time to act on it. My grandfather's work had to hold up where being wrong is paid for by someone who cannot survive the error.

He built faithful models so the truth could be seen in time, and he did not think much of it. Work needed to be done, so he rolled up his sleeves and did it. For most of my life I did not understand that his work is the quiet center of this book.

I know the not-thinking-much-of-it from the story of the air compressor, which my father told the way you tell a thing that has become a small family scripture. My father had stopped by the workshop Pooh built behind the family house. My grandfather spent countless hours in his workshop. He kept the place organized with every tool in its spot. My father noticed the air compressor was sitting slightly askew and out of its place, which in that workshop was like finding a word misspelled in a dictionary. He didn't want to disturb his father's work, so he tried to ask, softly, casually, whether he might borrow a drill bit, his eye on the compressor the whole time. The drill bits were getting dull, my grandfather said, and they were not ready to use. And then, while noticing my father's line of sight, he added, almost as an afterthought, that the compressor's engine had given out on him.

A week or two later my father came by again and the compressor was running. He asked about it, confused, as he thought his father had told him the engine was broken and wouldn't run. My grandfather shrugged without turning his head from whatever was in front of him and said he had machined new pistons and new rings and rebuilt the engine. Then he nodded toward the drill bits on the bench. Those are good to go now, he said. He had sharpened them too. He had machined an engine back to life and sharpened a set of bits nobody had asked him to sharpen, and he mentioned both the way you mention the weather. He did not think anything of it.

That was the man. The value he made was real and large, and the fame and fortune of it went to the enterprise he served, as it always does in the patient, ordinary machinery of salaried invention. That is how it is built to work. No one reached into his pocket. The value simply went up and out and settled somewhere other than with the man who made it, and he carried the difference without thinking anything of it.

My father carried it forward. He was a naval architect, a civil servant who worked for the Navy, who held a high clearance and traveled on a diplomatic passport. He was tight-lipped all my childhood about what he actually did. He sat in rooms with captains and admirals. What he passed to me was never subject matter, which I never heard, but a way of being in the world.

He taught it to me almost without seeming to teach. When I was a boy he said that my life was my own and I could choose to do anything with it, and the choice and the life were mine. But he did suggest that even if I decided to dig ditches for a living, I should be able to look at my work at the end of the day and know in my heart that no one had ever dug a better ditch. He said pride in your own work makes the hard days a little easier, and that knowing you did well is often enough, even when no one else will ever see what you did. Or if they see it, they will never know it was you that did it. And he had a way of asking, before he came to look at a chore I had done, whether I would be proud of what he was about to see, and then never forcing me to answer, because the question did the work without one.

I remember an afternoon. He had given my sister and me some chore, cleaning the living room and the backyard before we could play on a weekend. We were small and we rushed through it, cutting every corner we could think of. We swept the dust under the corner of the rug. We pushed the sticks from the yard under the old shed. Then we ran back breathless and panting while we announced we were done and asked whether we could go play. He started to rise from his chair, and then he paused with his hands still on the armrests, and he looked at us, and he said, Before I get up, when I go to check the work, will you be proud of what I am about to see? My sister and I looked at each other. Wait, we said. Wait, do not get up yet. Let us do a quick walk through. We ran for the broom and the dustpan and carried the dust under the rug to the trash. We crawled under the shed and pulled the sticks out and broke them down. We edged the lawn and swept the sidewalks, and by the time we walked back to him we were standing taller. We were proud of the work we did, and we were ready, finally, for him to look.

There were the mornings he drove me out toward the Appalachians before dawn to hunt. He used to say that the best part of any trip is the journey. While it was happening, I thought he was telling me to enjoy the view, or the air, or being philosophical about enjoying each step in the journey through life. I did not realize until years later that he was simply talking about getting to spend time with me.

He held a room in a way I have used my entire career. He sat with men who carried large personalities, and he never raised his voice. He didn't even speak at the room's volume. He spoke a hair below it, and only when he had something worth saying. A strange thing happens when someone known for speaking only when it matters starts to talk just under the noise of a loud room. You don't have to exercise any power over anyone. They quiet themselves down just to hear you.

So three generations of men in my family pulled long hours and generated value and watched the fortune of it settle into someone else's accounts, and did not, on the whole, think very much of it, because the not-thinking-much-of-it was itself the inheritance. The grandfather was the first instance I can name. And when I stood near the colleague years later and felt the stone settle in my chest, I was not feeling something new. I knew the shape of value flowing away from the person who made it because it was the shape of my own family, three generations deep. That is why I recognized him on sight. The bone-sense was inherited.

There was one more handing-down, and it came from the smallest member of the family. Years after my grandfather died, my sister grew up and had a son. He was at the age of learning everyone's names. I remember vividly that he pointed at her and said momma. He pointed at me and said Uncle Chris. He pointed at my father and reached for Pappou, the Greek word for grandfather, and what came out of his mouth instead was Pou. My father got a particular kind of smile on his face, and a bit of a twinkle in his eye that I did not, at the time, understand. He paused for a moment, then nodded. Pou it is, he said, and he did not explain anything to anyone in the room. I did not know, then, that his own father had been called Pooh, and that this small boy had just handed him, by accident, what phonetically was the same nickname his father used. I would not hear that my grandfather went by the name Pooh for at least another seven or eight years. My nephew was too young to understand the gift he had just given to his Pappou.

---

I started my own working life downstream of greatness, which is a useful place to learn humility. I was twenty-two, on a contract at the National Institutes of Health that was supposed to last three months, while the Human Genome Project raced toward completion. I make no claim on that work. It belonged to researchers I still count among the finest minds I have ever had the pleasure of interacting with. My job sat downstream of theirs. I built the things that took their research, ran it through peer review, and translated it for the people who needed it.

It sounds simple, and it was deceptively hard. A finding about a marker for some rare genetic condition had to carry enough detail for other research teams to replicate and verify it independently. It had to tell the specialists in the field what it was and why it mattered. And it had to be clear enough that a child living with that very condition could understand what had been found and what it might mean for her. At a baseline there were around fifteen versions for around fifteen audiences, sometimes climbing into the mid-twenties, running from the researchers who would build on the finding all the way down to the children themselves. We were not promising those children a cure. We were telling them that people were working, and that the work might one day mean a different kind of life for children like them. There is a particular look on a child's face when she understands that people care and are trying to help, and that maybe someday a child might not have to be sick the way she has been sick. One look, and I stopped sleeping well for years. Sleep just didn't matter to me as much as the work.

The project I had been hired to look at for three months had already been running for two years. It had been planned for one year, about a million dollars, and a team of around forty people. I am rounding the team down. It could have been thirty-six, or closer to fifty. Even then I was reaching for the smallest honest number I could stand behind, a habit I didn't know I had until much later, when I found I had built a whole way of thinking on top of it. By the time I arrived the project was at about a million and a half and a year past due, and the plan was to ask for another year, another million, and more people. When people explained the project to me, I noticed that most of the explaining was a defense of all the work that had been piled onto it. So I cut everything that had drifted from the original scope. Three months later there was a working proof of concept. The contract was renewed, they handed me another project a different team had given up on, and then another, and the renewals kept coming.

What made me good at it was not special knowledge. It was the same thing my grandfather used when he sharpened the drill bits nobody had asked him to sharpen. It was a willingness to ask the question my role did not include, to work at the seams the documentation never covered, to refocus on what the actual problem was instead of the problem everyone had agreed to keep having.

Years later, by then a CEO, I drove up one night to a cable television station on the East Coast. The lot was fairly empty under its lights, and the building was lit from inside. The broadcast was running on a queue of commercials that would run dry in less than five days. After that, every commercial break would play dead air, which would have meant refunding the revenue and likely losing those clients for good. The system that played the commercials worked perfectly. The system that loaded new ones into it was simply not online anymore. It was completely dark, and nobody in the building even knew where the server physically was.

I asked everyone the most logical questions I could think of. Around when had they noticed it? Had anyone seen anything different or out of the ordinary at that time? A guy who had only been there a year or so timidly spoke up. He pointed at a dusty machine in the corner and said that a few days back there had been a storm, and the little red light on a server over there had stopped blinking. He didn't know what the server did, and nobody there did. They were not careless. They were competent people who had inherited systems other people installed years earlier, following the startup and shutdown sequences in the standard operating procedure manuals they had been handed and doing exactly the work their roles defined. The question I happened to walk in willing to ask was not in any of their job descriptions, and it could not be. That is how institutions are built.

The machine turned out to be a decade-old server from a famous broadcasting company. The company found a warehouse with parts that had never been used and put me on the phone with a worker in Chicago, who said he would get on a plane if I bought him a round-trip ticket and put cash in his hand. Through the warehouse search, the meetings to release the funds, and the part's flight in, I slept on the production suite floor under the mixing board in stretches of three hours and then half an hour at a time. We were back up in three days. The station thanked us and asked about further work. When I gave them a number for a retainer, they declined. They had already gotten what they needed.

I tell you this to be honest about the skill's price, not to praise the skill, because the skill and the price are the same thing seen from two sides. For stretches that ran into months I billed over a hundred and twenty hours a week. For three weeks back to back before one deadline, I billed close to a hundred and thirty-eight hours every seven days, out of the hundred and sixty-eight a week contains. I delivered early. A stack of unopened paper paychecks sat on my desk. My hair grew long because there was no time for a haircut, so I started shaving my own head in the bathroom with the phone on mute during conference calls. When I remembered to eat, I ate from a drive-through window, on mute during conference calls. One morning I woke at my desk with the laptop still open and realized I had fallen asleep mid-sentence in an email. What I remember is cursing at myself, not for falling asleep but for not clicking send before sleep took me, because someone needed that email before they woke so they could keep the work moving. Sleep took me. I had stopped choosing it.

I learned that pattern at the NIH and carried it the rest of my career: an inherited willingness to absorb the whole cost of the work into my own body without thinking much of it. One day it would cost me the early years with my son, and it would cost me my marriage.

---

By the time I was deep into my best years, the company I worked for told me that the leave had run out. It told me correctly and without cruelty. The protected time for a medical emergency was over, and it was time to do what I was good at again. There was no villain in this. There almost never is. The company did precisely what its protocols told it to do, and my colleagues were not indifferent. The system did exactly what it was designed to do, which is the entire problem and the entire reason I eventually needed to build a way of seeing it. A free-market economist would tell me I had made voluntary trade-offs, and I had. I had options a coal miner does not have: rare skills, several possible employers, the freedom to move, and significant pay. With every advantage the language of voluntary choice could grant me, I still ended up doing the thing the pattern had always pulled me toward.

So when the leave ran out, I went. I pulled away from my family at the worst moment of my life and got on a plane and flew north. As it climbed I looked out the window and watched Savannah, Georgia, fall away beneath me, the river and the marsh and the city shrinking until I could see the whole shape of it at once, the way you can only see a place once you have left the ground.

And I thought of him. My colleague on his rooftops and his hills, looking down on Washington and Paris and New Delhi and cities all over the world, seeing what the people who lived their whole lives inside them had never climbed high enough to see. I had finally reached his vantage, pressed to a small oval window at altitude. From there I could see all at once and too clearly the exact shape of the cost I was living inside. I had spent my life learning how to get above a thing to see it. Until that plane lifted off, I had not understood that the thing I most needed to see from above was my own life, or that I would only get the altitude at the moment I was being carried away from everything in it that mattered.

What I was being carried away from was a small room full of colored light, where I held my son in the moments the nurses could fit it into their overworked schedules.

He had come early, and he was very small, and the nurses had told me, almost apologetically, that it is not good for a baby to go without being held by a human, that they tried, they always tried, but there were so many babies and so few of them, and could I come and lie back in the chair beside his bed and let them set him on my chest. Getting the chair and the wires arranged safely was slow work, so it was only two or three mornings out of seven. But on those mornings I held him. Left hand resting on top of him, right hand underneath, stabilizing his bottom and his weight on my chest, the whole of him almost nothing in my two hands. His little coos and tiny wiggles as he snuggled in were the stuff of daydreams. At first the feel of him was unfamiliar to me, the way anything is the first time. And then something turned over in me, and I understood that my chest was not just another kind of bed for him. It was warm in a way no plastic bed could ever be. That was the whole of it. A warm chest instead of a plastic bed. I counted my breaths, seven on the inhale and seven on the exhale, or eleven and eleven. Whichever count I chose at the start held for the whole sitting. I counted as many breaths as I could before I got distracted, and then I started again at one. I have never in my life been more completely in one place than I was in those moments, his weight in my hands and the count climbing, with nowhere else I wanted to be.

Then the leave ran out, and the deals had to close, and the mortgage had to be paid, and the lights in the house had to stay on. So I flew north.

The marriage did not make it through that period. But that is not my story to tell. There were other reasons. There always are. To say the work alone ended it would be a lie, a kind of self-flattery dressed up as confession. It did not. But the distance and the trauma and the absence at the moment that most needed presence were in it.

I had bought a beautiful house, and the beautiful house had a mortgage, and the mortgage had to be paid, and the only way to pay it was to keep doing the thing that pulled me away. I have thought, many times since, about the smaller life I did not choose. A tiny house on the coast. A cabin in the woods. A boat. Any of them would have cost so little to keep that I could have stopped working when it mattered most and stayed afloat for a while, long enough, maybe, to be where I was needed instead of on a plane. The beautiful house carried a cost that never appeared on the listing, the same kind of cost the cheaper apartment had carried, the kind nobody writes down, the kind that lands on a life and stays there. The house was a thing I had to keep feeding, and feeding it took the one thing it was supposed to shelter.

And here is the harder number, the one that closes the door on the comfortable idea that I simply earned too little, that more money would have fixed it. If it could not cover me at the ceiling, sit for a moment with what that means for everyone else. Anyway, sure, the house was beautiful.

The house is not the thing I miss.

---

What the years finally gave me was not the satisfaction of righting the things that felt wrong. It was the ability to shine a light on them, to make the unseen seen. It took me back to the morning that man greeted me in my mother's language. And it gave me the arithmetic that shows the system as it is, and that proves the decisions we made would not all have been the same if we had known the real cost.

The work of naming what hides behind a mask is older than this book. It is Dunbar's mask and Du Bois's veil, and Black American writing has carried it for over a century. The framework this book presents extends the move into the pricing of commons. The pricing is new. The move is old.

The apartment and the house both looked like good deals on paper. Each time, the question I failed to ask was the simplest one: is this the full accounting?

This is an honest accounting. It does not undo the damage.

The son I once held is somewhere in the world now. I do not know where. I wish I knew. We have no contact.

I am writing for the ones still ahead of us, about the unseen costs of the decisions not yet made, so that we can see the true cost while there is still time to act on it.

This is the ledger. I can only point out the empty columns. The numbers are yours to enter.
