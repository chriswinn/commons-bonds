# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 5: The Accountability Gap

## The pattern you cannot stop seeing

There is a shape that, once you have learned to see it, you cannot stop seeing. It does not announce itself. It runs quietly, the way the most durable arrangements always do, because the ones that draw attention get stopped and the ones that survive are the ones that look like ordinary business. The shape is this. Somebody takes value out of a resource, a community, or a system. The taking generates a cost. And then the cost gets pried loose from the party that benefited and set down onto somebody else, somebody with less power, less room to move, less ability to say no. The value floats up and out and settles into accounts. The cost sinks down and stays.

Then time passes. And when the reckoning finally arrives, as it always does, the one who took the value is gone. The company has reorganized. The executives have moved on. The generation that made the promise has died. The cost is still there, but the party that owes it cannot be found, and so the bill is handed to whoever is still standing in the place where it landed.

That is the whole mechanism. The reckoning always arrives. The only open question is who is still standing where it lands.

I am going to walk you through a spread of cases. A vermiculite mine in a Montana town. An oil platform in the Gulf of Mexico. A tanker on a reef in Alaska. A rare-earth complex on the edge of a desert in Inner Mongolia. A whole national financial system in the autumn of 2008. The retirement compact between the generations. And a healthcare system that has quietly made a crowdfunding website and the unpaid labor of exhausted families into its safety net of last resort. They are separated by geography, by industry, by decades. The arithmetic is different in every one. Watch what stays the same.

## Libby, Montana

Libby is a small town in the far northwest corner of Montana, tucked into the Kootenai Valley near the Idaho line. For most of the twentieth century it had a mountain just outside town that was worth a fortune, and the fortune was a gray, flaky mineral called vermiculite.

The W.R. Grace company ran the vermiculite mine at Libby from 1963 to 1990, a stretch of about twenty-seven years, though the deposit had been worked under other owners for decades before that. Vermiculite expands when you heat it, puffing up into light, fireproof granules, and that made it the perfect filler for home insulation. The Libby mine supplied more than seventy percent of the vermiculite used in the United States, the largest single share of the world's supply, and it was processed into a product called Zonolite that went into the attics and walls of tens of millions of American homes. The operation made money. Over its life it generated something on the order of a hundred million dollars in revenue. By the ordinary measures, it was a success.

The vermiculite at Libby was contaminated with a particularly lethal form of asbestos. Not the kind that needs decades of heavy industrial exposure to do its work, but a needle-fine variety that lodges in the lining of the lungs and stays there. The mining and milling threw it into the air. It coated the equipment, the roads, the rail cars, the men.

Internal company documents, the kind that only come to light years later when lawyers force them open in litigation, show that the company identified the contamination early and measured the asbestos exposure of its workers throughout the operation. It kept the records. It watched the numbers. And it kept mining. The dust did not stay at the mine. It rode home on the workers' clothes and into their kitchens, where their wives shook it out and their children breathed it. Kids played in the waste piles, in drifts of the stuff that the town had been told was harmless, that the high school used to surface its running track.

The toll is not an abstraction. Hundreds of residents of a town of a few thousand have died of asbestos-related disease. Thousands more carry the scarring in their lungs, the slow stiffening that shows up on an x-ray years before it shows up in your breath. In a town that size, that is not a statistic that lives at arm's length. It is a fraction of the people you would pass on the street, the people whose names are on the war memorial and the high school trophies, the people who built the place and could not leave it, because a town is not a job you quit. It is where your parents are buried and where your house is and where you know which roads ice over first. The rate at which Libby's people died of asbestos-related disease runs many times the national average, a difference so large that an epidemiologist could see it without statistics, the way you can see a fire without a thermometer. In 2009 the Environmental Protection Agency declared a public-health emergency at Libby, the first time in its history it had ever made such a declaration for a disaster made by human hands. It is worth pausing on what that first means. The agency had a category for floods and hurricanes, for the disasters that arrive from outside and could not have been chosen. It had never before reached for that category to describe a harm a company had measured, recorded, and continued to produce.

It is worth sitting for a moment with where the Zonolite went, because the case has a way of feeling distant until you do. Millions of American attics still hold it. If your house is old enough, the loose gray fill above your ceiling might be it. The distance between the reader and this case may be no greater than the distance between your chair and the room above your head.

Now the financial side, and here you have to be careful, because the easy version of this number is not honest. The documented, traceable public spending on Libby specifically, the EPA Superfund cleanup, the federal and state response, runs to roughly nine hundred million to a billion dollars. Against the mine's hundred million in lifetime revenue, that alone is a ratio of about nine or ten to one. But the cleanup is only the part that has an invoice. It does not include the uncompensated cost of the dead and the dying, the decades of lost work and life and care, which, priced separately and conservatively, push the true total plausibly into the low billions. I want to be honest about the seams here: that larger figure is a framework estimate built from disclosed components, an order-of-magnitude reading, not a single audited number. But even the conservative, fully-documented floor is most of a billion dollars in public money spent cleaning up after a hundred million in private revenue.

And here is the part that matters most for everything that follows. In 2001 W.R. Grace declared bankruptcy. The asbestos liabilities were channeled into a trust under the reorganization, the assets were restructured, and the corporate entity walked through the legal door that bankruptcy holds open. Shareholders and executives moved on with their lives. The town stayed where it was.

There was a criminal case. In 2005 the federal government indicted Grace and several of its senior executives, naming them by their roles in the operation, in what was at the time the largest environmental criminal prosecution the country had seen. It went to trial. The government had the internal documents, the exposure measurements, the long paper record of a company that knew. And in 2009, after a long and grinding proceeding, the defendants were acquitted.

That outcome shocked people who had followed the case, and I understand the shock. It feels like a moral failure, like the system simply refused to do the obvious thing. But I want to resist the temptation to read it as a failure of nerve or of justice, because reading it that way misses what the case actually teaches, which is something more disturbing than a single bad verdict.

I want to be precise about what that acquittal means, because it is the load-bearing point of this whole case. It is not evidence that the harm did not happen. The lungs are real and the graves are real. It is evidence of something else: that the criminal law, which is built to prosecute identifiable acts, committed with identifiable knowledge, at identifiable moments, is not structurally suited to a harm that unfolds across decades through thousands of small accumulated decisions. There is no single moment of the crime to point a jury at. There is only a slow, distributed shedding of cost across a generation.

The legal scholar Katharina Pistor has a way of seeing this that the chapter will lean on more than once. In her work on what she calls the code of capital, she shows that capital is not a thing but a quality, one that ordinary assets acquire when they are wrapped in particular legal modules, the law of property, of collateral, of corporate form, of bankruptcy. Those modules are not bugs. They are the design. The bankruptcy discharge that let Grace shed its liabilities, and the architecture of criminal proof that let the executives walk, are not failures of the legal system. They are features of it, working as built. Where Pistor diagnoses the modules that encode the severance, this book takes up the next question: how to price what the modules sever.

## Deepwater Horizon

On the twentieth of April, 2010, the Deepwater Horizon drilling rig in the Gulf of Mexico exploded, killed eleven workers, and began releasing oil into the water. Before the well was finally capped eighty-seven days later, it had put roughly 4.9 million barrels of crude into the Gulf, the largest marine oil spill in history. BP, the operator, had drilled the well through formations that its own engineers had flagged as extreme-risk geology, in an environment where the margin for error was already thin.

BP had taken enormous value out of the Gulf over the years. That value was real. It was banked, distributed to shareholders, counted as profit. None of it is in dispute.

Look at how the cost severed. It severed in space: the oil was refined and sold and burned in places far from the Gulf, while the poisoning settled into the waters where fishing families had worked for generations, people whose whole economy was tied to a coastline they could not pick up and move. It severed in time: the damage to a marsh or a fishery persists for years and decades after the quarterly report that recorded the cleanup charge has scrolled off the screen. And it severed ecologically, onto creatures that cannot file a claim. Toxins absorbed by keystone species impair reproduction for years; an oyster bed or a tuna spawning ground does not show up in court to demand restitution. The things least able to refuse the cost absorbed the most of it.

The money is, again, a place where honesty requires care. There is a tempting round number that circulates for the total losses, and it does not hold up to its sources, so I will not use it. What I can do is build a defended range from the components that are actually documented. The federal natural-resource damage assessment settled the ecological injury at roughly 17.2 billion dollars. On top of that sit the tourism losses, the fisheries losses, and the peer-reviewed estimates of coastal property-value decline, which the published research puts at something like 3.8 to 5.0 billion dollars, not the much larger figures that once circulated. Against that stack of documented harm, BP paid out somewhere in the range of sixty to sixty-five billion dollars in penalties, settlements, and trust-fund commitments across the various federal, state, and private actions.

So this is not a case where the company paid nothing. It paid a great deal. The point is subtler and harder to dismiss: even after one of the largest corporate penalty payments in history, the band of cost that was never recovered, the harm that never found its way onto any ledger BP had to settle, is wide, and the people inside that band are precisely the ones who could not move out of its way.

There is a sharper version of the same point, and it is geographic. The same spill reached the Gulf waters of Mexico. The settlement BP reached with Mexico was on the order of twenty-five million dollars, orders of magnitude smaller, per comparable stretch of damaged coastline, than what was paid on the American side. The oil did not know which way the border ran. The slick did not thin out at the line. What differed was the legal architecture available to the people on each side. American claimants could invoke the Oil Pollution Act of 1990, state tort and environmental law, and the machinery of class-action aggregation against a corporation operating on American soil. The Mexican claimants had a weaker set of tools against a foreign-domiciled company. Same oil, two prices, and the prices were set not by the physics of the spill but by which legal modules the injured party could reach for. That is Pistor's point made about as concrete as it gets.

And then there are the costs that never appeared on any ledger at all, because nobody had ever assigned them a line. The generational knowledge of where the shrimp run and when, passed father to son for a century, that is worth nothing in a damage assessment because it was never priced. A man who has spent forty years learning the moods of a particular stretch of water carries an instrument in his body that no school grants and no market sells, and when the water that taught it to him is poisoned, the instrument goes dark, and there is no line for that on any claim form. The cultural fabric of a fishing community, the bonds and the favors and the shared mornings that hold a place together, the way the watermen of any working coast hold one another together. The severed relationship between a people and a body of water that was, for them, not a resource but a home. Those were free to lose, precisely because they had no number. A cost set at zero is not a cost that does not exist. It is a cost the accounting refused to see, and the refusal is not an oversight. It is the engine. The whole arrangement runs on the things it is permitted not to count.

## Exxon Valdez

In the early hours of the twenty-fourth of March, 1989, the tanker Exxon Valdez ran aground on Bligh Reef in Prince William Sound, Alaska, and spilled some eleven million gallons of crude oil into one of the richest marine environments in North America. The cleanup ran for years. The legal aftermath ran far longer. A jury awarded billions in punitive damages, and over nearly two decades of appeals that award was cut down, and cut down again, until what finally survived was a fraction of what had first been ordered.

The Pacific herring fishery of the Sound collapsed in the years after the spill and never recovered to what it had been. The herring were a keystone, the forage fish that everything larger fed on, and the people whose livelihoods ran through them had no architecture through which to file for what they had lost. There is a particular cruelty in the timeline of that legal aftermath that is worth naming even in brief, because the reduction of the punitive award did not happen all at once but across nearly two decades, which means that many of the people who were owed the original sum waited so long for the appeals to grind through that the question of their compensation outlived a portion of the people themselves. Delay is its own kind of severance. A claim that is honored a generation late, at a fraction of its original size, has been quietly transferred in value from the injured to the party that injured them, and the transfer was accomplished not by any single decision anyone would defend out loud but by the ordinary, grinding patience of the legal architecture. The full accounting of this case, what it reveals when you decompose the damage line by line, belongs to the next chapter, and I will leave it there. For now it is one more entry in the same column: value extracted, cost severed onto a place and a people who could not relocate, and a legal aftermath that recovered a fraction of the harm.

## Baotou

If you have a smartphone in your pocket, or drive an electric car, or get some of your power from a wind turbine, you are connected by a supply chain to a place most people who use its products have never heard of. Baotou, in Inner Mongolia, is the center of a rare-earth processing complex that draws on the Bayan Obo deposit, one of the largest concentrations of these elements on the planet. Rare earths are the small, strange metals that make modern electronics work: the magnets, the phosphors, the catalysts. They are in the technologies we are counting on to carry us out of the fossil age.

Let me state the scale correctly, because the easy version overstates it. China accounts for roughly sixty percent of the world's rare-earth supply. Bayan Obo and the Baotou complex are not that whole sixty percent; they are roughly half of China's output. That is still an enormous share of a strategically critical resource flowing through one region.

The processing is brutal. Separating rare earths from the rock they come bound up with requires heavy use of acids and generates enormous volumes of toxic waste: heavy metals, residual acid, and, because these ores carry thorium, a measure of low-level radioactivity. The waste goes into tailings impoundments. A conservative figure for the wastewater intensity, drawn from the published work of Schreiber and colleagues and from the Chinese Society of Rare Earths, is on the order of ten tons of wastewater for every ton of rare-earth oxide produced, and that is the low end of the estimates. Residents near the impoundments report elevated rates of cancer, respiratory disease, and birth defects. Farmland within a radius of the complex has gone out of use.

I have to be honest with you about the data here, because the temptation is to reach for a dramatic round number, and the dramatic round number is not sourceable. Chinese restrictions limit independent health and environmental data from the region. That constraint is real, and I will not paper over it with a figure I cannot stand behind. The often-quoted claim of more than a hundred billion dollars in remediation cost is one I cannot trace to a defensible source, so I am not going to use it. What can be said responsibly is narrower and still damning: the peer-reviewed literature, work by Zhang and colleagues in 2022 and by Zhou and Ge in 2021, documents serious contamination and health effects; the annual environmental cost of the industry runs into the billions; industry revenue is on the order of ten to twelve billion dollars a year; and the share of that revenue that has historically gone to remediation is a small fraction. The cost that is not being paid is enormous, and saying exactly how enormous would require data the regime does not release.

Here is what makes Baotou the sharpest case of a particular kind. Most of the resources in this book come with an escape hatch. You can leave oil in the ground and invest the rents forward, the way Norway has done with its sovereign fund, turning a finite extraction into a permanent endowment. Rare earths offer no such bargain. There is no fund that reconstitutes a foreclosed deposit, because there is no engineered substitute at the scale the technologies require. When the substitutability of a resource runs that low, the framework's verdict sharpens to a hard edge: a severed cost like this cannot be compensated by reinvesting somewhere else. It can only be prevented, or paid. Baotou is where an abstract parameter about how substitutable a resource is stops being a line in an appendix and becomes a field of tailings ponds you could photograph.

And now the part I most need you to hold onto, because the whole book turns on it. The engineer designing the circuit board, the driver charging the electric car, the household drawing power from the wind farm, none of them is a villain. The mechanism does not need villains. The consumer at the end of that supply chain shares in the extracted value, the cheaper, better, cleaner-at-the-point-of-use technology, and is structurally separated from the cost by ten thousand miles and a dozen intermediaries. Whether any individual consumer is a good person is beside the structural point.

I want to dwell on this for a moment, because it is the hinge of the whole book and the easiest thing to misread. The instinct, when you learn what happens at Baotou, is to look for someone to blame, and the only person within reach is yourself, the buyer of the phone. That instinct is decent. It is also a trap, because it converts a structural problem into a personal guilt, and personal guilt is something you can discharge by feeling bad and then changing nothing. The point is not that you should feel guilty about the phone. The point is that the system has been built so that the cost of the phone never reaches you, never appears in its price, never enters the calculation you make when you buy it. You could not pay the true cost if you wanted to, because there is no mechanism that would let you. The spatial severance through a global supply chain is exactly what keeps the relationship between the consumer and the cost-bearer invisible. The distance is not an accident of the system. Manufacturing that distance is what the system does for a living. And the response to a manufactured distance is not personal abstinence, which changes the buyer's conscience and nothing else. It is an instrument that closes the distance, that puts the severed cost back into the price where the buyer would actually have to reckon with it. The mechanism reaches across political lines precisely here, because almost no one defends poisoning a village. What people disagree about is what to do, and you cannot even begin that conversation while the cost is hidden.

## 2008

Now the scale jumps again, and it jumps to the largest scale a single national economy can reach, because in the autumn of 2008 the mechanism struck the whole American economy at once. This is the case where there is no geographic alibi, no faraway town you can hold at arm's length, no cultural distance to hide behind. It reached into the retirement accounts of ordinary people across the entire country. It is the hardest case to look away from, and I am going to spend the most time here.

The 2008 crisis was a double transfer of risk, and to see it whole you have to start about thirty years before the crash.

The first transfer began in 1978, with a quiet change to the tax code that created a structure now known by the section number that authorized it, the 401(k). Over the following decades, American employers used it to replace the old defined-benefit pension, the kind that promised a worker a fixed monthly check for life, with the individual retirement account, the kind where the worker contributes and the worker bears the outcome. This was sold to workers as freedom. As ownership. As control over your own money, instead of dependence on a company promise. And there was something to that. But the part that went undisclosed was whose freedom it really was. It was, above all, the corporation's freedom from liability. The risks that the old pension had pooled on the company's balance sheet, the risk that markets would fall, that people would live longer than expected, that individuals would make poor choices under pressure, all of it moved off the institution and onto individuals who had no way to pool it or hedge it. One worker cannot diversify away the risk of retiring into a bad decade. The institution could. The transfer ran quietly for about thirty years and most people never noticed it happening.

The second transfer was compressed into a few years, roughly 2001 to 2008, and it ran the other direction, pushing risk back toward those same individual accounts through a different door. This was the era of the mortgage-backed security and the collateralized debt obligation, financial instruments built by slicing up home loans and reassembling the pieces. Their complexity was not incidental. It was structural. Only an instrument too complicated to evaluate could be sold at the prices these were sold at, to investors, including pension and retirement funds, who could not actually assess what they were buying. And these instruments found their way into the very same retirement accounts that the first transfer had created. So when the housing market turned and the instruments collapsed, in 2008, the losses landed in those accounts.

I want you to picture, without inventing a single specific person, the most ordinary form this took. The quarterly retirement statement that arrived in the spring of 2009. Same envelope, same typeface, same columns as every statement before it. The number that had climbed, more or less, for twenty years had fallen by an amount that erased years of saving. There was no one to call. There was no institution standing behind the number. The freedom the worker had been given turned out to mean that the loss, in full, was hers.

And for many households the statement was only one of two letters that arrived in those months. The other was about the house. The arithmetic of a foreclosure is colder than the arithmetic of a retirement account, because a house is not a number on a page; it is the place the children's heights are penciled on a door frame, the kitchen where the holidays happened, the address the schools and the doctors and the neighbors are organized around. When a household with little wealth and a large mortgage watched the home's value fall below what it owed, the equity that had been the family's entire net worth did not shrink. It went negative. The thing the family had been told was the safe, responsible, all-American place to put its money turned out to be the thing that took the rest of the money with it when it fell. None of these households had access to the rescue that was, in those same months, moving trillions of dollars to keep the institutions that had sold them the loans from failing. The institutions had a fire department. The households had a fundraiser at the church, if they were lucky, and a moving truck, if they were not.

Now widen the lens to the whole economy. American household net worth fell by roughly eleven trillion dollars in the crisis, by the Federal Reserve's own flow-of-funds accounting, its quarterly tally of the nation's balance sheet. That eleven trillion is the Fed's number, and I want to attribute it correctly, because the next part of the story comes from two economists whose own central figure is different and more specific.

Atif Mian and Amir Sufi, in their book House of Debt and in the research behind it, did not measure the total. They reconstructed how the loss was distributed, and the distribution is the heart of the matter. Their own central magnitude is the roughly 5.5 trillion dollars of housing wealth that evaporated, and what they showed about it is devastating in its simplicity. The loss did not fall evenly. It fell hardest on the households with the least wealth and the most debt, and it fell hardest on them because of the arithmetic of leverage. Consider two homeowners. One owns her house outright; when its value drops twenty percent, she loses twenty percent of that asset. The other put down a small fraction and borrowed the rest; when the same house drops the same twenty percent, the decline can wipe out her entire equity, because the debt does not shrink with the price. The poorer, more leveraged household loses a multiple of what the wealthier one loses on the identical price move. Mian and Sufi traced this through the spending data and found a differential in the marginal propensity to consume across the housing-wealth distribution on the order of three times, the lowest-wealth households cutting their spending far more sharply per dollar of lost wealth than the highest. The recession deepened precisely because the losses concentrated on the people who had no choice but to stop spending.

Then came the rescue, and the rescue is where the severance becomes impossible to miss. The Troubled Asset Relief Program committed seven hundred billion dollars. The Federal Reserve's emergency lending, on a cumulative basis across all its crisis facilities, exceeded sixteen trillion dollars, a figure I take from the Government Accountability Office's 2011 audit, which is the one large 2008 number that is genuinely well sourced. Executive bonuses at the rescued institutions resumed within roughly eighteen months. And against all of that mobilized capital, there were on the order of ten million foreclosure filings between 2008 and 2012, of which something like four to five million became completed foreclosures, roughly 4.1 million completed through 2012 by CoreLogic's count. The system found trillions of dollars in a matter of days for the institutions. It organized nothing of comparable scale for the households. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, in its 2011 report, concluded that the crisis was the result of human action and inaction, of choices, and not of forces beyond anyone's control.

That last point matters, because there are serious objections to the way I have just told this story, and I am not going to wave them off. There are four of them worth taking seriously, and I am going to state each one as strongly as its proponents would, and then answer it.

The first objection is about borrower agency. Many of the mortgages that defaulted went to borrowers who could not have serviced them under any reasonable assumption, and those borrowers signed the papers. Nobody forced the pen into their hands. To treat them only as victims is to deny them the dignity of their own choices. This is a real point, and the answer is not to deny the agency but to look at the conditions under which it was exercised. Agency requires information, and the information architecture was structurally adversarial. Origination commissions were tied to the volume of loans written, not to whether those loans were ever repaid, which rewarded the broker for closing the deal regardless of its soundness. Disclosed terms routinely differed from the terms buried in the contract. Adjustable-rate products were marketed specifically to borrowers whose grasp of how the rate would reset was expected to be incomplete, and the post-crisis enforcement record of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau documents the pattern in detail. The agency was real. The asymmetry of information was the lever that turned it against the people exercising it.

The second objection is that this was a global crisis, not a failure of American policy. European banks were heavily exposed, Asian economies caught the contagion through trade, and cross-border holdings of these securities spread the damage worldwide; the international Basel capital rules played their part. All true, and all of it amplifies the finding rather than dissolving it. The instruments that propagated the crisis around the world were predominantly American in origin, American mortgage-backed securities and the derivatives built on them. The originator-fee architecture that rewarded volume over quality, the conflict of interest by which the rating agencies were paid by the issuers whose products they rated, and the regulatory exemptions that let the whole structure scale, those were American policy choices. The world caught a fire that was lit in a particular place by particular decisions.

The third objection is the one that comes up most often, and it is that the bailout was repaid. The Treasury's accounting shows that most of the TARP money came back, and some retrospectives, including those from the GAO and the Congressional Budget Office, show a modest profit on parts of it. So where, the objection asks, is the unpaid cost? The answer is that TARP repayment is the wrong unit of account. The cost this chapter is pricing is not the federal government's outlay on the rescue. It is the unrecovered welfare loss to the foreclosed households who lost their homes; to the workers whose retirement balances reset to a lower base and now compound forward from that lower floor for the rest of their lives; to the regional economies that contracted and shed jobs; and to the reservoir of public trust that the asymmetry of the rescue itself drained, the lasting sense that the system mobilizes instantly for the powerful and slowly, if at all, for everyone else. None of those costs is recovered when TARP is repaid. None of them appears on the balance-sheet flow accounts the repayment argument cites. The thing that was repaid and the thing that was lost are not the same thing.

The fourth objection is that the law already fixed this, through the 2010 Dodd-Frank Act. And Dodd-Frank did real things. It created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the Financial Stability Oversight Council, it restricted banks from proprietary trading through the Volcker Rule, and it imposed tougher capital and liquidity requirements. Those are genuine reforms, and I want to be clear that this is a compliment, not a dismissal: Dodd-Frank is a partial, early installment of exactly the accountability architecture this book is arguing for. But it is partial, and it is reversible. The Volcker Rule was later relaxed. The consumer bureau was later weakened institutionally. The designations that mark certain firms as systemically important have been contested and rolled back. Reform that can be unwound has not closed the gap; it has narrowed it for a while. Chapter 9 returns to this and names Dodd-Frank among the entering wedges, the partial measures that show the architecture is buildable even though it has not yet been built to last.

I do not want to leave this case on the implication that the asymmetric rescue was inevitable, that a society facing a banking crisis simply has no choice but to save the institutions and let the households fall. It is not inevitable, and the cleanest proof is historical. Sweden in the 1930s faced a banking crisis structurally similar to the one the United States faced in 1929 to 1933 and again in 2008 to 2009, and Sweden combined the bank rescue with substantial relief for households. It left the gold standard and devalued its currency in 1931, which restored export competitiveness and eased the burden of household debt. It paired its bank rescue with mortgage modification. It put people back to work through public-works programs in the middle of the decade, restoring household income. And in 1938 the Saltsjöbaden Agreement institutionalized the cooperative welfare-state architecture that defined the country for the rest of the century. The comparative literature on banking crises, the work of historians like Kindleberger and Eichengreen, treats this as a standing demonstration that bank rescue and household relief are not an either-or, that a society can choose both. The United States chose otherwise in 2008 and 2009, and chose otherwise again in 2020. The choice was contingent. Chapter 9 develops the broader cross-country comparison; here I only need you to see that the road taken was a road chosen, not a road forced.

Three settings remain before this chapter pulls the thread together, and the next two move the mechanism into territory you might not expect to find it. So far the distance that severs the cost has been distance you could measure on a map. In the next case the distance is time itself. In the one after that, it is the boundary of a single human body.

## Social Security

The most devastating example in this chapter hides inside the one American institution that most people treat as the opposite of extraction. The promise that each working generation will care for the one before it. I want to say at the very start, and hold to it without wavering, that this is not a critique of Social Security. The program is genuine and essential. It has kept tens of millions of elderly Americans out of poverty. It is not a Ponzi scheme, and anyone who tells you it is has not understood it. My critique is narrow and specific. It is a critique of one feature of how the program's obligations were generated, and nothing else.

Here is how the system works. It was designed in 1935 as a pay-as-you-go arrangement: the payroll taxes of today's workers fund the benefits of today's retirees. For decades the demographics ran in the system's favor, with many workers for every retiree, and the system took in more than it paid out. The surplus accumulated in a trust fund, held in special-issue Treasury obligations. Then the demographics turned, exactly as everyone always knew they would, because the baby-boom retirement wave was visible on the horizon for forty years. By the early 2020s the program was paying out more than payroll taxes were bringing in, and under current law and current projections the trust fund is expected to deplete around 2033. After that, incoming payroll taxes would still cover roughly seventy-seven percent of promised benefits. The program does not vanish. It hits a wall it has known about for two generations.

Now I have to deal with a number, and this is the most consequential piece of figure discipline in the entire chapter, so I am going to be slow and explicit about it.

There is a figure that circulates in some critiques of Social Security, a claim of one hundred and eight trillion dollars in foregone compounding, presented as the wealth that would exist if the system's contributions had been invested in markets instead of run pay-as-you-go. You may have seen it called the largest cost severance in human history. I am not going to use it that way, because it does not survive contact with its own inputs. It is roughly two orders of magnitude larger than the nearest serious counterfactual anyone has constructed. It exceeds the trust fund's peak. It exceeds the program's entire cumulative income over its history. It exceeds even the theoretical ceiling you would get from the difference between bond and equity returns on every dollar that ever passed through. It is not a real magnitude. It is an artifact of compounding a number that was never available to be compounded. The only honest place for that figure in this book is as a named and dismantled example of how not to do this accounting.

So let me do the honest version. Across the program's life, cumulative contributions have run to something like twenty-six trillion dollars, and cumulative benefits paid have run to roughly the same, around twenty-six trillion. The money came in and went back out, by design, because that is what pay-as-you-go means. The only money that was ever actually sitting there available to invest was the running surplus, the accumulated reserve, which peaked at something on the order of 2.7 trillion dollars. If you take that genuinely investable base and ask what return it forwent by being held in Treasuries rather than in a diversified portfolio, compounded at a realistic real rate of return and net of the payouts the fund was simultaneously making, you get an answer in the low single-digit trillions. A few trillion dollars. Not a hundred. The honest floor is that the severed cost here runs into the trillions, and stating exactly which base compounds at what assumed rate net of what payouts is the difference between an honest estimate and a frightening one. For reference, the nearest external counterfactual, the kind of estimate the Brookings Institution or the Center for Retirement Research has produced on the assumption of a partial equity allocation beginning in the 1980s, comes in around one trillion dollars.

There is one more number to keep separate, because blurring it into the others is how the dishonest totals get built. The Social Security Trustees report a seventy-five-year actuarial shortfall, the gap between projected obligations and projected revenue over the long horizon, of roughly 22.6 trillion dollars. That is a real and serious figure, but it is a different quantity from the foregone-investment counterfactual, and the two must never be added together or substituted for each other to manufacture an impressive headline.

But I have been talking about the wrong thing, and I did it on purpose, to clear the brush. Because the cost severance in Social Security is not in how the surpluses were invested. Holding the reserve in Treasury obligations was administratively prudent; it is roughly what a careful fund manager would do. The severance is somewhere else entirely. It is in how the obligations were generated.

Think about the benefit expansions enacted across the 1960s, 1970s, and into the 1980s. Each was passed by a Congress whose members would, in nearly every case, have left office long before the demographic transition matured and the bills came due. Each was funded by the favorable ratio of workers to retirees that prevailed in exactly those decades, the demographic surplus that made expansion feel costless at the moment of the vote. And here is the part that removes any defense of ignorance: every actuarial report of the period openly projected the deficit window that the expansions would eventually run into. The actuaries drew the cliff on the map. The expansions were enacted anyway, during the window when the demographics made them painless, with the cost assigned to a future cohort of taxpayers who did not yet exist and who therefore could not be in the room to refuse.

That is the severance, and time is the distance that makes it possible. The electoral value of a generous promise was extracted at one moment, by the people who made it and the people who received it. The cost of funding that promise was assigned to a moment decades later, to people who had no vote at the time of the decision because they had not been born. You cannot say no to a bill that is addressed to you before you exist.

I want to be careful not to let this slide into a familiar and lazier complaint, the one that says politicians are simply irresponsible and short-sighted, as if the problem were the character of the people in office. The structural reading is more precise and more useful than that. A legislator who expands a benefit during a demographic surplus is not necessarily being cynical. He may sincerely believe the program is good, and the program may in fact be good. The point is that the incentive structure rewards him for the expansion and assigns the cost to a cohort that cannot vote against him, so that even a sincere and public-spirited legislator faces no countervailing pressure from the people who will eventually pay. Remove the bad actor entirely from the story and the severance still happens, because it is built into the relationship between the term of office and the demographic horizon. That is what makes it a structural problem rather than a moral one, and that is why the answer is an instrument, not an exhortation to elect better people. We have been electing people of every temperament for ninety years, and the gap has widened across all of them, because none of them was operating against a Foreclosure Bond that would have made the future's cost visible at the moment of the vote.

The contrast with Norway is exact, and I drew it in the chapter on the Norwegian fund, so I will only recall it here. Norway took its resource rents and channeled them into a sovereign wealth fund that compounds capital forward for future generations, deliberately accumulating against the obligations the future will carry. Social Security took the structurally identical problem, the question of how one generation provides for another across time, and answered it with a pay-as-you-go architecture chosen in 1935. Same structural problem. Different architectural choice. Different outcome. And the difference is not a matter of national wealth or demographic luck. It is a matter of which architecture was chosen at a specific moment by specific people.

Two objections deserve a hearing here, and both are good ones.

The first is the prudence objection. Holding the reserve in Treasuries, rather than in equities, is exactly what a responsible public fund manager should do, the objection says, because putting retirees' security at the mercy of the stock market would expose them to exactly the market and political-economy risk that a public pension exists to insulate them from. I concede this fully. It is correct at the level of portfolio management. But it answers a question I am not asking. My claim does not operate at the level of how the reserve was invested. It operates upstream, at the level of how the obligations were generated in the first place. The prudence defense and the severance claim pass each other in the dark; they are about different things.

The second is the tractability objection. The gap, this objection says, is mathematically solvable through a set of phased adjustments well understood by the Trustees and by every commission that has studied the program: modest increases in the payroll-tax rate, changes to the cap on taxable wages, refinements to the benefit formula at the high end, increases in the eligibility age tracking gains in longevity. The arithmetic closes. I concede the arithmetic completely; the gap is closeable. But notice what every one of those closures does. Each transfers the obligation onto the cohort caught in the demographic-deficit window, the people who will pay more or receive less, and not onto the cohorts who extracted the electoral value of generating the obligation in the first place, who are by now retired or gone. The gap closes by landing on exactly the people who could not refuse it. Tractability is not the absence of severance. It is severance unfolding precisely on schedule.

## The healthcare commons

The last case brings the mechanism all the way down to the smallest scale it can reach: the individual human body, at the most vulnerable stretches of a life, where the severed cost is the specific financial ruin of a specific household.

Start with what Americans have built, almost without deciding to, in place of a comprehensive medical safety net. They have built a crowdfunding website. Over the 2010s, medical crowdfunding grew from a curiosity into an institution. By 2020, more than a third of the campaigns on the largest platform were medical. Across roughly 2016 to 2020, the published research, principally the work of Igra and Kenworthy, counted on the order of 438,000 medical campaigns raising more than two billion dollars across roughly 21.7 million individual donations. And here is the part that tells you it is not a safety net at all: only about twelve percent of those campaigns met their stated goal, and about sixteen percent received nothing whatsoever. The platform's own chief executive has acknowledged that the site was never designed to be healthcare financing and became the default by accident, as desperate people found nowhere else to go.

I want to be careful with the comparative claim, because it is easy to overstate. It is sometimes said that the United States is the only wealthy nation where this happens, and that is not quite true; medical crowdfunding exists in Canada, the United Kingdom, Germany, and elsewhere. What is distinctive about the United States is the scale, and the role: not that the practice exists, but that it functions as a default backstop, doing the work a public system does elsewhere. The research of Saleh and colleagues, comparing the United States with Canada and the United Kingdom, documents the magnitude of that difference.

And the failures are not random. Medical campaigns started by people with distinctively Black or Hispanic surnames raise less money and are less likely to meet their goals, even after controlling for geography and the period of the campaign. Think about what that means. The informal backstop that grew up to catch the people the formal system failed reproduces the very inequities the formal system was supposed to reduce. A safety net keyed to the size and wealth of your personal network routes the most help to the people who need it least, because their networks are larger and richer, and the least help to the people whose networks are thin. It is charity with the distribution of capital, which is to say it is not a remedy for the problem; it is the problem wearing a kinder face.

Underneath the crowdfunding sits a structural shift that has been building for half a century. Since the early 1970s, as the work of Mishel and Bivens at the Economic Policy Institute documents, the median American wage has decoupled from productivity: the economy kept getting more productive, but the gains stopped flowing to the typical worker's paycheck. Over the same span, healthcare costs grew at multiples of general inflation. The household pressure that drives a family to a crowdfunding page is, in large part, simply the widening gap between those two curves, the flat wage and the rising cost, scissoring apart across a working life. The political scientist Jacob Hacker named the broader version of this in his book The Great Risk Shift: a decades-long transfer of economic risk from institutions onto individuals and families. The pension that became a 401(k) and the medical bill that became a fundraising page are not two different problems. They are the same shift, wearing different clothes.

The shift reaches its sharpest point at the end of a life, and here I am going to lean on a documented account rather than invent one, because the discipline of this book is to stay strictly within the record. I will not put words in anyone's mouth or scenes in anyone's house that the published record does not already hold. The journalist Katy Butler wrote a careful, sourced account of her own father's medical decline, and what it reveals about the incentives is precise, precise enough that you do not need to embellish it. The reimbursement architecture pays a physician a small fraction for a long office visit spent discussing whether an intervention is wise as it pays for performing the intervention itself, a procedure like the implantation of a cardiac pacemaker, a device whose manufacturer captures a substantial share of the payment. The liability architecture points the same direction: performing the procedure protects the physician against a malpractice claim, while declining it creates exposure. The conversation about whether to intervene is the cheap, unprotected path; the intervention is the lucrative, protected one. In Butler's family's case, the device sustained a body through years of subsequent cognitive decline, and the daily, unpaid labor of caring for that body fell on an elderly spouse, labor that appears on no ledger anywhere, that no accounting in the system was built to see. Think about where that labor sits in the accounting. The procedure was reimbursed; it generated revenue for a hospital, a physician, a device maker, and it appears, properly priced, in the gross domestic product. The years of care that the procedure made necessary were not reimbursed, because they were performed by a spouse for love and obligation, and so they appear nowhere, priced at zero, exactly the zero this book exists to refuse. The system metabolized the lucrative half of the decision and left the costly half to a person who never agreed to it, never billed for it, and could not have refused it without abandoning the person she loved. That is cost severance at the scale of a single household, and it is happening, right now, in tens of thousands of homes where the lights are on late and no one is keeping a ledger.

The physician and writer Atul Gawande described the systemic structure that produces this pattern at scale in his book Being Mortal. The reimbursement system rewards intervention at every decision point while underfunding the palliative-care infrastructure and training that would support a different kind of end. The aggregate result is the over-treatment of the dying, the individual case reproduced across the whole system. And the crucial thing is that no individual clinician has to choose extraction over compassion for this to happen. The pattern emerges from rational responses to incentives, and the incentives are themselves a policy-design choice. The same logic reaches into maternal care, into mental-health care, into the management of chronic disease. The body is the final commons, and it is being mined at the bedside by a system in which, once again, there need be no villain at all.

## The pattern made visible

Set the cases side by side and the shape stands out clean.

In each one, an entity, a company in some, a whole political generation in another, a national financial system in a third, extracts value from a resource or a system that could have been managed for the long term. The extraction generates costs: environmental in Montana and the Gulf and Inner Mongolia, financial in 2008, intergenerational in Social Security, medical and personal in the healthcare cases, and in several of them all of these at once. And those costs are severed, cut loose from the party that caused them and set down onto parties with minimal power to prevent the transfer: workers, communities, ecosystems, the future, the dying.

A word about that word, severance, because it has a second life that could mislead you, and I would rather name the risk than let it sit. Severance is also what a company calls the check it writes when it lets a worker go. A reader who hears that meaning first might think the term smuggles in exactly the frame this book resists, the treatment of a working life as a line item to be settled and closed. It does the opposite. Both senses share a root precisely because both name a cut. The worker is cut loose from the wage; the cost is cut loose from the party who caused it. The book keeps the word because the cut is the thing that matters, and softening the word would do in language exactly what the accounting does in fact, which is make the severing disappear from view.

There are recurring covers under which the original beneficiary escapes the full reckoning, and you have seen all of them in the cases. Distance, the ten thousand miles between the consumer and the tailings pond. Time, the decades between the benefit and the bill. The asymmetry of what is known, the company that measured the asbestos and kept mining. The asymmetry of who can refuse, the future taxpayer who was not yet born. The normalization of a cost as routine, the way a chronic harm stops registering once it has gone on long enough. And the sheer appearance of abundance, the sense that there is plenty, that prevents a cost from registering at all until the plenty is gone. The book returns to these covers in a later chapter and names them as a set; here I only want you to feel them operating, one or two in each case, never the same combination twice.

So run the roll-call of who absorbed it. The town that could not move. The ecosystems and the fishing communities that could not relocate. The rural communities near the tailings ponds with no exit and no voice. The foreclosed households. The future Americans who cannot opt out of an inherited obligation they had no vote in creating. The households whose crowdfunding campaigns fail, and the elderly spouse whose caregiving hours appear on no ledger anywhere.

There is a grim symmetry to the escape, once you line the cases up. The covers are not random; they sort by who is positioned to use them. The company uses the corporate form and the bankruptcy discharge, legal modules built precisely for the orderly shedding of liability. The political generation uses the calendar, the simple fact that a term of office is shorter than a demographic transition. The financial system uses complexity, instruments engineered to be unevaluable so that the risk could be sold to people who could not see it. And the healthcare system uses the boundary of the body and the privacy of the home, the way a family's quiet ruin stays a family's quiet ruin and never aggregates into a number anyone has to answer for. Different covers, same function. Each one puts distance between the party that took the value and the party that holds the cost, and distance is the whole game.

The companies declare bankruptcy. The politicians leave office. The promise-making generations pass away. The costs remain, transferred to whoever cannot escape them.

And I want to state the structural claim as plainly as I can, without a villain in it, because the absence of the villain is the whole point. This is not a system failing. It is a system working as designed. Not by anyone's conscious intention to do harm, but by the accumulated logic of structures that reward the extraction of value and have no instrument to price what the extraction forecloses. The deep version of the claim is that extractive arrangements, wherever the taking has outrun the restoring, have at bottom operated this way for a very long time, and I do not want to leave that as a free-floating assertion, because it is too large a claim to leave hanging. It has a lineage, a tradition of thinkers who diagnosed exactly this asymmetry before this book existed, and I am going to discharge the claim against them in what follows.

The forward question is not whether this is happening. The cases prove that it is happening. The question is what it would look like to build accountability that interrupts the pattern before the costs are severed, rather than chasing them afterward, once the beneficiaries have already departed and left no forwarding address.

## The same object, named honestly

I have been pricing things that a lot of careful people believe should never be priced. A reef. A community. A human body at the end of its life. The unborn. And I need to meet the strongest version of the objection to that, head on, because the objection is not foolish, and the book's central vulnerability lives exactly here. If I am not careful, what I am proposing looks like the very thing I am criticizing.

This is the place I have been building toward for the whole chapter, and I want to slow down for it, because if the argument fails here it fails everywhere. A reader who has followed me through Libby and the Gulf and Baotou and 2008 might reasonably be feeling a quiet unease that has nothing to do with whether the cases are true. The unease is this: if the answer to a poisoned village or a foreclosed household is to put a dollar figure on the harm, then have I not just agreed with the extractor about the most basic thing, that everything is for sale and the only argument is over the price? Have I not handed the world a new way to buy the right to ruin a reef, so long as the buyer can afford the line item? That worry is correct to have. It is the worry I would have. And the only way to answer it is to be precise about what a price is and what it does, which means being precise about what a commons is in the first place.

So let me start by saying what a commons is, structurally, because the structural definition is what makes the rest of the argument hold. A commons, in the sense this book uses, is not defined by being ecological. It is defined structurally: it is anything that is depended on and shared, but was never produced as a commodity for sale. On that definition, a reef and the atmosphere and a community and a body of human labor and a future generation are, structurally, the same kind of object. Each is a commons whose apparent abundance has masked its real cost. And the same apparatus that prices one can price all of them, running forward to price the foreclosure of an ecological or atmospheric future, and running backward to price the human extraction that has already happened.

The philosopher Michael Sandel has made the strongest case against doing what I am doing, and I want to state it at full strength. Putting a price on a sacred landscape, or on a community, or on the unborn, Sandel argues, launders the priceless into a transaction. It concedes the extractor's own frame, the frame in which everything has a price and the only question is what it is. Worse, monetizing a harm can legitimate it: once there is a price for ruining a thing, ruining it becomes a purchase that anyone with enough money is entitled to make. Pricing the commons, on this view, is not a defense of the commons. It is the final stage of its commodification. His book What Money Can't Buy is the sustained version of the worry, and I am not going to engage it somewhere else and gesture at it here. I am going to answer it here.

The answer turns on a single distinction. A cost set at zero is not a cost left sacred and unpriced. Zero is a price. It is the price the extractive accounting already assigned, and it is the most consequential price in the whole system, because it is the number that made the extraction profitable in the first place. When the asbestos in the dust was treated as costless, that was not the reef left holy and untouched by the market. That was the market pricing the lungs of Libby at zero. The framework's move is not to introduce a price where there was a reverent absence of one. It is to refuse the zero that was already there. Pricing a thing in order to reserve against its loss, in order to re-embed it in the web of obligations it was cut out of, is the opposite of pricing it in order to sell it. The number is not an invitation to buy. It is a wall built against the sale.

This is the point at which the lineage matters, because I am not the first to make this move, and the tradition I am standing in is precisely the tradition that refused commodification most fiercely.

Karl Polanyi, in The Great Transformation, written in 1944, gave us the idea of the fictitious commodity. Labor, land, and money, he argued, are treated by the market as if they were commodities produced for sale, when in fact none of them was. Labor is just human life; land is just nature; money is just a token of trust. To run them through the market as commodities is to subject the substance of society itself to the logic of the price, and the result, Polanyi argued, is a society that tears at its own fabric until it is forced to protect itself. The structural definition of a commons I just gave you is the operational descendant of Polanyi's fictitious-commodity thesis. The diagnostic move at the heart of this book, imagine the commons gone and ask what cost appears, is Polanyi's intuition turned into a procedure. And the instrument the book proposes is, quite precisely, Polanyi's double movement, the countermovement by which a society re-embeds what the market disembedded, built into an accounting tool. The framework is Polanyi's double movement made into an instrument. It prices and reserves against the very thing he could only diagnose.

The closest living anchor for the meta-claim is Nancy Fraser, and naming her here pays off a debt the book has been carrying since an earlier chapter, where I planted the idea of the severed and unpaid without giving it her name. Fraser distinguishes between exploitation and expropriation. Exploitation is what capital does through the wage relation: it takes, but it takes through the form of a contract, an exchange, however unequal. Expropriation is what capital does behind the wage relation, to the unpaid labor of social reproduction and to nature, taking without even the form of a contract. It takes from you without so much as the pretense of an exchange. The book's same-object meta-claim, that extracting from a reef and extracting from a community are, priced backward, the same move, is Fraser's terrain exactly. The expropriation she theorizes is the structural mechanism this whole chapter has been documenting case by case. Her work, in the essay behind Marx's hidden abode and in the book that grew from it, is the contemporary anchor for the claim. The Chapter that planted the seed comes due here, in her name.

Now I have to be honest about something, because the verified positioning of this book depends on it. Polanyi and Fraser, and the whole tradition they belong to, resist the monetization of the commons on principle. They would not, in all likelihood, endorse the act of putting a dollar figure on a reef or a community. I am not going to claim that they would. That would be to enlist them in something they spent their careers warning against. What I am claiming is narrower and, I think, defensible. The framework operationalizes their diagnosis, not their prescription, and it operationalizes it in the direction of cost-reckoning rather than commodification. It uses their account of what marketization does in order to force an honest accounting of what marketization costs. A framework that stands inside the tradition that refused the asset frame, and that uses the refusal rather than betraying it, is doing something different from the financialization the tradition opposed. The bond is the double movement made into an accounting tool. It is not the commodity form extended to one more thing.

But I can hear the sharper version of the objection forming, and it deserves a sharper answer. A critic might say that all my talk of cost-reckoning is a distinction without a difference, that pricing the commons is financialization by another route, that whatever I call it, I am taking a reef and a community and the atmosphere and turning them into a new asset class, the commons repackaged as something to be held, hedged, and traded. Part of the answer is the lineage I just named: claiming Polanyi and Fraser, the thinkers who diagnosed commodification as the harm, marks the difference between pricing to force an honest accounting and financializing the commons as an asset. Accounting a cost is not coding an asset.

But I would rather show you the difference than assert it, and to show it I am going to use the legal scholar's own terms. Katharina Pistor, whose work on the legal coding of capital I drew on at Libby, identifies four legal attributes that turn an ordinary asset into capital: priority, which ranks one claim ahead of others; durability, which makes a claim survive over time and through the holder's troubles; universality, which makes it good against the world rather than just against a counterparty; and convertibility, which lets it be turned, on demand, into state money. Those four attributes are what let capital circulate, accumulate, and ride out a crisis. Now look at the instrument this book proposes, the accountability bond, and check it against the list. It is engineered to lack the two attributes that let capital move. It is not convertible: it can be redeemed only for the discharge of the specific liability it records, not for money, not for any tradable claim, not for anything but the very obligation it exists to settle. And it is not transferable: it cannot be sold, traded, securitized, or repackaged into anything that circulates. A debt that can only be paid, never traded, is the structural opposite of a coded asset. That is the difference between coding an asset and accounting a cost, shown in the code's own terms rather than asserted against them.

The most forceful living version of the financialization worry comes from Brett Christophers, whose work on the asset economy and on rentier capitalism is the deepest contemporary statement of the concern that everything is being turned into something to be owned and extracted from. The answer in principle is the one I have just given, that accounting a cost is the opposite of coding an asset, and the fuller engagement with his specific argument belongs to a later chapter, where the book takes up rentier capitalism directly. I plant the marker here and leave it.

This passage does triple duty, and that is why it is the most important new thing in the chapter. It anchors the structural-commons identity, the claim that the reef and the community and the future are the same object priced in different directions. And from that single position, the position Polanyi and Fraser built, it disarms the whole cluster of objections that say the framework's own pricing is the commodification it set out to criticize: Sandel's worry about pricing the sacred, the legal-coding worry about financialization, the worry that the severance metaphor smuggles in a commodifying frame, and the worry that pricing flattens what cannot be flattened. They all fall to the same distinction, drawn from the same tradition. Refusing the zero is not the same as setting a price to sell.

## Architecture and rent-seeking: who shaped the system?

Before this book had a name for any of this, there was a tradition that had already diagnosed the asymmetry that every case in this chapter turns on. A powerful party that captures value shifts its costs onto a cost-bearer who is absent, or diffuse, or dominated, someone who is not in the room and cannot push back. I owe that tradition a debt, and I want to pay it by name.

The economist K. William Kapp, in his 1950 book The Social Costs of Private Enterprise, did something that was unusual for its moment: he systematically accounted for the costs that business socializes, the harms it pushes off its own books and onto the community, the environment, and the worker. Kapp wanted those social costs reckoned. He is the closest ally this framework has in the older literature, because his project and this one point in the same direction. Where he insisted that the socialized costs be counted, this book takes up the work of counting them, per extraction event, with an instrument he did not have. The framework continues Kapp's project.

The ecological economist Joan Martínez-Alier mapped the same asymmetry in the register of political ecology, through what he calls ecological-distribution conflicts: the systematic study of who bears the ecological cost of an economy and who captures its value, and how those two populations are almost never the same people. The whole spread of cases in this chapter is a catalog of the conflicts he taught a generation to see. But here I have to draw the careful line I drew with Polanyi and Fraser, because Martínez-Alier draws it himself. He rejects monetary valuation on principle. His position, grounded in the old argument about incommensurability that runs back through Otto Neurath, is that the things at stake in these conflicts cannot be put on a single monetary scale without losing exactly what matters about them. So I credit his diagnosis of the asymmetry, which is precise and indispensable, and I honor his refusal of the pricing. I do not claim he would endorse what this book does with the diagnosis. The diagnosis is his. The decision to price is mine, and I will not borrow his authority for a move he declined to make.

These traditions named the asymmetry. The framework's contribution is to operationalize the diagnosis by pricing it. And this is the place where the large claim from the previous section, that extractive economies have at bottom operated this way for a long time, finally discharges, because it is not my assertion floating free. It is Kapp's accounting and Martínez-Alier's mapping and Polanyi's double movement, the diagnosis a century of thinkers built, which this book takes up and turns into an instrument.

Now, the framework's apparatus answers one specific question: who bore the cost. It builds the ledger of where the severed costs landed and on whom. But there is a second, distinct, and necessary question that the ledger does not answer on its own. Who shaped the architecture that distributed the cost the way it did. Who built the legal and regulatory and political structures that decided the cost would flow downhill to the people least able to refuse it, rather than staying with the party that caused it.

For that question, there is a rigorous toolkit already built, and it comes from the Public Choice tradition, the work of James Buchanan, Gordon Tullock, and those who followed them. Tullock's 1967 analysis of rent-seeking and Mancur Olson's 1965 work on how concentrated interests organize themselves more easily than diffuse ones gave us a precise vocabulary for the question of who shapes the rules. Where the extractors and the cost-bearers are clearly distinct populations, the rent-seeking chain runs cleanly, and you can trace it: identify the concentrated-interest actors who shaped the architecture, the lobbying expenditure and the coalitions that produced the design, whose interest the design served and whose it cost.

Run the cases through that lens and a roster appears. At Libby, the documented lobbying and the sought-after delays in asbestos regulation that Grace's own released internal documents reveal. At Deepwater Horizon, the Minerals Management Service, the agency that was supposed to regulate offshore drilling and had instead become a textbook captured regulator, its personnel accepting industry-funded travel and gifts, the revolving door turning between the agency and the firms it oversaw, the capture so well documented that the Interior Department's inspector general had reported on it before the blowout and the agency was split apart in the restructuring after. At 2008, the chain of deregulatory choices, the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999 that tore down the wall between commercial and investment banking, the Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000 that kept the derivatives at the center of the crisis out of regulatory reach, the preemption rulings by which the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency shielded national banks from state consumer protections, all of them shaped by financial-industry lobbying documented by campaign-finance watchdogs and laid out in congressional hearings. In healthcare, the pharmaceutical lobby, consistently among the largest in the country, and the long-standing legal prohibition, only recently modified, on Medicare negotiating drug prices, which is about as clean a rent-seeking outcome as exists. And forward, into the chapter on McDowell County, the multi-generational history of coal's dominance over the legislative and regulatory architecture of West Virginia.

There are cases where this lens does not cut as cleanly, and I want to name them honestly rather than force them. In Baotou, the supply chain is shaped by global consumer demand as much as by any identifiable concentrated interest, and the line between extractor and cost-bearer blurs. In Social Security, the dynamic is intertemporal, and today's beneficiaries are in many cases yesterday's payers, so the extractor and the cost-bearer can be the same person at different ages. Where the rent-seeking actor is hard to identify, the framework's structural accounting carries the weight that the actor-identification cannot.

I want to be exact about the relationship here, because it is easy to misread. The framework draws on the rent-seeking vocabulary; it does not endorse the larger Buchanan project, and it does not need to. The analytical tools, the concentrated-versus-diffuse interest, the rent-seeking chain, the captured regulator, survive many different readings of Public Choice's broader political commitments. They are in the toolkit, not in the opposing camp. The framework's accounting of who bore the cost supplies precisely the ledger to which the Public Choice analysis of who shaped the architecture applies. Readers who want the architecture-shaping analysis at full strength should read Buchanan, Tullock, and their successors in parallel with this book, and I mean that as a genuine invitation, including to the readers at institutions like the American Enterprise Institute, Cato, Mercatus, and George Mason, who have built that tradition and who will find here a ledger their own tools were made to work on. The later chapter on the political economy of reform develops the complement at greater length.

## Restitution and Foreclosure

The cases produce a question, and the rest of the book is the answer to it. What would an honest accounting of all these severances actually require?

The accountability gap has two temporal faces, and any architecture that addresses one without the other addresses only half the problem. There is harm already done: the realized portfolio of extraction, the lives and communities and ecosystems already damaged, the cost already severed and sitting where it landed. And there is harm not yet done: the future costs that current extraction is foreclosing right now, the options being barred to generations that cannot yet speak. A response that only cleaned up the past would leave the future to be severed all over again. A response that only protected the future would abandon everyone already standing where the cost fell.

So the framework proposes two coordinated instruments, named here for the first time and formalized in the chapters that follow. The Restitution Bond discharges harm already realized; it is the backward-looking instrument, the one that answers to the people and places that have already been harmed. The Foreclosure Bond discharges the future-generation costs that current extraction is foreclosing; it is the forward-looking instrument, the one that answers to the people who do not yet exist. Together they form the aggregate accountability bond the framework's central relation requires, where the cost severance is what remains when you subtract the bond posted from the residual value of the commons.

I want to name these two instruments slowly, because this is the first place in the book they are named at all, and a name that is rushed is a name that will be misheard for nine chapters. The Restitution Bond looks backward. It answers to harm that has already happened, to the lungs of Libby and the herring of the Sound and the foreclosed households, to the realized portfolio of damage that is already sitting where it landed. The Foreclosure Bond looks forward. It answers to harm that has not yet happened but is being set in motion right now, to the options the present is barring to a future that cannot speak for itself. They are not two versions of the same instrument. They answer to two different obligations, one of which we owe to people who exist and one of which we owe to people who do not yet exist, and conflating them is one of the most common ways this kind of argument goes wrong. Keep them separate and each does its work cleanly.

Why a bond, and not one of the more familiar instruments? Because the alternatives each fail at a specific point. A tax is paid at the moment of the transaction into general revenue, and it builds no architecture that matches a specific posted amount to a specific future obligation; the money goes into the pot and the obligation goes unmet. A fee licenses an activity rather than discharging a harm; it is permission to proceed, not provision against the consequence. A fund aggregates money without discharging any particular liability; it accumulates but it does not answer to anyone in particular. A bond does what none of those does. It is posted before the full cost is borne. It carries forward across the long tail of the cost, the decades over which the harm actually unfolds. And it discharges observably, a specific figure paid against a specific liability, so that you can see whether the obligation has been met.

The idea is not exotic, and it has real-world ancestors that the book draws on. Reclamation bonds under the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977 require a mining operator to post money against the future cost of restoring the land. Decommissioning bonds in oil and gas require an operator to post against the eventual cost of closing a well. Norway's sovereign fund is the same logic at the scale of a nation, capital posted forward against a future the present is drawing down. The reparations-economics tradition treats a historical injustice as a documented debt, which is structurally bond-like in exactly the way the Restitution Bond needs. And the civic-republican tradition gives us a standing obligation not to dominate future generations, an obligation for which a posted bond is the natural accounting form.

A word on why the instrument is called Restitution while the tradition that informs it is called Reparations, because the difference is deliberate and not a matter of euphemism. Restitution, in the old legal-philosophical sense of restitutio in integrum, restoration to the whole, is the broader and older term for the obligation to restore what was taken. The framework needs an umbrella wide enough to cover the full range of cost-severance contexts, environmental, occupational, communal, ecological, and Restitution is that umbrella. The reparations tradition, the work of Ta-Nehisi Coates and of the economists William Darity and Kirsten Mullen and Darrick Hamilton and the scholars around them, is the primary source that informs the backward-looking instrument, the discipline from which the framework learned most of what it knows about backward-looking accountability. To call the instrument the Reparations Bond would narrow that umbrella to one historically specific application and would make the portability of the method read as a loose analogy rather than the direct application it actually is. The narrower alternatives fail for their own reasons: Restoration presupposes a physical restorability that many of these harms do not allow, Indemnification routes the whole thing through an insurance register the framework does not use, and Damages collapses the bond into the mere measurement of the harm. The framework joins the reparations tradition rather than translating it or rebranding it. In the language Darity and Mullen use, with their three-part account of redress as acknowledgment and atonement, then redress proper, then closure, the Restitution Bond is the instrument that operationalizes the material-restitution component of redress. I want to be exact about the relationship to their work, because it would be easy to get it backward. Darity and Mullen did not merely diagnose a harm and leave the magnitude to someone else. They already priced it; they computed reparations magnitudes through a rigorous matched-comparison method. The framework does not supply a number they were missing. It ports their method to other extraction-community contexts and specializes it. McDowell County's wealth collapse measured against comparable non-extraction Appalachian counties. Libby's cumulative health-cost burden measured against comparable communities that did not have a Grace mine. The opioid devastation of Appalachia measured against comparable counties that the documented marketing maps of a company like Purdue did not concentrate on. That is the same matched-comparison discipline, applied to new contexts. It is a method-port, not a magnitude-supply. The framework joins the tradition by doing its work.

The choice of the word Foreclosure for the forward instrument is just as deliberate, and the reader who hears the housing crisis of 2008 in it is meant to. That is continuity, not coincidence. Foreclosure, from the old sense of barring-out, describes both events exactly. In the mortgage sense, a household is barred from a primary asset by a legal architecture that lets the bar fall at scale. In the framework's sense, future generations are barred from their options in the commons, a habitable atmosphere, intact ecosystems, irreplaceable material reserves, by a legal and economic architecture that lets the bar fall across time. Same mechanism, different substrate. The alternatives miss the feature that matters: Future-Generations reads as soft philanthropy, Sustainability has been co-opted into corporate ESG branding, Reclamation is a narrower technical term from mining law, and Conservation and Intergenerational both miss the specific sense of being barred out.

The forward instrument needs its own grounding, and the lineage divides cleanly along the temporal seam. On the environmental-economics side, John Hartwick's rule, formulated in 1977, holds that the rents from depleting a finite resource should be invested so that the capacity to produce well-being is maintained across generations; the reclamation-bond tradition and Norway's fund are its applications. That is the foreclosure-prevention lineage, and it should be kept distinct from the reparations lineage, because the two faces of the bond answer to different obligations.

On the moral-grounding side, the forward instrument needs an answer to a hard question: how can we owe anything to people who do not yet exist, and whose very existence and identity depend on the choices we make? Derek Parfit, in Reasons and Persons, gives the framework the level at which the obligation can stand. The obligation operates at the level of outcomes rather than of specific persons; we can be obligated to bring about a better state of the world for whoever lives in the future, even if we cannot point to the particular individuals. I want to be candid that this does not dissolve the non-identity problem Parfit himself left open, the puzzle that our choices determine who the future people are, so that they cannot straightforwardly be said to be harmed by being brought into a worse world rather than a better one. The framework names that as a standing limit, an open question, not a defeated one.

This is the place to be precise about the role the civic-republican tradition plays, because it would be easy to overload it. Philip Pettit in Republicanism and Quentin Skinner in Liberty Before Liberalism developed the idea of freedom as non-domination, the condition of not being subject to another's arbitrary power, and that idea extends naturally across generations: to foreclose the future's options is to dominate the future, to subject it to a power it cannot contest. The Foreclosure Bond is the accounting form of the commitment not to dominate the future. But I want to assign the load carefully. Non-domination does its strongest work on the within-generation harms this chapter has documented, the worker, the community, the foreclosed household, people who are subject right now to a power they cannot refuse. For the forward arm, the intergenerational arm, the primary grounding leans on the ethics of how we weigh the future: on Parfit's standing claim, and on the ethics of discounting that the next chapter takes up at length, the work of the philosopher John Broome, who argues that the discount rate we apply to future harms is a moral choice and not a technical one, that pure time preference has no ethical defense, and that a generation that imposes a climate harm on its successors owes them restitution for it. The civic-republican reading is a genuine complement to that forward grounding, and a valuable one, but it is the complement, not the load-bearing frame. The forward arm rests primarily on the intergenerational ethics.

The rhetorical architecture of the backward instrument has a companion too, in Ta-Nehisi Coates's argument for reparations, which named a mechanism, named a harm, and named a debt, and made the naming itself the argument. The framework's backward instrument is a structural extension of that architecture into extraction-community contexts. And the empirical method that gives the backward instrument its rigor comes from a specific body of work: Darity and Mullen's From Here to Equality as the contemporary landmark; the matched-comparison method of Umbrellas Don't Make It Rain, by Darrick Hamilton and William Darity and their colleagues, with its finding that Black households at the highest stratum of educational achievement hold less wealth than white households at lower strata, which establishes that the structural variable is wealth and not behavior; and, earlier, Dalton Conley's Being Black, Living in the Red, which established wealth as the structural variable a generation before. I should note one discipline of citation: I rely here on the published work of these scholars, and not on any private or unpublished communication treated as field consensus or as an endorsement of this framework, which it is not.

There is one contemporary field that already does what this framework's apparatus does, running the accounting in both directions at once, and intellectual honesty requires that I concede its priority plainly. The work of Andrew Fanning and Jason Hickel on compensation for the appropriation of the atmospheric commons, published in 2023, together with the reparatory-justice plan advanced by the CARICOM group of nations and the Loss and Damage Fund established at the United Nations climate conferences in 2022 and made operational in 2023, prices an over-appropriated atmospheric commons as a debt that runs in both directions: backward, for historical emissions already released, and forward, for the climate stability being foreclosed, built on the spine of the social cost of carbon. That program reached the bidirectional pricing of a global commons before this book did, and it argues the case at the scale at which the problem is usually argued, the national aggregate, the question of who owes whom across a shared global carbon budget. What this framework adds is not the idea. It is the grain. The framework prices a single extraction event rather than a national balance, and it reserves against that one event rather than netting one nation's account against another's. The idea of pricing an over-appropriated commons in both directions is theirs, and they got to it first; the per-event resolution is what the framework contributes. I want to be careful to stay inside a boundary here. I credit the field, and I name no atmospheric or climate-reparations dollar figure, and I take no position on who owes whom. That is not a number this book is in a position to assign.

That boundary holds for the whole backward direction, and I want to state it before the chapter closes its diagnostic. The same apparatus that prices the foreclosure of a future runs in reverse to price realized human extraction, and the most developed work in that reverse direction is the reparations economics already named, pricing the racial wealth gap and the gap in life expectancy between Black and white Americans as a measurable legacy of a documented history, with McDowell County's life-expectancy gap as the coal-extraction variant of the same legacy-effect method. But there is a vector here the framework does not reach, and I will not pretend otherwise. The direct pricing of coercion itself, what to assign to coerced labor as a cost dimension distinct from its measurable downstream legacy effects, remains methodologically unresolved in the reparations-economics field, and it is outside this book's empirical scope. The framework reaches coerced cases only through their legacy effects: the wealth gaps, the longevity gaps, the disruption of knowledge transmission, the intergenerational trauma. The direct pricing of the coercion vector is an open question this book defers, and the moral weight of it I leave to those with the standing to carry it. Indigenous land dispossession and the cultural knowledge severed by colonization sit in the same deferred territory, structurally reachable by the same method applied backward, computable in subsequent work, but not in this book. I will not produce a coercion or reparations dollar figure in these pages. Crediting the field that does this work is something I am glad to do. Filling in that column is not mine to do.

Which brings the chapter to its diagnostic close, the empirical state of bond posting across American extraction regimes, stated as a fact and not a metaphor. The bond that has actually been posted is, almost everywhere, a small fraction of the residual commons value, short by orders of magnitude. The clearest place to see it is the program built for coal's most documented occupational harm. The federal Black Lung program has paid roughly forty-four billion dollars through 2009, by the Government Accountability Office and Congressional Research Service accounting, a conservative and honestly-dated floor for the full run of the program, because no authoritative cumulative total exists for the years since. And the program's trust fund carried roughly 5.1 billion dollars in outstanding debt as of September 2024, by the Department of Labor and the GAO, a separate quantity from the cumulative paid, and I keep them separate on purpose. Set against the severance that disease was supposed to compensate, even those figures fall short, and there is a deeper problem inside them. The instrument exists at scale, but it is mis-assigned in part. When the coal excise tax that is supposed to fund the trust falls short, the difference is borrowed from the federal general fund, which is to say from the public, rather than recovered from the extraction that severed the cost in the first place. The party that benefited is not the party paying. Reclamation bonds across the mining regimes cover several billion dollars less than the actual cost of reclamation. And against the Social Security intergenerational obligation, there is no Foreclosure Bond posted at all. This is the accountability gap, stated as a measurable fact: the bond posted is a small fraction of the cost severed. The next chapter supplies the methodology for measuring it. The chapter on the political economy of reform supplies the path toward closing it.

I should say, finally, what kind of claim this is and is not, because the distinction governs everything the rest of the book does. The question of what is owed is a separate question from what any society is currently prepared to collect. The methodology applies regardless of whether the bond is politically postable today. The framework's contribution at this point is accounting, not advocacy. The political path from where bond levels currently sit toward the residual commons value is a genuinely open question, scoped as a matter of political philosophy rather than a single prescription the book legislates, and it belongs to a later chapter.

The chapter has done its work. It has named the severance and traced it across the cases, from a town to an ecosystem to a supply chain to a whole economy to time itself to the individual dying body. It has met the strongest counter-positions on the financial crisis and on Social Security. It has engaged the commodification worry through Polanyi and Fraser and Sandel, and shown that refusing the zero is not the same as setting a price to sell. It has restored the rent-seeking complement and credited the traditions that diagnosed the asymmetry first. And it has named the two-instrument response and laid out its lineage. What remains is the methodology that measures the severance, the stress-test that asks whether the framework holds everywhere, the single worked number that proves it can be done, the political economy of building the architecture, and the close. The cases have settled the first question past arguing. The question that remains open is not whether cost severance exists. It is what we are going to do about it.
