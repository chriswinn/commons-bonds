# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 5: The Accountability Gap

## The pattern you cannot stop seeing

Once you have seen it once, you see it everywhere, and you cannot make yourself stop.

An entity takes value out of a resource, a community, or a system. That much is ordinary, and most of the time it is even good. Value gets made, and someone collects it, and the world goes on. But alongside the value the taking generates a cost. The cost is real. It has to land somewhere. And the way these arrangements are built, the cost does not land on the party that collected the value. It is detached, lifted off the books, and set down somewhere else, on a party with less power, less money, less mobility, less ability to say no. Then the years pass. And when the reckoning finally arrives, as it always does, the entity that collected the value is no longer reachable to pay. The company has dissolved. The executives have retired. The generation that made the promise has died. The cost remains, sitting on whoever happened to be standing nearest when it came down.

The bill always arrives. The only open question is who is still standing where it lands.

I want to walk you through a spread of cases, because the mechanism is easiest to disbelieve when you see it once and easiest to believe when you see it seven times in seven different costumes. A vermiculite mine in a small Montana town. An oil platform in the Gulf of Mexico. A tanker on a reef in Alaska. A rare-earth complex in Inner Mongolia. The entire financial system of the United States in 2008. The retirement compact between the generations. And the healthcare system that has quietly made a fundraising website its safety net of last resort. The arithmetic is different in every one. The mechanism is identical in every one. That sameness, surfacing across geography and industry and decades, is the thing I want you to feel before I name it, because if you feel it first you will trust it later, when I start to take it apart.

## Libby, Montana

W. R. Grace ran a vermiculite mine at Libby, Montana, from 1963 to 1990, a little under thirty years. It was a good mine, in the only sense the market measures. Libby vermiculite supplied more than seventy percent of the vermiculite used in the United States, and Grace turned it into Zonolite, a loose-fill attic insulation that went into the homes of tens of millions of Americans. The lifetime revenue ran to something on the order of a hundred million dollars. By every number that showed up on a quarterly statement, the operation worked.

The vermiculite was laced with a particularly lethal form of asbestos. This was not a thing nobody could have known. Internal company documents, surfaced years later in litigation, show that the contamination was identified early and that worker asbestos exposure was measured throughout the years the mine ran. The mining continued. Men carried the dust home on their clothes, and their wives shook it out doing the laundry, and their children breathed it. Children played on the waste piles. The disease does not announce itself for decades, which is part of what makes this kind of severance so efficient: the cost is invisible at the moment the value is collected, and by the time it surfaces the collector is long gone.

Hundreds of Libby residents are dead of asbestos-related disease. Thousands more have been diagnosed with lung abnormalities. The Environmental Protection Agency declared the town a public-health emergency, the first time it had ever made such a declaration for a man-made disaster. The town is small. Almost everyone in it knows someone the mine killed.

I will tell you where the Zonolite went, because the case has a way of staying at arm's length until you do. It went into attics. Millions of them. It is in attics right now, all over the country, sitting quietly above ceilings in houses whose owners have never heard the word Libby and never will. The distance between this case and you may be the distance between your chair and your own ceiling. That is the kind of distance the mechanism manufactures: a cost shipped so far from where it was made that the person living above it cannot see it at all.

Now set the value against the cost. The honest version of this comparison is harder to state than the dramatic one, and the dramatic one is a trap. There is a figure that circulates, a clean and quotable ratio of total downstream damages to revenue, and I am not going to use it, because it does not survive scrutiny: it stitches together Grace's entire national asbestos liability with the Libby-specific harm, and a careful critic would take it apart in an afternoon. So I will build the number the way I built the reef in an earlier chapter, from disclosed parts I can defend. The documented public spending tied specifically to Libby, the EPA Superfund cleanup and the Department of Justice and Montana state environmental restoration figures, runs to roughly nine hundred million dollars to a billion, against a lifetime revenue near a hundred million. That alone is a ratio on the order of nine or ten to one of public cost to private revenue. And it does not yet count the uncompensated mortality and the lifetimes of illness, which, priced separately and conservatively, push the true severance into the low billions. I will call that an order-of-magnitude estimate, built from disclosed components, and leave it there, because a number you can defend to the decimal is worth more than a number that makes a better headline.

Grace declared bankruptcy in 2001. The assets were reorganized. Shareholders were made whole on the terms a reorganization allows, executives moved on, and the corporate person walked away from the liability through a legal door built for exactly that purpose. The town stayed.

Here is the part that is easy to misread. In 2005 the federal government indicted Grace and several senior executives, naming them by role. It was the largest environmental criminal prosecution of its time. After a long trial, in 2009, it ended in acquittal. The acquittal is not evidence that the harm did not happen. The harm is in the lungs and the graves; it is not in dispute. The acquittal is evidence of something else, something structural: that the criminal law is built to prosecute identifiable acts, performed with identifiable knowledge, at identifiable moments, and that a mechanism operating across twenty-seven years through thousands of small accumulated decisions is not the shape that architecture was built to catch.

This is where I want to place a thinker whose work runs underneath this whole chapter. Katharina Pistor, in *The Code of Capital*, argues that capital is not a substance but a legal artifact: ordinary assets become capital when the law's modules, the modules of property and corporate form and bankruptcy, are coded around them to give them durability and protection. On Pistor's reading the bankruptcy discharge and the acquittal at Libby are not failures of the system or aberrations from it. They are the system working, design features of the legal modules that encode capital and protect it from the costs it generates. Pistor diagnoses the modules that encode the severance. The framework this book is building prices what the modules sever. That is the division of labor between us, and I will return to her later, in her own terms.

## Deepwater Horizon

On April 20, 2010, the Deepwater Horizon drilling rig blew out in the Gulf of Mexico. Eleven workers were killed. Roughly 4.9 million barrels of oil escaped into the Gulf over eighty-seven days, through formations BP's own engineers had flagged as extreme-risk geology before the well was drilled. BP had been taking enormous value out of the Gulf for years, and that value was real. It was banked, distributed to shareholders, spent into the wider economy. None of it was fictional. The platform's productivity was a fact.

So was the cost, and the cost severed in every direction a cost can run. It severed across space: the oil was refined and sold in places far from the water, and the poison settled into the marshes and estuaries where local fishing families had worked for generations. It severed across time: the damage persists long after the quarter in which the blowout happened closed its books and the next quarter began. It severed across the living world itself: oil and dispersant move through keystone species and the toxins reach reproduction, suppressing it for decades, and the creatures bearing that cost cannot file a claim, cannot retain counsel, cannot appear before any commission to be counted.

The recovery numbers are again a place where the honest accounting is less tidy than the dramatic one, and again the dramatic one is unsourceable. No defensible source produces a single round figure for total economic losses to the Gulf, and no source supports a single clean percentage of recovery. So build it from parts. The Natural Resource Damage Assessment, the formal federal accounting of injury to natural resources, came to roughly $17.2 billion. Add the tourism losses, which in the low estimate, the fifteen-month scenario from Oxford Economics, run near $7.6 billion. Add the peer-reviewed coastal property-value losses, in the range of $3.8 to $5.0 billion. Set those against BP's penalties and settlements and trust-fund commitments, which fall in a bracket of roughly $60 to $65 billion. Hold that bracket steady, the same figure throughout. What you get is not a single decimal but a range, and the range tells you that a large band of real cost was never recovered, and that the people standing inside that band could not move out of it.

The clearest proof that the price was set by legal architecture rather than by physical harm is what happened at the border. The same slick reached Mexican waters in the Gulf. The settlement BP reached with Mexico came to about $25 million, orders of magnitude smaller, per comparable mile of damaged coastline, than the American settlements. The oil did not know where the border was. The difference in price tracked the difference in legal architecture available to claimants on each side: on the American side, the Oil Pollution Act of 1990, state tort and environmental law, and the machinery of class-action aggregation against a corporation within reach of American courts; on the Mexican side, a far weaker set of tools against a foreign-domiciled company. Same oil, two prices, set by which legal modules the injured party could reach for. Pistor's point made concrete in salt water.

And then the costs that never appeared on any ledger BP had to balance: the fishing knowledge a family had carried for generations and could no longer pass down, the disruption of a culture organized around a working water, the severed relationships between a community and an ecosystem it had read like a face. Those costs were free to lose, in the only sense the accounting recognized, precisely because they had no line on any document. A thing with no line is a thing with no price, and a thing with no price is a thing you can destroy at no charge.

## Exxon Valdez

In March 1989 the tanker *Exxon Valdez* ran aground on Bligh Reef in Prince William Sound, Alaska, and spilled millions of gallons of crude into one of the richest cold-water ecosystems on the continent. I am going to keep this case short, because a later chapter takes it up in full and runs the whole accounting; here I only want to mark its place in the pattern.

The legal aftermath stretched across two decades. A jury returned a large punitive award against Exxon, and on appeal, over the years that followed, the award was cut down sharply, again and again, until what survived was a fraction of what was first assessed. Meanwhile the Pacific herring fishery in the Sound, which had been a living for the people who worked it, collapsed in the years after the spill and did not recover, and there was no architecture through which the herring, or the people who had depended on them, could file for the loss they bore. The herring had no standing. The fishery had no instrument. The award shrank on appeal. The full accounting waits for a later page; the shape is the shape we have already seen twice.

## Baotou

There is a city in Inner Mongolia called Baotou, and near it sits one of the largest rare-earth processing complexes in the world, drawing on the Bayan Obo deposit. Its output is in your life whether you know it or not. Rare earths are in the magnets that run electric vehicles and wind turbines, in the screens and speakers and circuits of the electronics on your desk and in your pocket. They are, in a real sense, the materials the clean-energy transition is being built out of.

Let me state the share correctly, because the loose version overstates it. China accounts for something near sixty percent of the world's rare earths. Baotou, drawing on Bayan Obo, is roughly half of China's own output. So this one complex sits under a very large fraction of a supply chain the modern economy cannot do without, but it is not, by itself, sixty percent of the world. The honest figure is impressive enough without inflation.

The processing is brutal in its byproducts. Separating rare-earth oxides generates enormous volumes of toxic waste: heavy metals, residual acids, and low-level radioactivity from the thorium bound up in the ore, pumped into tailings impoundments. The conservative figure for wastewater intensity, the low end reported by Schreiber and colleagues in 2022 and by the Chinese Society of Rare Earths, is on the order of ten tons of wastewater per ton of rare-earth oxide produced. Residents near the impoundments report elevated rates of cancer, respiratory disease, and birth defects. Farmland within a radius of the operation is no longer usable for farming.

I have to be honest about the data, because the honesty is part of the case. Chinese restrictions limit the health and environmental information that reaches the outside world, and I will not pretend to a precision the record does not support. But the constraint is not silence. Specific peer-reviewed work documents the contamination and its reach: Zhang and colleagues in 2022, Zhou and Ge in 2021, among others. And here I have to retire a number you may have seen, a claim that remediation would cost more than a hundred billion dollars. I cannot source it, and I will not ship a figure I cannot defend. What can be said with sources is plainer and still damning: the annual environmental cost runs into the billions, against an industry generating roughly ten to twelve billion dollars a year, with the share historically spent on remediation a small fraction of either. The full cost, on a horizon that has to include the centuries the radioactive fraction will remain dangerous, is larger than any annual figure can show, and I will label that for what it is, a framework estimate built from disclosed inputs, not a number I found on a ledger.

Baotou is also the book's clearest case of a resource with almost no substitute, and that changes the verdict. Oil can be left in the ground, and the rents from the oil you do extract can be invested forward, which is the bargain Norway struck. Rare earths offer no such bargain. There is no fund that reconstitutes a foreclosed deposit, because there is no engineered stand-in for these materials at the scale the technologies need them. When substitutability runs that low, the framework's conclusion sharpens to a point: a severed cost like this one cannot be made good later by reinvestment somewhere else. It can only be prevented, or paid. Baotou is where the limit of substitution stops being a parameter in an appendix and becomes a field of tailings ponds you could stand at the edge of.

And here is the beat that matters most, the one the whole case is built to deliver. The engineer holding the phone, the driver in the electric car, the household running on wind power: none of them is a villain. The mechanism does not need villains. It never has. The consumer at the end of this supply chain shares in the value the rare earths created and is separated, by ten thousand miles and a dozen intermediaries, from the community bearing the cost. Whether any individual consumer is a good or a bad person is simply beside the structural point. Spatial severance through a global supply chain is exactly the thing that keeps the relationship between the consumer and the cost-bearer invisible. The distance is not an accident of the system. The system manufactures the distance for a living. That is what it is for.

## 2008 and the double risk transfer

The cases so far have been a town, an ecosystem, a sound, a supply chain. Now the mechanism strikes a whole national economy at once, and there is no geographic alibi left, no faraway place to set the cost down where the reader does not live. This is the case that is hardest to look away from, and it is the case I will spend the longest on, because it carries the strongest objections anyone brings to this whole argument, and those objections deserve to be met at full strength.

What happened in 2008 was the second of two risk transfers, and you cannot understand the second without the first.

The first transfer began quietly in 1978, when a provision of the tax code created the structure that became the 401(k), and gathered force over the decades that followed as American employers replaced defined-benefit pensions with individual retirement accounts. This was presented to workers as freedom and as ownership: your money, your account, your choices, portable, yours. The part that was not advertised was whose freedom it actually was. A defined-benefit pension is a promise the institution carries on its own balance sheet, and carrying it means carrying the risk that the market turns, that people live longer than projected, that the fund underperforms. Moving to individual accounts moved all of that risk off the institution and onto the individual, who has no way to pool it, no way to hedge it, no actuary, no diversification across thousands of lives. The freedom that was transferred was chiefly the corporation's freedom from liability. This ran quietly for about thirty years, and most people never noticed the transfer, because nothing had yet come to collect on it.

The second transfer was compressed into a few years, roughly 2001 to 2008, and it ran in the opposite direction. This was the construction, at industrial scale, of mortgage-backed securities and the collateralized debt obligations stacked on top of them. The complexity of these instruments was not incidental; it was structural, in the precise sense that only an instrument too complicated to evaluate could be sold to investors who could not evaluate it. And they were sold, among other places, into the very retirement accounts the first transfer had created. So when they collapsed in 2008, the losses did not land in some abstract financial space. They landed in those accounts.

The quarterly statement that arrived in the spring of 2009 looked exactly like the one that had arrived every quarter for twenty years. Same typeface, same columns, same logo. The only difference was the number, which had climbed steadily across two decades and had now fallen by an amount that erased years of saving. There was no one to call. The freedom that had been sold as ownership turned out, on that statement, to mean that the loss was hers, and hers alone, with no institution standing behind it and no promise left to enforce.

Step back from the individual account to the whole household sector, and the scale comes into view. United States household net worth fell by roughly $11 trillion, by the Federal Reserve's own flow-of-funds accounting, the series the Fed publishes as the Z.1 release. That is the Fed's figure, and I attribute it to the Fed. What the economists Atif Mian and Amir Sufi did, in their book *House of Debt* and the academic work behind it, was reconstruct how that enormous loss was distributed, and the distribution is the heart of the injustice. The loss did not fall evenly. It fell hardest on the households with the least wealth and the most debt, and it fell hardest on them through the plain arithmetic of leverage. An owner with no mortgage who watches a home's price fall loses a slice of equity. A highly leveraged owner watching the same price fall by the same amount can have their entire equity wiped out, because the debt does not shrink when the price does. Mian and Sufi traced this across the wealth distribution and found that households at the bottom cut their spending far more sharply per dollar of lost wealth than households at the top, a difference in the marginal propensity to consume on the order of three to one across the deciles. Their own central magnitude, I should say, is the roughly $5.5 trillion of housing wealth that evaporated; the $11 trillion total is the Fed's number, not theirs, and the two should not be merged. What Mian and Sufi own is the mechanism of the distribution, and it is theirs decisively.

Now set the rescue beside the loss, because the asymmetry is the whole point. For the institutions, the response was instant and vast. The Troubled Asset Relief Program authorized $700 billion. The Federal Reserve's cumulative emergency lending, by the Government Accountability Office's 2011 audit, exceeded $16 trillion, and that audit figure is the one well-sourced number in this whole story, so it is the one I lean on. Executive bonuses resumed within about eighteen months. For the households, there was nothing comparable. Foreclosure filings ran to roughly ten million between 2008 and 2012, of which four to five million were completed, about 4.1 million through 2012 by CoreLogic's count. The system mobilized trillions in a matter of days for the institutions and organized nothing of remotely similar scale for the families losing their homes. In 2011 the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission concluded that the crisis was the result of human action and inaction, of choices that were made, and not of forces beyond anyone's control. It did not have to happen. It was built.

I said the strongest objections live here, and I mean to meet them, one at a time, without using the same move four times in a row.

The first objection is about agency. Many of the mortgages that defaulted went to borrowers who could not have serviced them under any reasonable model, and those borrowers signed. Nobody forced the pen into anyone's hand. This is true, and it deserves a real answer rather than a dismissal. The answer is that agency requires information, and the information architecture surrounding these loans was not neutral; it was structurally adversarial. Origination commissions were tied to the volume of loans written, not to whether the loans were ever repaid, which paid people to write loans that would fail. Disclosed terms diverged from contract terms. Adjustable-rate products were marketed to borrowers whose understanding of how a rate reset works was expected, by the people selling them, to be incomplete. The post-crisis enforcement record of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau documents this pattern in case after case. The agency was real. The information asymmetry was the lever that turned the real agency toward the cliff.

The second objection is that the crisis was global, not a story you can pin on American policy. European banks held vast exposures, Asian export economies caught the contagion, mortgage-backed securities sat on balance sheets across the world, and the Basel capital framework shaped bank behavior internationally. All true. But the global character of the collapse amplifies the finding here rather than dissolving it, because the instruments that propagated the disaster were predominantly American in origin. The mortgage-backed securities and the collateralized debt obligations were largely originated in the United States. The originator-fee architecture that paid for volume over quality, the issuer-pays model that let the firms being rated choose and pay their own rating agencies, the regulatory exemptions that let the whole structure scale, those were American policy choices. The world caught the fire. The fire was lit here.

The third objection is the one that sounds most decisive: TARP was repaid. Treasury's accounting shows that most of the money came back, and on some accountings the program turned a modest profit. This is correct, and it is also the wrong accounting unit, which is the thing to see clearly. TARP measures whether the federal government got its money back from the institutions it lent to. It does not measure, and was never built to measure, the cost this chapter is pricing: the welfare lost by the foreclosed households, the workers whose retirement balances reset to a lower base from which all future compounding would have to start again, the regional economies that contracted and did not fully recover, and the public trust that was spent down when the country watched trillions appear in days for the banks and nothing of the kind appear for the families. None of that is recovered by TARP repayment. None of it shows up on the federal balance sheet the repayment argument cites. The bill was paid back on one ledger and left entirely unpaid on the other, and the other is the one with people on it.

The fourth objection is that Dodd-Frank fixed all this. The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the Financial Stability Oversight Council, restricted proprietary trading through the Volcker Rule, and imposed heightened capital and liquidity requirements. These are real changes, and I will not pretend otherwise; in fact, I will say something stronger and more uncomfortable for my own argument. Dodd-Frank is a partial, early installment of precisely the accountability architecture this book is calling for. That is a compliment, not a dismissal. But a reform that is partial and reversible has not closed the gap. The Volcker Rule was later relaxed. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau was later weakened, institutionally. The designations that mark a firm as systemically important have been contested and rolled back. A reform that can be unwound has not consolidated to the point where its persistence is structurally guaranteed, and an accountability architecture that survives only as long as the political winds allow is not yet an accountability architecture. A later chapter names Dodd-Frank among the entering wedges, the partial beginnings the framework would build out and lock in; here it is enough to say the wedge was entered and the door is not yet open.

It is worth saying, before leaving 2008, that the asymmetric rescue was a choice and not a necessity, because there is a society that faced a structurally similar crisis and chose differently. Sweden in the 1930s confronted banking-crisis pressure analogous to what the United States faced in 1929 to 1933 and again in 2008 to 2009, and it paired the rescue of its banks with substantial relief for its households. The krona was taken off the gold standard in 1931, which restored export competitiveness and eased the real burden of household debt. Bank rescue was coordinated with mortgage modification rather than set against it. Public-works employment programs in the mid-1930s restored household income directly. And the Saltsjöbaden Agreement of 1938 institutionalized the architecture of the welfare state that grew out of that period. This comparison is drawn from the comparative-banking-crisis literature, from Kindleberger and Eichengreen among others, and it stands here as a plain demonstration that rescuing the banks and relieving the households are not an either-or. A society can choose both. The United States chose otherwise in 2008 and 2009, and chose otherwise again in 2020. The choice was contingent. A later chapter develops the cross-country comparison; the point I need here is only that the road not taken was a real road.

Three settings remain in this part of the book, and the same mechanism runs through all of them. In the next, the distance the cost travels is not space but time itself. In the one after, the boundary it crosses is the edge of a single human body.

## Social Security

The most devastating example I know hides inside a system Americans treat as the very opposite of extraction: the promise that each generation will care for the one before it. I want to be precise about what I am criticizing here, because it would be easy to hear this as an attack on the program, and it is not. Social Security is genuine. It is essential. It is not a fraud and not a Ponzi scheme, and the people who call it those things are wrong. My critique is narrow and specific. It is a critique of one architectural choice in how the program is funded, and of nothing else.

The mechanics first. Social Security was designed in 1935 as a pay-as-you-go system: the payroll taxes of current workers fund the benefits of current retirees, directly, in the same year. For decades the system ran a surplus, taking in more than it paid out, and the surplus was held in special-issue Treasury obligations in a trust fund. The retirement of the baby-boom generation was foreseeable forty years in advance; the demographics were not a surprise to anyone who looked. By the early 2020s, outflows had come to exceed payroll-tax inflows. Under current law and current projection, the trust fund depletes around 2033, after which incoming payroll taxes would still cover roughly seventy-seven percent of promised benefits. The system does not vanish at depletion. It falls short.

Now the figure, which I have to handle carefully, because there is a version of it in circulation that is simply dishonest, and the dishonesty matters more than the number. The dishonest version says that Social Security represents something like $108 trillion in foregone compounding, the wealth that would have accumulated if all those payroll contributions had been invested in markets instead of paid out, and it sometimes gets called the largest cost severance in human history. I want to dismantle that figure rather than use it, because it cannot be reconstructed from its own inputs. It runs roughly two orders of magnitude above the nearest serious counterfactual. It exceeds the trust fund's peak, the cumulative income of the program, and the ceiling you would get from the most generous equity-versus-bond differential anyone could defend. It is not a real cost-severance magnitude, and I will not attach the words largest in history to it.

Here is the honest accounting. Over the life of the program, cumulative contributions come to roughly $26 trillion, and cumulative benefits paid come to roughly the same, around $26 trillion. The two nearly cancel, which is what pay-as-you-go means: the money came in and went back out, year after year, to people who needed it. The only money ever actually available to invest was the running surplus, the accumulated reserve, which peaked around $2.7 trillion. Take that investable surplus, compound it at a realistic real rate of return net of the payouts the system still had to make, and the foregone-return counterfactual comes to a few trillion dollars. Not a hundred. The severed cost here runs into the trillions, and that is a real and serious number, but it is single-digit trillions arrived at honestly, not a hundred trillion arrived at by pretending the whole river of contributions sat in an index fund instead of paying grandmothers' rent. (The nearest external estimate, from work at Brookings and the Center for Retirement Research, lands around $1 trillion on the assumption of a 40-percent equity allocation from 1984 forward, and that is available as a single sourced figure for anyone who wants one.) And I keep this entirely distinct from another large number that gets blurred into it: the Trustees' seventy-five-year actuarial shortfall, which is about $22.6 trillion. That is the actuarial shortfall, a projection of the funding gap over a defined horizon. It is a different quantity, measuring a different thing, and merging the two into one impressive total is exactly the kind of accounting this book exists to refuse.

But I have buried the most important point under the arithmetic, and the point is not in the arithmetic at all. The cost severance in Social Security is not in how the surplus was invested. Holding the reserves in Treasury obligations was administratively prudent; a sober fund manager would have done the same. The severance is in how the obligations were generated.

Think about who enacted the benefit expansions of the 1960s, 1970s, and 1980s, and when their consequences would mature. Those expansions were voted in by members of Congress who, in nearly every case, would have left office long before the demographic transition they were funding against arrived. They were paid for, in the moment, by the favorable ratio of working-age people to retirees that those very decades enjoyed. And every actuarial report of the period openly projected the deficit window the expansions would eventually hit. The information was on the table. The incentive was to expand benefits during the demographic surplus, collecting the electoral value of the promise immediately, while assigning the funding cost to a deficit window the actuaries had clearly marked and that the people making the promise would never have to face. Time is the distance here. The electoral value was extracted at one moment; the cost was assigned to future taxpayers who did not yet exist, who could not be consulted, and who cannot now refuse.

A different country made a different architectural choice at a comparable moment, and an earlier chapter told that story, so I will only point at it. Norway channels its resource rents into a sovereign fund that compounds capital for future generations rather than spending the future in the present. Same structural problem, different architecture, different outcome. And the difference is not a function of national wealth or favorable demographics; it is a function of an instrument chosen at a specific moment. The 1935 decision to fund American old-age security pay-as-you-go was a choice, not a law of nature, and Norway is the proof that the other choice was available.

Two objections deserve answers, and I will concede each one most of the way before saying where it stops.

The first is the prudence objection: holding the reserves in Treasuries is exactly what a sound fund manager does, and putting them in equities would have exposed retirees to market crashes and to the political-economy risks of a government holding stock. I concede this completely. At the level of portfolio management, the choice was prudent. But my claim does not operate at the level of portfolio management. It operates upstream of it, at the generation of the obligations, and a defense pitched at the portfolio level simply does not reach a claim pitched at the obligation level. The two arguments pass each other in the dark, addressing different things.

The second is the tractability objection: the gap is solvable, through phased adjustments the Trustees and various commissions have laid out, by raising the payroll rate, lifting the cap on taxable wages, refining the benefit formula at the high end, or adjusting the eligibility age to track longevity. I concede the arithmetic entirely. The gap can be closed. But notice what every one of those closures does. Each of them transfers the obligation onto the cohort living through the demographic-deficit window, who will pay more or receive less, and not onto the cohorts who extracted the electoral value of generating the obligation in the first place, because those cohorts are gone. Tractability is not the absence of severance. It is severance unfolding on schedule, exactly as designed, paid by the people the design always meant to make pay.

## The healthcare commons

The mechanism has crossed a town, an ecosystem, a supply chain, an economy, and a span of generations. Now it crosses the smallest boundary it will cross in this chapter, the edge of a single human body, at the most vulnerable stretches of a life, where the severed cost is no abstraction at all but the specific financial ruin of a specific household.

Consider what American medical crowdfunding has become. Across the 2010s, raising money online for medical bills went from a curiosity to a fixture. By 2020, more than a third of the campaigns on the largest crowdfunding platform were medical. Across roughly 2016 to 2020, on the order of 438,000 medical campaigns raised more than $2 billion across about 21.7 million individual donations. And here is the part that tells you what kind of safety net this is: about twelve percent of those campaigns met their goals, and about sixteen percent received nothing at all. The platform's own chief executive has acknowledged, in public, that it was never designed to be healthcare financing and that it became the default backstop anyway, which is to say the country backed into a medical safety net made of strangers' generosity without ever deciding to.

I want to be careful with one claim that often rides along with this case. It is sometimes said that the United States is the only wealthy nation where this happens, and that is too strong; medical crowdfunding exists in Canada, in the United Kingdom, in Germany. What holds, and what the comparison in Saleh and colleagues' 2020 work in *JAMA Network Open* supports, is that it operates at a scale and in a role in the United States that it does not occupy elsewhere: not as an occasional supplement but as a default. The campaign-scale figures come from Igra and Kenworthy's 2022 work in the *American Journal of Public Health*. The point survives the softening: a country has made a website its safety net, and the website fails most of the people who come to it.

The failure is not random, which is the next thing the research shows. Medical campaigns mounted by people with distinctively Black or Hispanic surnames raise less money and are less likely to meet their goals, controlling for geography and for the period in which they ran. Sit with what that means. The informal backstop reproduces the very inequities the formal safety net was supposed to reduce. A system of mutual charity keyed to the size and wealth of your personal network routes the most help to the people who need it least, because the people with the largest and wealthiest networks are, on average, the people who were already best protected. The backstop is regressive by construction.

Underneath all of this runs a structural curve that has been bending for fifty years. The economists Lawrence Mishel and Josh Bivens, at the Economic Policy Institute, have documented the decoupling of median wages from productivity that began in the early 1970s: the economy kept getting more productive, and the typical worker's pay stopped tracking that productivity. Over the same span, healthcare costs grew at multiples of general inflation. The household pressure that drives a family to a fundraising page is the widening gap between those two curves, the flat one and the climbing one. Jacob Hacker named the broad pattern in *The Great Risk Shift*: the steady transfer, across decades, of economic risk from institutions onto individuals and families. The pension that became a 401(k) and the medical bill that became a fundraising page are the same shift wearing different clothes.

And then there is the dying body, where the architecture shows itself most starkly. The journalist Katy Butler documented her own family's encounter with it, and I will stay strictly inside her published account. A long office visit spent discussing whether a treatment is wise, weighing alternatives, talking through what a frail patient actually wants, is reimbursed at a small fraction of what a procedure such as a pacemaker implantation pays, and a large share of the procedure's value flows to the device's manufacturer. The liability architecture points the same direction the money does: performing the procedure protects the clinician against a malpractice claim, while declining it creates exposure. So the incentives, financial and legal alike, all lean toward intervention. In the case Butler recorded, the years of cognitive decline that followed were sustained by the device, and the unpaid caregiving labor of an elderly spouse, which appears on no ledger anywhere, carried the cost the system had generated and declined to count.

Atul Gawande, in *Being Mortal*, names the systemic shape of this. The reimbursement system rewards intervention at every decision point while underfunding the infrastructure and the training of palliative care, and the aggregate of all those individually rational responses is the over-treatment of the dying, the individual case reproduced at the scale of a whole system. No single clinician has to choose extraction over compassion for this to happen. The architecture produces the pattern by collecting rational responses to incentives that are themselves a policy-design choice. The same shape extends into maternal care, into mental-health care, into the long management of chronic disease. The body is the commons here, and the costs of its mismanagement are severed onto the households and the unpaid caregivers who were standing closest when the bill came down.

## The pattern made visible

Stand back now and look at the seven cases together, and the single shape under them comes clear.

In every case an entity, whether a company or a generation or a political system, takes value out of a resource or a system that could have been managed for the long term. The taking generates costs, which are environmental in Libby and the Gulf and Baotou, financial in 2008, intergenerational in Social Security, medical and personal in the healthcare cases. And in every case those costs are severed off, lifted away from the party that collected the value and set down on parties with little or no power to stop the transfer: the workers, the communities, the ecosystems, the generations not yet born.

I have been leaning on a single word for this, severance, and the word has a second life I should account for, because a reader who hears that second meaning first might think I am smuggling something in. Severance is also what a company calls the check it writes when it lets a worker go. Someone might worry that by using the word for cost I am quietly importing the very commodifying-of-labor frame this book resists. It is the opposite. Both senses of the word share a root for a reason, because both name a cut: in the one case the worker cut loose from the wage, in the other the cost cut loose from the party that caused it. The book keeps the word because the cut is the thing. Softening it would do in language exactly what the bad accounting does in fact, which is make the cutting disappear.

Look across the cases and you can name the covers under which the party that collected the value escapes the full reckoning. There is distance, the supply chain so long the consumer cannot see the cost-bearer. There is time, the decades between the promise and the day it comes due. There is the asymmetry of what is known, the company that measured the asbestos exposure the workers could not. There is the asymmetry of who can refuse, the future taxpayer who was never asked. There is the normalization that lets a cost read as a routine cost of doing business. And there is the appearance of abundance that keeps the cost from registering at all, the seafood still on the menu, the attic insulation working as advertised. A later chapter names these covers as a set and gives them their proper labels; here I only want you to see them operating, not catalogued.

Walk the cases one more time as a roll-call of who absorbed the cost. The town that could not move. The fishing communities that could not relocate when the water turned. The rural community downwind of the tailings with no exit and no political voice. The foreclosed households. The future Americans who cannot opt out of the inherited obligation. The families whose fundraising campaigns fail and the elderly spouse whose caregiving hours appear on no ledger. In every case the party that collected the value is gone by the time the bill arrives. The company has declared bankruptcy. The executives have moved on. The promise-making politicians have left office. The promise-making generations have died. And the cost remains, transferred to whoever could not get out of its way.

I want to state the structural claim plainly, and without a villain, because the absence of the villain is the most important thing about it. This is not a failure of the system. It is the system working as it was built to work, not through anyone's conscious intention to do harm but through the accumulated logic of structures that reward the extraction of value and impose no charge for the severing of cost onto those least able to resist it. I will go further, and make a claim I owe you the grounding for: at bottom, every extractive economy, every place where the taking has outrun the restoring, has operated by this same mechanism. That is a large claim, and I am not going to leave it floating in the air. I am going to set it down here and discharge it in the pages just ahead, through a lineage of thinkers who diagnosed exactly this asymmetry long before this book named it, and through a second lineage that has spent a century refusing to let the costs of enterprise be quietly socialized. Hold the claim, and let me earn it.

The question the cases leave us with is not whether this is happening. The cases settle that. The question is what it would look like to build accountability that interrupts the pattern before the cost is severed, rather than chasing the cost afterward, once the party that owes it has already left the room.

## The same object, named honestly

I have to slow down here, because the move I am about to make is the one the whole book stands or falls on, and it is also the move that opens the book to its most serious objection. The objection is that by putting a price on a commons, I am doing the very thing I claim to oppose. I am commodifying. So before I name the lineage I am standing in, I have to face that objection squarely, and I have to do it inside the same passage, because there is no elsewhere to defer it to.

Start with what a commons is, structurally. I do not define a commons by whether it is ecological. I define it by its structure: a commons is anything that is depended on and shared but is not produced as a commodity, anything we all draw on that no one manufactured for sale. On that definition a coral reef and the atmosphere and a human community and a body of labor and a future generation are, structurally, the same kind of object: a commons whose abundance hid its cost until the cost arrived. And if they are the same kind of object, then the same accounting apparatus can price all of them, running forward to price the foreclosure of ecological and atmospheric futures and running backward to price the human extraction already realized. The reef and the community are not analogies for each other. They are instances of one thing.

Now Michael Sandel's objection, stated at full strength, because a weak version would be a cheat. To put a price on a sacred landscape, on a living community, on the unborn, Sandel argues in *What Money Can't Buy*, is to launder the priceless into a transaction, and in doing so to concede the extractor's own frame: that everything has a price, that nothing is beyond the reach of the market. Worse, monetizing a harm can legitimate it, turning a wrong into a fee, a thing you are permitted to do as long as you pay for it. That is a real danger, and I do not wave it away.

The answer is in the difference between the two things a price can be. A cost set at zero is not a sacred value left reverently unpriced. Zero is a number. It is the number the extractive accounting already wrote down, the number that made the extraction look profitable in the first place. When the asbestos in the insulation, the oil in the marsh, the foreclosed retirement, the dying-body cost borne by an unpaid spouse all carry a price of zero, that zero is not respect. It is the accounting that severs the cost. The framework refuses that zero. It does not price the commons in order to sell it. It prices the commons in order to reserve against the harm and to re-embed the cost in the value that generated it. Pricing-to-reserve is the opposite of pricing-to-sell, and the whole argument turns on holding those two apart.

This is where I name the tradition I am working inside, because I am not the first to think this, and the honest thing is to say whose descendant the idea is. Karl Polanyi, in *The Great Transformation*, written in 1944, argued that the market economy rests on a fiction: it treats labor, land, and money as commodities, as things produced for sale, when none of them is. He called them fictitious commodities. Labor is just human life; land is just nature; money is just a token of trust. To run them through the market as if they were ordinary goods is to disembed them from the society and the nature they actually belong to, and that disembedding does damage. The structural definition of a commons I just gave you is the operational descendant of Polanyi's fictitious-commodity thesis. The diagnostic move at the center of this book, imagine the commons absent and ask what cost appears, is a way of operationalizing Polanyi's intuition. And Polanyi's double movement, the way a society pushes back to re-embed what the market tore loose, is exactly what the bond apparatus is for: it is an accounting instrument for re-embedding. The framework is Polanyi's double movement made into a tool. It prices and reserves against what Polanyi could only diagnose.

Nancy Fraser is the closest living anchor for the same-object claim, and naming her here pays off a debt this book has been carrying since an earlier chapter, where I planted the idea of expropriation without yet giving it her name. Fraser draws a distinction between exploitation and expropriation. Exploitation takes through the wage relation: the worker is paid, and the surplus is taken on top of the wage, through the contract. Expropriation takes without even the form of a contract, helping itself to the unpaid social-reproductive labor and to the nature that sit, in her phrase, behind the wage relation, holding it up while going uncompensated. The same-object claim this chapter makes is Fraser's terrain. Reef-extraction priced backward and community-extraction priced backward are instances of the expropriation she theorizes, the taking that does not even bother with a contract. The work is in her essay "Behind Marx's Hidden Abode" and in *Cannibal Capitalism*, and the seed planted earlier in this book comes due, now, with her name on it.

Here I have to be exact about the relationship, because it is easy to overclaim and the overclaim would be a betrayal. Polanyi and Fraser, and the tradition they stand in, resist monetization on principle. They are suspicious of the asset frame; they refuse to let everything be reduced to a price; that refusal is central to what they were doing. So I am not going to claim that either of them would endorse pricing the commons. That is not the claim. The claim is narrower and, I think, sturdier: this framework operationalizes their diagnosis in the cost-reckoning direction, turning their analysis of how the market disembeds and extracts into an instrument that forces an honest reckoning of what was taken, and it does so against the commodification they opposed, not in service of it. A framework that stands inside the tradition that refused the asset frame is using their refusal, not betraying it. The refusal of the asset frame is exactly what the framework is built to honor.

That distinction also answers a second worry, which is sharper than Sandel's and harder to dismiss: the worry that pricing the commons is financialization by another route, the commons repackaged as a new asset class for the market to trade. The first part of the answer is the lineage I just named. To claim Polanyi and Fraser, the thinkers who diagnosed commodification as a harm, is to mark the line between pricing-to-force-honest-accounting and financializing-the-commons-into-an-asset. Accounting a cost is not coding an asset.

But I would rather show you that than assert it, and I can show it in the financializer's own terms. Return to Pistor, whom I met at Libby. Pistor identifies four legal attributes that turn an ordinary asset into capital, four bits of code the law wraps around a thing to make it a coded asset: priority, which ranks your claim ahead of others; durability, which makes the claim last; universality, which makes it good against the world; and convertibility, which lets you turn it into cash or into another tradable claim on demand. Now look at the accountability bond against that list. The bond is deliberately engineered to lack the two attributes that let capital circulate. It is not convertible: it can be redeemed only for the discharge of the specific liability it records, not for money and not for any tradable claim. And it is not transferable: it cannot be sold, traded, or repackaged into a security. A debt that can only ever be paid, and never traded, is the structural opposite of a coded asset. That is the difference between coding an asset and accounting a cost, shown in the law's own categories rather than asserted against them.

The most forceful living version of the financialization worry belongs to Brett Christophers, whose work on the asset economy presses exactly this concern, and a later chapter takes up his argument at length, where it can be met in detail. Here I will leave the answer in principle: accounting a cost is the opposite of coding an asset, and the bond's missing attributes are the proof.

This is why this passage carries more weight than any other in the chapter. From a single position, the position Polanyi and Fraser built, it anchors the claim that the reef and the atmosphere and the community and the labor and the future generation are one object, and it disarms, all at once, the cluster of objections that the framework's own pricing is the commodification it set out to critique. The commodification worry, the financialization worry, the worry about the severance word itself, all of them are answered from the same ground. That triple duty is why this passage had to exist.

## Who shaped the system

I have been documenting an asymmetry through this entire chapter, a powerful party that captures value and an absent, diffuse, or dominated party that absorbs the cost, and I have not yet credited the tradition that diagnosed that asymmetry long before I came to it. I should, because the framework does not stand alone; it continues work that others began.

K. William Kapp, in *The Social Costs of Private Enterprise*, published in 1950, did something the economics of his day mostly would not: he sat down and systematically accounted for the costs that private enterprise generates and then offloads onto society, the social costs that never appear in the firm's own books. Kapp wanted those costs reckoned. That is exactly the project this book continues, by pricing those social costs per extraction event rather than leaving them, as Kapp found them, diffused and unbilled. He is the closer ally of the two thinkers I want to name, because he and I want the same thing: the cost on the books.

Joan Martínez-Alier, working in political ecology, mapped what he called ecological-distribution conflicts, tracing across the world who bears the ecological cost of extraction and who captures its value. That mapping is the diagnosis at the heart of this chapter, drawn in detail across cases far beyond the seven I have used. And here I have to make the same careful move I made with Polanyi and Fraser, because Martínez-Alier earns the same care. He rejects monetary valuation on principle. His objection is incommensurability, the position, which he traces back to Otto Neurath, that some values cannot be placed on a common monetary scale without losing precisely what matters about them. So I credit his diagnosis of the asymmetry, which is powerful and which this chapter relies on, and I honor his refusal of the pricing, and I do not claim that he would endorse what the framework does with the diagnosis. The tradition named the asymmetry. The framework operationalizes the diagnosis by pricing it, and it does so knowing that some in the tradition would decline the pricing, and respecting the decline.

That credit settles part of the debt I incurred a few pages back, when I claimed that every extractive economy has operated by this mechanism. Kapp and Martínez-Alier are the diagnostic spine of that claim, alongside Polanyi and Fraser.

Now to a different and complementary question, because the framework's apparatus answers one question and not another. The apparatus accounts for who bore the cost. It builds the ledger. But there is a distinct question it does not answer on its own: who shaped the architecture that distributed the cost the way it did. For that question there is a rigorous and well-developed toolkit, and it comes from a tradition some readers of this book will not expect me to reach for. The Public Choice tradition, the work of James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock and those who followed them, Tullock's 1967 analysis of the waste created by rent-seeking and Mancur Olson's 1965 account of how concentrated interests out-organize diffuse ones, supplies exactly the vocabulary for asking who built the architecture and to whose benefit.

Where the party capturing the value and the party bearing the cost are clearly distinct populations, that chain runs cleanly, and you can follow it. You can identify the concentrated-interest actors who shaped the architecture, the lobbying spending and the coalitions that produced the design, whose interests it was built to serve and whose it was built to cost. The cases in this chapter come with rosters. At Libby, Grace's own released internal documents show the lobbying and the sought-after delay in asbestos regulation. At Deepwater, the Minerals Management Service was a captured regulator, taking industry funding for personnel travel and gifts through a revolving door so well documented that the post-disaster reorganization split the agency apart, and the Interior Department's inspector general had reported the capture before the blowout, not after. In 2008, the architecture was shaped by the repeal of Glass-Steagall through the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999, by the Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000 that left derivatives unregulated, and by Office of the Comptroller of the Currency preemption rulings, all of it shaped by financial-industry lobbying that the Center for Responsive Politics and congressional hearings documented in detail. In healthcare, pharmaceutical lobbying has led United States industry spending for years, and the long prohibition on Medicare negotiating drug prices, since modified, was a textbook rent-seeking outcome. And the coalfields of West Virginia, which a later chapter takes up in full, carry a multi-generational history of coal's dominance over the state's legislative and regulatory architecture.

There are cases where the line between the value-capturer and the cost-bearer blurs, and there the rent-seeking analysis gets harder to run. Baotou sits inside a demand-shaped global supply chain in which the consumer is implicated, not innocent. Social Security has an intertemporal structure in which today's beneficiaries are yesterday's payers, so the populations overlap across time. In those cases the rent-seeking chain does not run as cleanly, and the framework's structural accounting carries more of the weight.

I have to be exact about how I am using Public Choice, because the tradition comes with politics attached and I am not adopting the politics. I am drawing on the rent-seeking vocabulary, not endorsing the broader Buchanan project. The rent-seeking tools are in the toolkit, not in the opposing camp. The framework's accounting of who bore the cost supplies the ledger to which Public Choice's analysis of who shaped the architecture naturally applies, and the two fit together. A reader who wants the architecture-shaping analysis in full should read Buchanan and Tullock and their successors in parallel with this book, and the rent-seeking analytical vocabulary survives many readings of Buchanan's larger project, including readings that reject most of it. I am saying this in part to readers at AEI and Cato and Mercatus and George Mason, who know this tradition far better than most and who may be surprised to find it welcomed here rather than fought. It is welcomed. A later chapter develops the complement at greater length.

## Restitution and Foreclosure

The cases have produced a question, and it is time to name the answer, though only at the level of structure, because a later chapter formalizes the machinery and another handles the politics of building it. The question is simple to state: what would an honest accounting of all these severances actually require?

It would require an architecture with two faces, because the accountability gap has two temporal faces. There is harm already done, the realized portfolio of extraction: the lives already lost in Libby, the communities already hollowed, the ecosystems already poisoned. And there is harm not yet done, the future costs that current extraction is foreclosing right now: the foreclosed atmosphere, the foreclosed deposit, the options being closed off for people who cannot yet object because they do not yet exist. An architecture that addresses one face and not the other addresses half the problem.

So there are two instruments. The Restitution Bond discharges harm already realized; it is the backward-looking instrument, the one that answers to people who were actually affected. The Foreclosure Bond discharges the future-generation costs that present extraction is foreclosing; it is the forward-looking instrument, the one that answers to outcomes rather than to specific identified persons. Together they make up the aggregate accountability bond the framework's central relation requires, where the cost severed equals the residual value of the commons minus what the bond posts against it.

Why call it a bond, and not a tax or a fee or a fund? Because each of those alternatives fails to do the specific work. A tax is paid at the moment of a transaction into general revenue, and it carries no architecture matching a posted amount to a particular future obligation; the money goes into the pool and the obligation is never tracked against it. A fee licenses an activity; it grants permission to do the thing, rather than discharging the harm the thing does, and licensing is exactly the legitimation Sandel warned about. A fund aggregates money without discharging anything against a specific liability. A bond does the thing none of those do: it is posted before the full cost is borne, it carries forward across the long tail of the cost, and it discharges observably, by a specific figure, against a specific liability. The structure is not new. Reclamation bonds exist under the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977. Decommissioning bonds are posted against the closure of oil and gas wells. Norway's sovereign fund is the same logic at the scale of a nation. The reparations economics treats a documented historical debt as an obligation to be discharged, which is bond-like in its structure. And the civic-republican commitment not to dominate the future is the kind of standing obligation for which a posted bond is the natural accounting form.

The naming deserves a word, because both names are deliberate and both carry an echo. Take Restitution first. The instrument is called Restitution, and the tradition that informs it most deeply is called Reparations, and the difference is intentional. Restitution, restitutio in integrum, is the older and broader legal and philosophical term for the obligation to restore what was taken, and the framework needs an umbrella wide enough to cover the full field of cost-severance contexts: environmental, occupational-disease, community, ecosystem. The contemporary reparations tradition, the work of Ta-Nehisi Coates and of William Darity and Kirsten Mullen and Darrick Hamilton and Dalton Conley, is the primary source the framework draws on; its methodology is how the framework knows what it knows about looking backward and pricing a debt. But to call the instrument a Reparations Bond would narrow the umbrella to one historically specific application and make the portability of the method read as a loose analogy rather than the direct application it is. The narrower alternatives fail for their own reasons. Restoration presupposes a physical restorability that many harms do not allow. Indemnification routes everything through an insurance register the framework does not use. Damages collapses the bond into the measurement instrument and loses the distinction between the two. So the framework joins the reparations tradition rather than translating it or rebranding it, and it positions the Restitution Bond as operationalizing the material-restitution component of what Darity and Mullen call redress, inside their own three-part account of acknowledgment and atonement, redress, and closure, using their language inside their typology rather than against it.

Foreclosure is the other name, and the reader hears the 2008 housing event in it first, which is not a coincidence I want to hide; it is a continuity I want to claim. Foreclosure, from forclore, to bar from, names two things at once. It names the mortgage event, in which a household is barred from a primary asset by a legal architecture that lets the bar happen at scale. And it names the framework's forward instrument, in which future generations are barred from the options a commons holds, a habitable atmosphere, an intact ecosystem, an irreplaceable material reserve, by a legal and economic architecture that lets the bar happen across time. Same mechanism, different substrate, the household barred from the house and the future barred from the world. The alternatives miss the feature that matters. Future-Generations reads soft and philanthropic. Sustainability has been co-opted into corporate ESG language. Reclamation is a narrower regulatory term. Conservation and Intergenerational both miss the bar-from at the center of the thing. Foreclosure keeps it.

Now the lineage that grounds the forward instrument, woven through the cases rather than listed off in a column, because a roll-call of names buries the reader and I would rather place each thinker where the cases earn the placement.

The forward instrument needs a moral grounding, an answer to why anyone owes anything to people who do not yet exist, and the deepest version of that grounding is Derek Parfit's. In *Reasons and Persons*, published in 1984, Parfit worked at the level of outcomes rather than specific persons, and his account grounds an obligation to the future that does not depend on knowing exactly which future people will exist. I should be honest that Parfit also left open the non-identity problem, the puzzle that our present choices change which people will exist, so that those future people cannot straightforwardly be said to be harmed by the choices that brought them into being. The framework does not claim to have solved that problem. It names it as a standing limit, not a defeated one, and operates at the level of outcomes where Parfit's grounding still holds.

Parfit carries the primary forward load, and he carries it together with an ethics of discounting that a later chapter takes up in full, the work of John Broome, who argues that the discount rate we apply to future harms is a moral choice and not a technical one, that pure time preference, the bare preference for now over later simply because it is now, has no ethical defense, and that a generation imposing climate harm on its successors owes them restitution for it. That discounting ethics, developed at length later, co-grounds the forward arm alongside Parfit.

The civic-republican tradition belongs here too, but in a particular and subordinate place, and I want to be careful about where I put it. Philip Pettit, in *Republicanism*, and Quentin Skinner, in *Liberty Before Liberalism*, develop freedom as non-domination, the idea that you are unfree not only when someone interferes with you but when you live subject to another's arbitrary power even if that power is never exercised. Non-domination does its strongest work on the within-generation harms this chapter has documented, the worker who cannot refuse, the community living under a power it cannot check. Extended forward, it gives the Foreclosure Bond a reading as the accounting form of a commitment not to dominate the future. But I offer that forward reading as a complement, not as the load-bearing frame. The forward arm leans primarily on Parfit's grounding and on Broome's discounting ethics; the republican reading enriches it rather than carrying it.

The environmental-bond economics of the forward instrument has its own lineage, and it is separate from the reparations lineage by design, because the two faces of the bond must not be confused. John Hartwick's rule, from 1977, holds that a nation depleting a finite resource can sustain its consumption across generations if it reinvests the resource rents into reproducible capital, and Norway's fund is the cleanest real-world application of it. That tradition, the reclamation bond and the sovereign fund and Hartwick's rule, grounds the Foreclosure Bond as a foreclosure-prevention instrument, not a reparations instrument. The forward face prevents; the backward face repairs; they are not the same instrument and I keep them apart.

The backward instrument has its own grounding, and it runs through the reparations tradition I have already begun to name. Ta-Nehisi Coates, in *The Case for Reparations*, gave the argument its rhetorical architecture: name the mechanism, name the harm, name the debt. The framework's backward instrument is a structural extension of that architecture into extraction-community contexts. And the economic substance comes from Darity and Mullen, whose *From Here to Equality*, published in 2020, is the contemporary landmark and the framework's primary informing source, together with the matched-comparison method that Hamilton, Darity, Price, Sridharan, and Tippett laid out in *Umbrellas Don't Make It Rain* in 2015, with its striking finding that Black households in the highest achievement stratum hold less wealth than white households in lower strata, which tells you that the structural variable is wealth and not behavior, and with Dalton Conley's earlier *Being Black, Living in the Red*, from 1999, establishing wealth as the structural variable in the first place.

I want to be exact about the relationship here, because it is easy to get it backward and the backward version would be a discourtesy. Darity and Mullen already priced. They computed reparations magnitudes; they did the quantitative work. The framework does not supply a magnitude they only diagnosed and left blank. It ports their matched-comparison pricing method to other extraction-community contexts and specializes it. McDowell County's wealth collapse can be priced against comparable non-extraction Appalachian counties. Libby's cumulative health-cost burden can be priced against comparable communities without a Grace mine. The opioid mortality demography of Appalachia can be priced against comparable counties that the documented marketing maps of a company like Purdue did not concentrate on, a third leg of the matched comparison that a later chapter takes up in full. This is direct application of their discipline, joining the tradition, not adding a number to a diagnosis. The methodology travels because it was built to travel, and the framework is one of the places it travels to.

There is one contemporary field that, like this framework, already runs its accounting in both directions at once, and intellectual honesty requires that I credit it and concede what it reached first. The work of Andrew Fanning and Jason Hickel, in *Nature Sustainability* in 2023, prices the over-appropriation of the atmospheric commons as a debt, and the CARICOM reparatory-justice program and the Loss and Damage Fund, established at the United Nations climate conferences in 2022 and operationalized in 2023, price that same over-appropriated commons on a social-cost-of-carbon spine, as a debt running backward through historical emissions and forward through the climate stability being foreclosed. That program reached the bidirectional atmospheric commons before this framework did, and it argues the case at the scale the problem is usually argued, the national aggregate, the question of who owes whom across a shared global carbon budget. I concede the priority plainly. What this framework adds is not the idea but the grain. It prices a single extraction event rather than a national balance, and it reserves against that event rather than netting one nation's account against another's. The idea of an atmospheric commons priced both ways was reached first, by them, at the macro scale. The contribution here is the per-event resolution, one ton at a time. I name no atmospheric or climate-reparations figure and I take no position on who owes whom; that is a question I have no standing to answer and do not try to.

That same restraint governs the backward direction more broadly, and I want to mark its boundary honestly. The same apparatus runs in reverse, and the most developed backward work is Darity and Mullen's pricing of the racial wealth gap and of the longevity gap, the measurable difference in life expectancy that extraction leaves behind as a legacy effect, with McDowell County's life-expectancy gap as the coal-extraction variant of that same legacy-effect method. But I have to name a limit. The coercion vector itself, the question of what cost to assign to coerced labor as a dimension distinct from its measurable legacy effects, remains methodologically unresolved in the reparations-economics field, and it is outside the empirical scope of this book. The framework reaches coerced cases through their legacy effects, the wealth gaps and longevity gaps and disrupted transmission of knowledge and intergenerational trauma that can be measured. The direct pricing of the coercion vector is an open question this book defers, and I defer the moral weight of it to those who have standing I do not. Indigenous land dispossession and the cultural knowledge severed by colonization sit in that same deferred territory, structurally reachable by the methodology applied backward, to be computed in other work, not this one.

Let me close the diagnostic with the plainest fact available, the empirical state of bond posting across American extraction. Across these regimes, the bond posted is far below the residual value of the commons, by orders of magnitude. The black-lung program has paid out roughly $44 billion through 2009, by the Government Accountability Office and Congressional Research Service figures, and I date that floor honestly: it is a conservative figure for the program's full run, and no authoritative cumulative total exists for the years after 2009. Distinct from that cumulative payout is the trust fund's own debt, roughly $5.1 billion outstanding as of September 2024, by Department of Labor and GAO accounting. And no instrument anywhere prices the severance the fund was built to compensate. The deeper finding is that the instrument exists, at scale, but is in part mis-assigned: when the coal excise tax falls short of the program's costs, the difference is borrowed from the federal general fund, which is to say from the public, rather than recovered from the extraction that severed the cost in the first place. The reclamation bonds cover several billion dollars less than the actual cost of reclamation. And the Social Security intergenerational obligation has no Foreclosure Bond posted against it at all. This is the accountability gap, stated as a measurable fact rather than a metaphor: the bond posted is a small fraction of the cost severed. A later chapter supplies the methodology for measuring it precisely; another supplies the politics of closing it.

One discipline governs all of this, and I will end the lineage on it. The question of what is owed is a separate question from what is politically feasible to collect, and the methodology applies regardless of whether any society is currently prepared to post the bond. The framework's contribution here is accounting, not advocacy. The political path from the bond levels we post today toward the residual value of the commons is a separate question, an open matter of political philosophy rather than a single instrument this book pretends to legislate, and a later chapter takes it up as exactly that open question.

So the chapter has done its work. It has named the severance and documented it across the cases, at the scale of a town and an ecosystem and a supply chain and a whole economy, at the scale of the generations and at the scale of a single dying body. It has met the strongest counter-positions at full strength rather than the weak versions of them. It has engaged the worry that pricing the commons is itself the commodification it critiques, and answered that worry from the ground Polanyi and Fraser built. It has restored the rent-seeking complement and credited the traditions that diagnosed the asymmetry first. And it has named, for the first time, the two-instrument response, with the lineage that grounds it. What remains is the methodology that makes the accounting precise, the stress-test that asks whether the mechanism is truly universal, the single worked number that proves the case on one community, the political economy of building the instruments, and the close. The cases have settled that cost severance exists. The only question left is the one this book exists to ask, which is not whether it is happening, but what we are going to do about it.
