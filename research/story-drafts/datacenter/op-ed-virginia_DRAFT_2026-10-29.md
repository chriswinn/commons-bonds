# Knock on the Wall Before You Build

The hardest data center I ever had to find was hidden inside a wall.

A client had run a mainframe in its basement for years without a problem, so during a remodel they had it enclosed in drywall. Then the basement flooded, with just under three feet of water, and they called me. I walked the room knocking on walls, listening for the hollow sound that would tell me where the machine was. I spent years in rooms like that one, and its lesson has stayed with me. A system that runs quietly long enough gets walled off and forgotten, right up until the water comes in.

Virginia is now walling off something far larger. Northern Virginia holds about 13 percent of the world's data-center capacity and a quarter of the capacity in the Americas, by the count of the General Assembly's own Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission [V1]. Data centers bought 28 percent of the electricity Dominion Energy sold in Virginia last year [V2]. The artificial-intelligence boom means more of them, larger and hungrier, and the General Assembly convenes in January with that boom on its desk.

I am not writing to argue against them. The benefits are real, and they belong on the ledger. In Loudoun County, data centers now generate 38 percent of general-fund revenue, by the county administrator's own account [V3], money that pays for schools and roads that would otherwise come out of homeowners' pockets. The tools these buildings run are already changing how work gets done, including in medicine, the field I am now training in.

The trouble is not that data centers cost something. Everything does. The trouble is that the price we charge for them leaves part of the bill out, and the part left out lands on people who never signed anything.

Start with the grid. The commission estimated that a typical Dominion residential customer's bill could rise by $14 to $37 a month by 2040 for new generation and transmission alone [V4], with data-center demand the main driver. Cross the Potomac and the bill keeps going. Maryland's Office of People's Counsel says Marylanders will pay $1.6 billion over ten years for transmission built mainly to serve data centers in other states, chiefly Virginia [V5]. Add the thousands of diesel backup generators permitted across Northern Virginia [V6], and the water drawn to keep the machines cool.

Then there is time, the cost that is hardest to see. The servers inside a data center are typically written off over five or six years [V7]. The transmission lines and power plants built to feed them are designed to last forty years or more [V7], and households pay for them over decades, in their rates. If the demand moves, or the technology changes, or the boom cools, the servers leave with their owners. The poles and the payments stay with the ratepayers.

Virginia's regulators have started to see this. Last November the State Corporation Commission created a new rate class for the largest customers, beginning in 2027. It requires contracts of at least fourteen years, minimum payments on most of the power they contract for, and collateral [V8]. That is a real step, and the commission deserves credit for it. But it protects part of the bill for part of the time. It does not reach the grid investment that outlives a fourteen-year contract, or the generators, or the water, or the building itself when it goes dark.

We have done this before, a few hours west of here. When coal was mined across Appalachia, federal law required operators to post bonds against the cost of restoring the land [V9]. The bonds were simply too small. The documented cost of reclaiming mined Appalachian land runs $7.5 billion to $9.8 billion, while the bonds posted against it total about $3.8 billion. The difference is a bill addressed to no one in particular, which in practice means it is addressed to the public. The instrument was right. The number was wrong.

So here is what I would ask the General Assembly to do. Before a large data center connects, write down its whole bill: the power, the transmission, the water, the air, and what happens to the site and the grid built for it when the servers leave. Then require the operator to post a bond sized to that bill, held in reserve and released as the obligations are actually met. And make the state's sales-tax exemption for data-center equipment [V10] conditional on it. A company confident in its own forecasts gives up nothing by backing them with collateral. A company that is not confident is telling us something we should hear before we build, not after.

Three things this is not.

It is not an argument against artificial intelligence. The benefits go on the same ledger as the costs, and some of them are large.

It is not an argument against Loudoun or Prince William. Those counties made a rational bargain with the information they had.

And it is not a partisan argument. Whoever creates a cost should be the one to bear it. That is not left or right. It is honest accounting and balanced books.

My client learned what was behind the drywall the hard way, standing in three feet of water. Virginia can learn it the easy way. Knock on the wall now, while the bill is still small enough to read.

*Chris Winn is a nursing student in Hampton and a former healthcare-IT executive whose career began at the National Institutes of Health. He is the author of* Commons Bonds, *a forthcoming book on pricing the costs that extraction leaves behind.*
