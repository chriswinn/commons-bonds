**Subject:** Pitch: The AI boom's missing invoice

Dear Editors,

The artificial-intelligence boom has a bill, and households in Virginia have started to read part of it. Northern Virginia holds about 13 percent of the world's data-center capacity [V1]. The state's own legislative auditors estimate that a typical residential electric bill could rise by $14 to $37 a month by 2040 for new generation and transmission [V4]. Next door, Maryland's consumer advocate says Marylanders will pay $1.6 billion over ten years for transmission lines built mainly to serve Virginia's data centers [V5]. And the servers inside those buildings are written off in five or six years, while the grid built to feed them is paid for over four decades [V7].

The public argument about data centers asks whether they are good or bad. I'd like to argue that this is the wrong question. The right one is whether their price includes their cost, and my answer is that it doesn't. We have seen this accounting before. Federal law made Appalachian coal operators post bonds against the cost of restoring mined land [V9], and the bonds came to roughly $3.8 billion against a documented need of $7.5 billion to $9.8 billion. The public is paying the difference. The instrument was right; the number was wrong. With data centers we have a rare chance to get the number right before the building goes up rather than decades after.

The essay would walk the full ledger of a large data-center campus from the public record: power, transmission, water, backup diesel, tax revenue, jobs, and the question nobody prices, which is what happens to the grid and the site when the servers leave. It counts the benefits on the same page as the costs. Loudoun County now draws 38 percent of its general fund from data centers [V3], and that is real. It then proposes a concrete instrument: a bond, posted up front and sized to the whole bill. It would build on the new rate class Virginia's regulators created last year, with fourteen-year contracts, minimum payments and collateral [V8], and learn from the coal bonds' failure to size the obligation honestly.

I come at this from inside the machine room. I spent years working in data centers, including one memorable search for a mainframe a client had walled into drywall before their basement flooded. My career began at the National Institutes of Health and later took me to the CEO's seat of a healthcare-IT company. I'm now a nursing student in Hampton, Virginia, and the author of *Commons Bonds*, a forthcoming book on pricing the costs that extraction leaves behind.

I see the piece at 3,500 to 4,500 words. It is timely through Virginia's General Assembly session in January and the rate cases that follow.

With thanks for considering it,

Chris Winn
