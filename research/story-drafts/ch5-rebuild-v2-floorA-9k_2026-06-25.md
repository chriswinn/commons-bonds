# Commons Bonds
*By Chris Winn*

## Chapter 5: The Accountability Gap

## The pattern you cannot stop seeing

There is a shape that, once you have seen it clearly, you start finding everywhere, and it is always the same shape. An entity takes value out of a resource, a community, or a system. The taking generates a cost. The cost gets detached from the party that banked the value and set down instead on someone with less power to refuse it, less money to fight it, and less ability to move away from it. And by the time the cost comes due, the party that took the value is no longer reachable to pay. The company has dissolved. The executives have retired. The generation that made the promise has died. The cost remains, and it has to land on somebody, and it lands on whoever is still standing closest to where it falls.

The bill always arrives. That is the only thing about this you can count on. The single open question is who is still standing where it lands.

I want to walk you through a spread of cases before I name anything, because the naming will mean more once you have felt the recurrence in your own body. A vermiculite mine in a Montana valley. An oil platform burning in the Gulf of Mexico. A tanker on a reef in Alaska. A rare-earth complex in Inner Mongolia. The whole American financial system in the autumn of 2008. The compact between the generations that funds a retirement. A healthcare system that has quietly turned an internet fundraising page into a safety net. Seven settings. Different industries, different decades, different continents.

Each one has its own arithmetic. The mechanism underneath the arithmetic is identical every time.

## Libby, Montana

W.R. Grace ran a vermiculite mine outside the small town of Libby, in the northwest corner of Montana, from 1963 to 1990, twenty-seven years of steady operation. The mine was a serious commercial property. It supplied more than seventy percent of the vermiculite used in the United States, and the lightweight insulation made from it, sold under the name Zonolite, was blown into the attics of tens of millions of American homes. Across its life the operation produced revenue on the order of a hundred million dollars. It was, by any ordinary measure, a success.

The vermiculite was laced with a particularly lethal form of asbestos. Internal company documents, surfaced years later in litigation, show that Grace identified the contamination early and went on measuring asbestos exposure in its workforce throughout the years of operation while continuing to mine. The dust did not stay at the mine. Workers carried it home on their clothes, and their wives breathed it shaking out the laundry, and their children breathed it, and children who played on the waste piles near town breathed it. Hundreds of residents have died of asbestos-related disease. Thousands have been diagnosed with lung abnormalities. The Environmental Protection Agency declared Libby a public-health emergency, the first time it had ever made such a declaration for a man-made disaster.

Here is the part that should make the case impossible to keep at arm's length. The Zonolite that left Libby is not gone. Much of it is still where it was installed, sitting in the insulation of millions of attics, including, statistically, in a meaningful share of the homes of people who will read this book. The distance between the reader and this case may be no greater than the distance between a reader's chair and the ceiling above it.

Now set the value against the cost. Grace banked roughly a hundred million dollars over the life of the mine. Against that, I am not going to hand you a clean, dramatic ratio, because the honest figures do not produce one. The documented public spending specific to Libby, the Superfund cleanup, the federal and state response, sums to somewhere around nine hundred million to a billion dollars, which by itself is a ratio of nine or ten to one against the lifetime revenue. The uncompensated human cost, the mortality and the chronic illness priced separately, plausibly carries the total into the low billions. I will call that what it is: an order-of-magnitude estimate, built from disclosed components, not a number lifted whole from a single source. The mortality rate in Libby has run at many times the national average.

Grace declared bankruptcy in 2001. Its assets were reorganized. Shareholders and executives moved on with their lives. The town stayed where it was.

There is a piece of this case that does more work than any figure, and it is about the law. In 2005 the federal government indicted Grace and several senior executives, naming them by role, in what was at the time the largest environmental criminal prosecution the country had mounted. After a long trial it ended, in 2009, in an acquittal across the board. I want to be careful about what that acquittal does and does not mean. It is not evidence that the harm did not happen. The harm is in the death certificates. The acquittal is evidence of something else: that the criminal law, an architecture built to prosecute identifiable acts committed with identifiable knowledge at identifiable moments, is not structurally suited to a mechanism that operates across decades through thousands of accumulated decisions, no one of which is the crime by itself. Katharina Pistor, in her work on how law turns ordinary things into capital, calls these legal modules, and the bankruptcy discharge and the acquittal are not failures of those modules. They are design features. The modules are built to encode and protect capital, and they do exactly that. Where Pistor diagnoses the modules that encode the severance, this book is trying to price what the modules sever.

## Deepwater Horizon

On the twentieth of April, 2010, the Deepwater Horizon platform blew out in the Gulf of Mexico, killing eleven workers and releasing roughly 4.9 million barrels of oil over the eighty-seven days it took to cap the well. The operator, BP, had drilled down through geological formations that its own engineers had flagged as extreme-risk. The value BP had pulled out of the Gulf over the years was real. It had been banked, distributed to shareholders, spent.

The costs severed in every direction at once. Spatially: oil refined and sold in markets far from the water poisoned the waters where fishing families had worked for generations. Temporally: the damage outlasted by years the quarterly reports it briefly disrupted. Ecologically: keystone species absorbed toxins that affect reproduction across decades, and the creatures bearing that cost cannot file a claim, retain a lawyer, or appear in a settlement.

What was recovered against what was lost does not resolve into a tidy figure, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. The natural-resource damage assessment alone ran to around $17.2 billion. Tourism losses, on a roughly fifteen-month horizon, ran on the order of $7.6 billion. Peer-reviewed estimates of coastal property-value loss come in around $3.8 to $5.0 billion. Set those and the other documented losses against the roughly $60 to $65 billion that BP paid out in penalties, settlements, and restoration funds, and you get not a single decimal but a band: a recovery ratio well below complete, with a large remainder of cost that no payment reached. The people inside that remainder are the ones who could not move. The fishing knowledge that took generations to build, the cultural fabric of the coast, the severed ecological relationships, none of that appeared on any ledger BP had to balance, and it was free to lose for exactly that reason: it had no line on any document.

The same oil drifted into Mexican waters. BP's settlement with Mexico came to roughly $25 million, orders of magnitude smaller, per comparable stretch of damaged coastline, than what was paid on the United States side. The slick did not respect that asymmetry. The slick distributed itself by current and wind. The settlement distributed itself by legal architecture: on the American side, the Oil Pollution Act of 1990, a developed body of state tort and environmental law, and the machinery of class-action aggregation; on the Mexican side, a far weaker set of tools for pursuing a foreign-domiciled corporation across a border. Same oil. Two prices. The difference set entirely by which legal modules the injured party could reach for.

## Exxon Valdez

In March of 1989 the Exxon Valdez ran aground on Bligh Reef in Prince William Sound and spilled millions of gallons of crude into some of the most productive cold water on the continent. A jury later returned a large punitive award against Exxon; on appeal, across years of litigation, that award was cut down sharply. The Pacific herring fishery in the Sound collapsed in the years after the spill and never properly returned, and the people who had worked it had no architecture through which to file for the loss they bore. I am handing the full accounting of this case forward to the next chapter, which gives it the room it deserves. I name it here only so that the line runs unbroken: Libby, Deepwater, Valdez, and now Baotou.

## Baotou

Baotou, in Inner Mongolia, sits at the center of a rare-earth processing complex fed by the Bayan Obo deposit, and its output is woven into nearly every advanced technology a modern reader touches: the phone in your hand, the wind turbines on the ridgeline, the motor in an electric car. The scale is easy to misstate, so let me state it carefully. China accounts for roughly sixty percent of the world's rare earths. Bayan Obo and Baotou together account for roughly half of China's share. The complex is enormous, but it is not the whole world's supply by itself.

The processing is brutal on the surrounding land. Separating rare-earth oxides generates heavy metals, residual acids, and, because the ores carry thorium, a fraction of low-level radioactivity, and the waste runs into tailings impoundments near where people live. Residents report elevated rates of cancer, respiratory disease, and birth defects. Farmland within a radius of the works is no longer usable. A conservative figure for the wastewater intensity is around ten tons of wastewater per ton of rare-earth oxide, at the low end of the published range. The data here is genuinely constrained, because access to Chinese health and environmental records is restricted, and I will not pretend to a completeness I do not have. But constraint is not silence. Peer-reviewed work exists. Zhang and colleagues, in 2022, and Zhou and Ge, in 2021, document the environmental burden in specific terms.

You will sometimes see a figure of a hundred billion dollars or more attached to the remediation of this place. I cannot source it, and I am not going to use a number I cannot stand behind. What can be said is more modest and more defensible: the annual environmental cost runs into the billions, the industry's revenue runs somewhere around ten to twelve billion dollars a year, and the share of that revenue that has ever gone to remediation has historically been a small fraction.

Rare earths are the book's clearest case of a resource with almost no substitute, and that fact sharpens everything. Oil can be left in the ground, and the rents from extracting it can be invested forward; that is the bargain Norway struck, and the next chapters will return to it. Rare earths offer no such bargain. No fund reconstitutes a foreclosed deposit, because there is no engineered stand-in at the scale these technologies demand. When the substitutes run that thin, the verdict hardens: the severed cost cannot be made good by reinvesting somewhere else. It can only be prevented, or paid. Baotou is where the limit of substitution stops being a parameter in a function and becomes a field of tailings ponds.

And here is the landing point this case exists to deliver. The engineer designing with the magnets, the driver of the electric car, the household drawing power from the turbine, none of them is a villain. The mechanism does not need villains. The consumer shares in the value that came out of Baotou and is separated, by ten thousand miles of supply chain, from the cost that stayed there. Whatever the individual's moral standing, it is beside the structural point. Spatial severance through a global supply chain is precisely the thing that keeps the consumer and the cost-bearer from ever seeing each other. The distance is not an accident of the system. The system manufactures the distance for a living.

## 2008 and the double risk transfer

Now the scale jumps, and the cases so far were rehearsal for this one. The 2008 financial crisis struck the entire economy at once, and there is no geographic or cultural alibi left to reach for. This is the case where the mechanism becomes hardest to look away from, and it is the case I will give the most room, because it is the place where the strongest objections to everything I am arguing come due, and I want to meet them in the open.

The first transfer was slow and it was sold as a gift. In 1978 a provision of the tax code created the structure that became the 401(k), and over the decades that followed, employers steadily replaced defined-benefit pensions, the kind that promised a fixed retirement income, with individual accounts. This was presented to workers as freedom and as ownership: your money, your account, your choices. The part that went undisclosed was whose freedom it actually was. It was, chiefly, the corporation's freedom from a liability. Market risk, longevity risk, and the risk of one's own behavior under uncertainty all moved off institutional balance sheets and onto individuals who had no way to pool or hedge any of it. That transfer ran quietly for about thirty years.

The second transfer was fast. Between roughly 2001 and 2008, the financial industry built mortgage-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations whose complexity was not incidental but structural, because only an instrument too complex to evaluate could be sold to investors who could not evaluate it. These were packaged into the very retirement accounts the first transfer had created. When they failed in 2008, the losses came to rest in those accounts.

Picture the quarterly statement that arrived in the spring of 2009. Same typeface, same columns as always. The number that had climbed for two decades had fallen by an amount that erased years of saving. There was no one to call. The freedom, it turned out, had meant that the loss was hers to carry alone.

On the household side, the magnitudes are large and they need to be attributed carefully. United States household net worth fell by roughly eleven trillion dollars, a figure that comes from the Federal Reserve's flow-of-funds accounts, the Z.1 release. That is the Fed's number, and I attribute it to the Fed. Atif Mian and Amir Sufi, in *House of Debt*, did something different and equally important: they reconstructed how that loss was distributed. It fell hardest on the households with the least wealth and the most debt, through the plain arithmetic of leverage. The owner with no mortgage loses a fraction of value when prices fall. The owner who borrowed against almost the whole of the house can be wiped out entirely by the same price decline. Mian and Sufi's own central magnitude is about $5.5 trillion of housing wealth, and I keep their work for what it does best, the leverage and distribution and spending analysis, rather than borrowing it for the larger total. Across the housing-net-worth distribution they document a marginal-propensity-to-consume differential on the order of three to one between the lower deciles and the upper: the household at the bottom cut its spending far harder per dollar of lost wealth than the household at the top, which is why a loss concentrated at the bottom did so much damage to the wider economy.

Then look at the rescue, and look at its shape. The Troubled Asset Relief Program committed $700 billion. The Federal Reserve's cumulative emergency lending exceeded sixteen trillion dollars, a figure established by the Government Accountability Office's 2011 audit, which is the one 2008 number on this scale that is genuinely well sourced. Executive bonuses at rescued institutions resumed within roughly eighteen months. Against all of that, the household side saw something near ten million foreclosure filings between 2008 and 2012, of which four to five million completed, by CoreLogic's tally about 4.1 million completed through 2012. The system found a way to move trillions in a matter of days for the institutions. It organized nothing of comparable scale for the people in the houses. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission concluded in 2011 that the crisis was the result of human action and inaction, not of forces beyond anyone's control.

I can hear the objections, and they are good ones. Let me take them one at a time, because a reader who has them deserves to have them met rather than managed.

The first is borrower agency. Many of the mortgages that defaulted went to people who could not have serviced them under any reasonable assumption, and they signed the papers anyway. That is true, and it matters. But agency runs on information, and the information architecture was built to be adversarial. Origination commissions were tied to volume, not to whether the loan would default. Disclosed terms differed from the terms in the contract. Adjustable-rate products were marketed to borrowers whose grasp of how a rate reset would work was expected, by the people selling them, to be incomplete. The pattern is documented in the enforcement record the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau built after the crisis. The agency was real. The asymmetry of information was the lever that turned it.

The second is that this was a global event, not a failure of American policy. European banks were exposed, Asian export economies caught the contagion, mortgage-backed securities sat on balance sheets across borders, and the Basel capital framework played its part. All true, and all of it amplifies the finding rather than dissolving it. The instruments that propagated the damage were predominantly American-originated. The fee architecture that rewarded origination over quality, the conflict of interest baked into an issuer-pays model for the rating agencies, and the exemptions that let the whole thing scale were American policy choices. The fire spread worldwide. The matches were struck here.

The third is that TARP was repaid, and the Treasury's accounting shows substantial recovery and, on some retrospectives, a modest profit. I do not dispute the accounting. I dispute that TARP is the right unit of account. The cost this case is about is not the federal outlay on the rescue. It is the unrecovered welfare loss to the foreclosed households, the workers whose account balances reset to a lower base from which to compound for the rest of their lives, the regional economies that contracted around them, and the trust between citizens and institutions that the asymmetric rescue spent down. None of that is recovered when TARP is repaid. None of it appears anywhere on the balance-sheet accounts the recovery argument points to.

The fourth is that Dodd-Frank fixed this. The 2010 act created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the Financial Stability Oversight Council, restricted proprietary trading through the Volcker Rule, and raised capital and liquidity requirements. Those are real changes, and I want to be clear that this is a compliment, not a dismissal: Dodd-Frank is a partial, early installment of precisely the accountability architecture this book is arguing for. But it was partial, and it has proven reversible. The Volcker Rule was later loosened. The Bureau was later weakened. Systemic-importance designations have been contested and rolled back. A reform that can be unwound has not closed the gap, and it has not consolidated to the point where its persistence is structurally guaranteed. Chapter 9 names Dodd-Frank among the entering wedges, the partial measures already in the world that point toward the architecture this book describes.

It is worth knowing that the asymmetric rescue was a choice and not a law of nature, because a country in a structurally similar position chose otherwise. Sweden in the 1930s faced banking-crisis pressure of the same family as the United States faced in 1929 to 1933 and again in 2008 to 2009, and Sweden paired its bank rescue with substantial relief for households. The 1931 exit from the gold standard and devaluation of the krona restored export competitiveness and eased the burden of household debt. Bank rescue was coordinated with mortgage modification. Public-works employment programs in the mid-1930s put income back into households. The 1938 Saltsjöbaden Agreement institutionalized the welfare-state architecture that followed. The comparison comes out of the comparative-banking-crisis literature, Kindleberger and Eichengreen among others, and it stands as a plain demonstration that rescuing the banks and relieving the households are not an either-or. A society can choose both. The United States chose otherwise in 2008 and 2009, and again in 2020. Chapter 9 develops the cross-country comparison; here it is enough to register that the choice was contingent.

Three settings remain after this one, and they keep the same mechanism while changing the distance across which the cost is shifted. In the next, the distance is time itself. In the one after, it narrows all the way down to the boundary of a single body.

## Social Security

The most devastating example hides inside the system Americans treat as the opposite of extraction: the promise that each generation will care for the one before it. I want to be precise, because this section carries the most consequential figure discipline in the chapter, and because it would be easy to read what follows as an attack on the program. It is not. Social Security is genuine, essential, and not a Ponzi scheme. The critique here is narrow. It is aimed at one thing only, the specific architecture by which the obligations were funded.

The mechanics are straightforward. The system was designed in 1935 as pay-as-you-go: today's workers fund today's retirees. For decades it ran surpluses, held in special-issue Treasury obligations in the trust fund. The retirement of the baby-boom generation was foreseeable, and foreseen, for forty years. By the early 2020s, outflows had begun to exceed payroll-tax inflows, and under current law and projection the trust fund depletes around 2033, after which payroll-tax revenue would fund roughly seventy-seven percent of promised benefits.

You will encounter, in some quarters, a figure of $108 trillion described as the foregone compounding return that Social Security cost the country, sometimes paired with the phrase "the largest cost severance in human history." I want to take that figure apart in the open, because it is false, and using it would discredit the honest case. It cannot be reconstructed from its own inputs. It runs roughly two orders of magnitude above the nearest defensible counterfactual. It exceeds the trust fund's own peak, exceeds the cumulative income of the entire program, and exceeds the ceiling you would get from the most generous equity-versus-bond return differential. It is the dishonest version, and it should be named as such and then set down.

Here is the honest version, with its method disclosed. Over the program's life, cumulative contributions come to roughly twenty-six trillion dollars, and cumulative benefits paid come to roughly the same, around twenty-six trillion. The money that was ever actually available to invest was not the whole flow but the running surplus, the accumulated reserve, which peaked around 2.7 trillion dollars. If you take that investable surplus and compound it at a realistic real return, net of the pay-as-you-go payouts that were always being made, the foregone-return counterfactual comes to a few trillion dollars, not a hundred. The severed cost runs into the trillions. That is the floor I will stand on. The nearest external estimate, from work at Brookings and the Center for Retirement Research, lands near a trillion dollars on a forty-percent-equity-from-1984 basis, available as a sourced single number if a reader wants one.

There is a separate quantity that should never be blurred into this one. The Trustees project a seventy-five-year actuarial shortfall of roughly $22.6 trillion. That is the gap between projected obligations and projected revenue over the actuarial window. It is a real and important number, and it is not the same thing as the foregone-investment counterfactual. Two different quantities. I will not stack them into one impressive-sounding total.

Now the structural heart of the case, which is its strongest move. The cost severance here is not in how the surpluses were invested. Holding the reserves in Treasuries was administratively prudent; I will defend that choice in a moment against the people who attack it. The severance is in how the obligations were generated. The benefit expansions enacted across the 1960s, 1970s, and 1980s were passed by Congresses nearly all of whose members would leave office long before the demographic-transition obligations they created came due. Those expansions were funded by the favorable ratio of working-age people to retirees that prevailed in exactly those decades. And every actuarial report of the period openly projected the deficit window the expansions would eventually hit. The political reward for generating the promise was collected in the demographic-surplus moment. The cost of funding it was assigned to taxpayers who did not yet exist and could not refuse. Time is the distance across which the cost was shifted. The value was extracted at one moment; the bill was mailed to a later one.

Norway, which the earlier chapter laid out and which I will not re-derive here, faced a version of the same structural problem and chose a different architecture: channeling resource rents into a compounding sovereign fund that accumulates real capital for the generations to come. Same problem of intergenerational obligation. Different design choice, made at specific moments by specific people. Different outcome. The difference is not national wealth or demography. It is architecture.

Two objections deserve a real hearing. The first is the public-finance prudence argument: holding reserves in Treasuries is exactly what a sound fund manager does, and equities would have exposed retirees to market and political-economy risk. I concede this fully. It is correct at the level of portfolio management. But it does not reach the claim, because the claim operates upstream, at the point where the obligations were generated, not at the point where the reserves were invested. The two arguments pass each other in the night.

The second objection is about tractability: the gap is closeable through phased adjustments, payroll-rate increases, changes to the taxable-wage cap, refinements to the benefit formula at the high end, eligibility ages that track longevity, all laid out by the Trustees and various commissions. I concede the arithmetic. The gap is mathematically closeable. But every closure transfers the obligation onto the cohort caught in the demographic-deficit window, the people who will pay more or receive less, and not onto the cohorts that extracted the electoral value of generating the obligation in the first place. Tractability is not the absence of severance. It is severance unfolding on schedule.

## The healthcare commons

Now the distance narrows to the edge of a single body, and the severed costs become the specific financial ruin of specific households at the most vulnerable stretches of a life.

Watch what has happened to medical crowdfunding. Across the 2010s, the practice grew from a novelty into something structural. By 2020, more than a third of the campaigns on the largest platform were medical. Across roughly 2016 to 2020, on the order of 438,000 medical campaigns raised more than two billion dollars across some 21.7 million individual donations. About twelve percent of those campaigns met their goals. About sixteen percent received nothing at all. The platform's own chief executive has acknowledged that it was never designed as a mechanism for financing healthcare and that it became one by default. I want to be careful with the framing here: crowdfunding for medical costs exists in Canada, in the United Kingdom, in Germany. The load-bearing claim is not that it exists only in the United States. It is that it operates at this scale, and as a default, in a wealthy country that has chosen not to build the formal net the informal one is standing in for. The work of Saleh and colleagues, in 2020, documents the magnitude of the American case against Canada and the United Kingdom; the campaign-scale figures come from Igra and Kenworthy, in 2022.

There is a layer beneath the aggregate that is worse than the aggregate. Medical campaigns run by people with distinctively Black or Hispanic surnames raise less money and are less likely to meet their goals, controlling for geography and period. The informal backstop reproduces the very inequities the formal net was meant to reduce. A safety net keyed to the size and wealth of your personal network routes the most help to the people who need it least, which is the precise opposite of what a safety net is for.

Under all of this sits a structural gap that has been widening for half a century. Mishel and Bivens, at the Economic Policy Institute, have documented the decoupling of median wages from productivity that set in after the early 1970s: the economy kept getting more productive, and the typical paycheck stopped keeping pace. Over the same span, healthcare costs grew at multiples of general inflation. The household pressure is the widening space between those two curves. Jacob Hacker named the broad pattern in *The Great Risk Shift* in 2006: a systematic transfer of economic risk from institutions onto individuals and families. The pension that became a 401(k) and the medical bill that became a fundraising page are the same shift wearing different clothes.

The mechanism operating on a single body at the end of a life is documented in Katy Butler's published account of her father's care. The reimbursement architecture pointed one way at every turn. A long office visit to discuss whether a treatment was even wanted was reimbursed at a small fraction of what a procedure such as a pacemaker implantation paid, and a large share of the procedure's cost flowed to the device manufacturer. The liability architecture pointed the same direction: performing the procedure protected the clinician against a malpractice claim, while declining it created exposure. The pacemaker kept a failing body going through years of subsequent cognitive decline. The caregiving labor that sustained those years was borne by an elderly spouse, and it appeared on no ledger anywhere. I am rendering this strictly within what Butler documented, because the discipline of this book is to invent nothing about a real person's life.

Atul Gawande, in *Being Mortal*, set out the systemic version of the same thing. The reimbursement structure rewards intervention at every decision point while underfunding the infrastructure and training for palliative care, and the aggregate result is the over-treatment of the dying, the individual case reproduced at the scale of a whole system. No single clinician has to choose extraction over compassion for this to happen. The pattern emerges from many rational responses to incentives, and the incentives are themselves a policy-design choice. The same logic runs through maternal care, mental-health care, and the long management of chronic disease.

## The pattern made visible

Stand back now and let the cases resolve into one figure. An entity, which might be a company or a generation or a political system, takes value out of a resource or a system that could have been managed for the long term. The taking generates costs: environmental, occupational, financial, the foreclosing of options a future would have had. And those costs are severed onto parties with the least power to prevent the transfer, the workers, the communities, the ecosystems, the people not yet born.

A word about the word, because it has a second life that can mislead. Severance is also the name of the check a company writes when it lets a worker go, and a reader who hears that meaning first might suspect the term smuggles in the very commodifying-of-labor frame this book resists. It does the opposite. Both senses share a root because both name a cut: the worker cut loose from the wage, the cost cut loose from the party that caused it. The book keeps the word because the cut is the thing it is naming, and softening the word would do in language exactly what the accounting does in fact, which is to make the severing disappear.

The original beneficiary escapes the full reckoning under a handful of recurring covers. Distance, when the cost lands somewhere the beneficiary will never see. Time, when the cost lands on a generation that cannot yet vote. The asymmetry of what is known, when one party understands the risk and the other is structurally kept from understanding it. The asymmetry of who can refuse, when one party can walk away and the other cannot. The normalization of the cost as a routine condition of doing business. And the appearance of abundance, which keeps the cost from registering as a cost at all until the abundance runs out. These are illustrations, not a closed list; a later chapter names the full set.

Walk the cases back through in a single breath. The town that could not move. The fishing communities and the ecosystems that could not relocate. The rural communities near the tailings ponds with no exit. The foreclosed households. The future Americans who cannot opt out of an obligation they inherited. The campaigns that failed to fund and the elderly spouse whose labor appeared on no ledger. In each, the beneficiary leaves and the cost stays. The companies declare bankruptcy. The politicians leave office. The promise-making generations pass away. The cost remains, transferred to whoever cannot escape it.

The claim that holds all of this together is structural, and it has no villain in it. This is not a system failing. It is a system working as designed, not by anyone's conscious intention but by the accumulated logic of structures that reward the extraction of value and externalize its cost onto whoever is least able to resist. I want to make a larger claim and then earn it: that wherever the taking has outrun the restoring, economies have, at bottom, operated this way for a very long time. I will not leave that hanging as an assertion. The next two sections discharge it, through the people who diagnosed the asymmetry before this book existed and the people who named the underlying object for what it is. The forward question is not whether this is happening; the cases settle that. The question is what it would look like to build accountability that interrupts the pattern before the cost is severed, rather than chasing it afterward once the beneficiary has gone.

## The same object, named honestly

I want to name the object all of these cases share, because once it has a name the whole argument changes shape.

A commons, in the sense this book means it, is defined structurally and not by whether it happens to be ecological. A commons is anything that is depended upon and shared but was never produced as a commodity for sale. On that definition a coral reef, the atmosphere, a community, a body of labor, and a future generation are not five different kinds of thing. They are one kind of thing: a commons whose abundance hid its cost. And the same apparatus prices all of them, running forward against the foreclosure of an ecological or atmospheric option, and backward against an extraction already realized in human lives.

The strongest objection to everything I am about to propose comes from Michael Sandel, and it is the objection I most need to answer, so let me state it at full strength. To put a price on a sacred landscape, on a community, on the unborn, is to launder the priceless into a transaction. It concedes the extractor's own frame. It risks legitimating extraction-for-a-fee, turning a thing that should never have been for sale into a thing with a sticker on it. Sandel argues, in *What Money Can't Buy*, that there are goods money should not touch, and that pricing them corrodes the very goods it claims to protect.

Here is the answer, and it is the hinge of the whole book. A cost set at zero is not a cost left sacred and unpriced. Zero is a number. It is, in fact, the number the extractive accounting already assigned, the number that made the extraction profitable in the first place. The reef priced at zero, the community priced at zero, the future priced at zero: those are not neutral abstentions from the market. They are the market's verdict, entered silently and in the extractor's favor. What this framework refuses is that zero. Pricing the commons in order to reserve against its loss, in order to re-embed it in the obligations a society actually honors, is the opposite of pricing it in order to sell it. One move puts a thing on the auction block. The other takes it off.

This move is not mine. It descends directly from Karl Polanyi. In *The Great Transformation*, published in 1944, Polanyi named labor, land, and money as fictitious commodities, things treated as though they were produced for sale when in truth they never were. The structural definition I just gave, the commons as the depended-upon-but-not-produced-for-sale, is the operational descendant of that thesis. The diagnostic move at the center of this book, which is to imagine the commons absent and ask what cost then appears, operationalizes Polanyi's intuition. And the double movement he described, society reaching to re-embed what marketization had torn loose, is exactly what the bond apparatus is. It is an accounting instrument for re-embedding. The framework is Polanyi's double movement made into a tool. It prices and reserves against what Polanyi was able only to diagnose.

The closest living anchor is Nancy Fraser, and she pays off a debt this book incurred two chapters ago. Fraser distinguishes expropriation from exploitation. Exploitation takes through the wage relation, with the form of a contract. Expropriation takes without even that form, appropriating the unpaid labor of social reproduction and the unpaid contribution of nature that sit behind the wage relation and make it possible. The same-object claim, that reef-extraction and community-extraction priced backward are a single move, is Fraser's terrain. It takes from you without even the form of a contract. The earlier chapter planted a seed about the etymology of severance and the logic of expropriation and left it unnamed. The name is Fraser, and her work, "Behind Marx's Hidden Abode" and *Cannibal Capitalism*, is where the seed comes due.

I have to be honest about the relationship here, because the honesty is the whole point. The tradition Polanyi and Fraser built resists monetization on principle. They did not argue for putting a number on the commons. They argued that the commons should never have been subjected to the logic of the market at all. So I am not claiming that either of them would endorse the pricing. I am claiming something narrower and, I think, more defensible: that this framework operationalizes their diagnosis, and it runs that diagnosis in the cost-reckoning direction, against the commodification they opposed. A framework that stands inside the tradition that refused the asset frame is using their refusal, not betraying it. The number is not there to make the commons sellable. The number is there to force an honest reckoning where a dishonest zero has been sitting all along.

A critic will press one more time, and the press is fair: pricing the commons is financialization by another route, the commons repackaged as a new asset class. The first part of the answer is the lineage I have just laid out. Claiming Polanyi and Fraser, the very thinkers who diagnosed commodification as a harm, marks the difference between pricing to force an honest accounting and financializing the commons as an asset. Accounting a cost is not coding an asset. But I do not want to leave that as an assertion, so let me show it in the code's own terms, using Pistor's account of what turns an ordinary asset into capital. Pistor identifies four legal attributes: priority, which ranks a claim ahead of others; durability, which extends it through time; universality, which makes it good against the world; and convertibility, which lets it be exchanged for money or for another tradable claim. The accountability bond is engineered to lack the two attributes that let capital circulate. It is not convertible: it can be redeemed only for the discharge of the specific liability it records, never for money or any tradable claim. And it is not transferable: it cannot be sold, traded, or repackaged into a security. A debt that can only be paid, and never traded, is the structural opposite of a coded asset. That is the difference between coding an asset and accounting a cost, demonstrated rather than merely claimed.

The most forceful living version of the financialization worry belongs to Brett Christophers, whose work on the asset economy makes the case that more and more of the world is being turned into rent-bearing property. The answer in principle is the one just given, that accounting a cost is the opposite of coding an asset; the fuller engagement with his specific argument in *Rentier Capitalism* waits for a later chapter.

This passage does triple duty, and that is why it sits at the center of the chapter. It names the structural-commons identity, the claim that reef and atmosphere and community and labor and future generation are one object priced forward or backward. It pays off the Fraser seed by name. And from a single position, the position Polanyi and Fraser built, it disarms the whole cluster of objections that say the framework's own pricing is the commodification it claims to critique.

## Who shaped the system

Before I get to the toolkit for asking who built the architecture, I owe a debt to the tradition that diagnosed the asymmetry this entire chapter has been documenting, the asymmetry in which a powerful capturer of value shifts the cost onto a bearer who is absent, diffuse, or dominated.

K. William Kapp set it out systematically in *The Social Costs of Private Enterprise* in 1950, an accounting of the costs that private enterprise socializes, pushing them off its own books and onto the public, the worker, and the environment. Kapp wanted those social costs reckoned, and in that sense he is the closest ally this book has: it continues his project by pricing the social costs per extraction event rather than leaving them, as he found them, unbilled. Joan Martínez-Alier, working in political ecology, mapped the ecological-distribution conflicts in which one party captures the value of an environmental taking and another, usually poorer and with less voice, bears the ecological cost. He named the asymmetry with a precision the cases here keep illustrating. And Martínez-Alier rejects monetary valuation on principle, on grounds of incommensurability that he traces to Otto Neurath: some values, he holds, cannot honestly be put on a common monetary scale at all. So I credit his diagnosis of the asymmetry, I honor his refusal, and I do not claim he would endorse the pricing. It is the same careful move I made with Polanyi and Fraser. These traditions named the asymmetry. The framework operationalizes the naming by pricing it, and this is where the larger claim from a few sections back, that extractive economies have at bottom always worked this way, partly comes to rest.

There is a second, distinct question, and a rigorous tradition built to answer it. The framework's apparatus accounts for who bore the cost; that is the ledger. But a separate and necessary question is who shaped the architecture that distributed the cost the way it did. The Public Choice tradition, Buchanan and Tullock and those who followed, supplies the toolkit for that question. Tullock's 1967 work on rent-seeking and Olson's 1965 analysis of how concentrated interests outmaneuver diffuse ones are the load-bearing tools. Where the extractors and the cost-bearers are distinct populations, the rent-seeking chain runs cleanly, and you can trace it: identify the concentrated-interest actors who shaped the design, the lobbying and the coalitions that produced it, whose interests it served and whose it cost.

The cases supply the roster. At Libby, Grace's own released internal documents show the lobbying it pursued and the regulatory delay it sought on asbestos. At Deepwater, the Minerals Management Service had been a captured regulator, its personnel taking travel and gifts from the industry it oversaw, its staff moving back and forth through a revolving door, the capture so thoroughly documented that the agency was split apart after the disaster, with the Interior Department's inspector general having reported on it before the blowout. In 2008, the relevant architecture was built by the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999, the Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000, and a series of preemption rulings by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, all shaped by financial-industry lobbying documented by the Center for Responsive Politics and in congressional hearings. In healthcare, pharmaceutical lobbying has led American industry totals for years, and the long prohibition on Medicare negotiating drug prices, since modified, is a textbook rent-seeking outcome. And the coal economy of McDowell County, which a later chapter takes up in full, rests on a multi-generational history of the industry's dominance over West Virginia's legislative and regulatory architecture.

Not every case yields a clean chain. Where the extractor and the cost-bearer blur into the same population, the rent-seeking analysis runs out of traction. Baotou's cost is shaped by a demand that reaches back to every consumer in the supply chain. Social Security's intertemporal dynamic makes today's beneficiaries the former payers. In those cases the framework's structural accounting carries the weight that actor-identification cannot.

I want to be exact about the relationship, because it would be easy to misread. The framework draws on the vocabulary of rent-seeking; it does not endorse the broader Buchanan project. The rent-seeking tools belong in the toolkit, not in the opposing camp. The framework's accounting of who bore the cost supplies precisely the ledger to which a Public Choice analysis of who shaped the architecture applies, and the two fit together. A reader who wants the architecture-shaping analysis in depth should read Buchanan, Tullock, and their successors in parallel, and the analytical vocabulary of rent-seeking survives many readings of Buchanan's wider program. This is an invitation to readers at the American Enterprise Institute, at Cato, at Mercatus, and at George Mason, to bring their tools to a ledger they have not yet been handed. Chapter 9 develops the complement at length.

## Restitution and Foreclosure

The cases produce a question, and the question is the pivot of the whole book. What would an honest accounting of these severances actually require? The framework's answer is a pair of instruments, and I am going to name them here at the level of structure only. The next chapter formalizes them. A later chapter handles the politics of putting them in place.

The accountability gap has two temporal faces. There is harm already done: the lives, communities, and ecosystems already damaged, the realized portfolio of extraction. And there is harm not yet done: the costs that current extraction is foreclosing for the generations to come. An architecture that addresses one and not the other addresses half the problem and calls it whole.

So there are two instruments. The Restitution Bond discharges harm already realized; it looks backward, and it answers to specific people and places already hurt. The Foreclosure Bond discharges the future-generation costs that current extraction is foreclosing; it looks forward, and it answers not to specific identifiable people but to outcomes a future will or will not have. Together they form the aggregate accountability bond the framework's central relation requires, in which the cost severed is the residual value of the commons minus the bond posted against it.

Why a bond, and not the more familiar instruments. A tax is paid at the moment of a transaction into general revenue, with no architecture that matches a posted amount to a particular future obligation. A fee licenses an activity rather than discharging a harm. A fund aggregates money without discharging anything in particular. A bond is different in exactly the ways that matter here: it is posted before the full cost has been borne, it carries forward across the long tail of the cost, and it discharges observably, a specific amount against a specific liability. The world already runs instruments shaped this way. Reclamation bonds under the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977. Decommissioning bonds posted against the eventual closure of oil and gas wells. Norway's sovereign fund, which is a bond-like commitment at national scale. The reparations economics that treats a historical wrong as a documented debt, which is bond-like in its structure. And the civic-republican obligation not to dominate the future, for which a posted bond is the natural accounting form.

The naming of the backward instrument is deliberate, and the choice of "Restitution" over "Reparations" is worth explaining. Restitution, restitutio in integrum, is the older and broader legal-philosophical term for the obligation to restore what was taken. The framework needs an umbrella wide enough to cover the full range of cost-severance contexts: environmental, occupational, community, ecosystem. The reparations tradition of Coates, Darity, Mullen, Hamilton, and Conley is the primary informing source; its methodology is how the framework knows what it knows about backward-looking accountability. But "Reparations Bond" would narrow the umbrella to one historically specific application, and it would make the method's portability read as analogy when in fact it is direct application. The framework joins the tradition rather than translating or rebranding it. The narrower alternatives fail for their own reasons: Restoration presupposes a physical restorability many harms do not allow; Indemnification routes through an insurance register the framework does not use; Damages collapses the bond into the mere measurement instrument. To be exact about the relationship to the reparations field's own categories: the Restitution Bond operationalizes the material-restitution component of redress, using Darity and Mullen's language inside their own three-part typology of acknowledgment-and-atonement, redress, and closure, rather than against it.

The forward instrument carries the word "Foreclosure," and the reader will hear the 2008 housing event in it first. That is continuity, not coincidence. Foreclosure, from forclore, to bar from, describes both events. The mortgage foreclosure bars a household from a primary asset through a legal architecture that lets the bar happen at scale. The framework's forward instrument addresses the way future generations are barred from commons-options, a habitable atmosphere, intact ecosystems, irreplaceable material reserves, by a legal and economic architecture that lets the bar happen across time. Same mechanism, different substrate. The alternatives miss the point: Future-Generations reads soft and philanthropic; Sustainability has been co-opted into corporate reporting; Reclamation is the narrower statutory term; Conservation and Intergenerational both miss the bar-from feature that is the whole content of the word.

The lineage that grounds these instruments runs through the cases rather than in a list, and I want to keep it that way so that no reader gets buried under names.

Pistor is already on the table from Libby, where the bankruptcy discharge and the acquittal turned out to be design features of the legal modules that encode capital. Where Pistor diagnoses the modules that encode the severance, the framework prices what the modules sever; the instrument is the accounting answer to the legal architecture she describes.

The forward instrument's moral standing comes first from Derek Parfit, whose *Reasons and Persons*, published in 1984, grounds an obligation to future generations at the level of outcomes rather than specific persons. I want to be honest about a limit here: Parfit's non-identity problem, the puzzle that our choices change which people come to exist, so that we cannot straightforwardly say we have wronged a particular future person, is not solved by this framework. I name it as a standing limit, not a defeated one. The bond operates on outcomes, on whether a future has a habitable atmosphere, not on the identity of any particular future person, which is the level at which the obligation can be stated without resolving the puzzle.

This is where I need to re-weight a grounding I have leaned on before. The civic-republican tradition, Pettit's *Republicanism* of 1997 and Skinner's *Liberty Before Liberalism* of 1998, defines freedom as non-domination, the condition of not being subject to another's arbitrary power, and it can be extended to say that a present generation must not dominate a future one. That extension is real and useful, but it does its strongest work on the within-generation harms this chapter has documented, the worker and the community subject to a power they cannot check. For the forward arm, the load is better carried by intergenerational ethics: Parfit's standing claim, together with the ethics of discounting that the next chapter takes up, which is John Broome's argument that the discount rate applied to future welfare is a moral choice rather than a technical one, and that pure time preference, valuing a future person's welfare less simply because it is in the future, has no ethical defense. The republican reading of non-domination is best offered as a complement to that grounding, not as the load-bearing forward frame.

The environmental-bond economics of the forward instrument descend from Hartwick's rule, set out in 1977, which holds that the rents from depleting a finite resource should be reinvested so that future generations are not made poorer, the reclamation-bond tradition, and Norway's fund, the cleanest application of the principle in the world. These are foreclosure-prevention lineages, and I keep them separate from the reparations lineage, because the two faces of the bond should not be blurred.

For the backward instrument, Ta-Nehisi Coates supplies the argument-architecture, in *The Case for Reparations*: name the mechanism, name the harm, name the debt. The framework's backward instrument is a structural extension of that architecture into extraction-community contexts. And the contemporary landmark is the work of William Darity and Kirsten Mullen, in *From Here to Equality*, with the matched-comparison empirical method developed by Hamilton, Darity, Price, and colleagues in *Umbrellas Don't Make It Rain*, and the earlier wealth-as-structural-variable method of Dalton Conley in *Being Black, Living in the Red*. The key finding of the matched-comparison method is that Black households in the highest achievement stratum hold less wealth than white households in lower strata, which means the structural variable is wealth, not behavior. I want to be precise about what the framework takes from them, because it would be easy to overstate it. Darity and Mullen already priced. They computed magnitudes; the framework does not supply a number they were missing. What the framework does is port their matched-comparison pricing method to other extraction-community contexts and specialize it: McDowell County's wealth collapse measured against comparable non-extraction Appalachian counties; Libby's cumulative health-cost burden against comparable communities Grace never touched; the opioid-mortality demography of Appalachia against comparable counties that the documented marketing maps of Purdue's product did not concentrate on. That third leg, the opioid case, a later chapter carries in full. This is direct application of their discipline, joining the tradition, not "they diagnosed and we quantified."

There is one contemporary field that, like this framework's own apparatus, runs in both directions at once, and it reached the bidirectional atmospheric commons before this book did, so I want to credit it plainly. Andrew Fanning and Jason Hickel, in *Nature Sustainability* in 2023, priced the appropriation of the atmosphere by historical emitters as a debt; the CARICOM reparatory-justice plan advances the same logic at the level of nations; and the Loss and Damage Fund, established at the UN climate conferences in 2022 and operationalized in 2023, institutionalizes the idea. All of it prices an over-appropriated atmospheric commons as a debt that runs backward, against historical emissions, and forward, against foreclosed climate stability, on a social-cost-of-carbon spine. I concede the priority openly. That program reached the bidirectional atmospheric commons first, and it argues the case at the scale the problem is usually argued, the national aggregate, who owes whom across a shared global carbon budget. What this framework adds is not the idea but the grain. It prices a single extraction event rather than a national balance, and it reserves against that event rather than netting one nation's account against another's. I will credit the field and stop there. I assign no atmospheric or climate-reparations dollar figure, and I take no position on who owes whom; that is not mine to enter.

The backward direction has a boundary I want to mark honestly. The same apparatus runs in reverse, and the most developed backward work is Darity and Mullen's pricing of the racial wealth gap and the longevity gap, the difference in life expectancy as a measurable legacy effect, with McDowell's life-expectancy gap as the coal-extraction variant of the same legacy-effect method. But the coercion vector itself, what to assign to coerced labor as a cost dimension in its own right, distinct from its legacy effects, remains methodologically unresolved in the reparations-economics field, and it is outside this book's empirical scope. The framework reaches coerced cases through their legacy effects, the wealth gaps and longevity gaps and disruptions of knowledge transmission and intergenerational trauma. The direct pricing of the coercion vector is an open question this book defers, with the moral weight of it deferred to those who have standing to carry it. Indigenous land dispossession and colonization-severed cultural knowledge sit in the same deferred territory, structurally accessible to the methodology applied backward, to be computed in later work, not here. I will not produce a coercion or a reparations dollar figure in these pages.

So measure the gap, as a fact rather than a metaphor. Across American extraction regimes the bond posted is far below the residual value of the commons, by orders of magnitude. The Black Lung Disability Trust Fund has paid out roughly forty-four billion dollars through 2009, a conservative and honestly-dated floor for the program's full run, because no authoritative cumulative total exists past that date. The trust fund's own outstanding debt stood at roughly 5.1 billion dollars as of September 2024, a separate quantity from the cumulative paid, and I keep the two distinct. No instrument prices the severance the fund was built to compensate. The deeper trouble is that the instrument exists at scale but is mis-assigned: when the coal excise tax falls short of what the fund owes, the difference is borrowed from the federal general fund, from the public, rather than recovered from the extraction that severed the cost in the first place. The reclamation bonds posted across the coalfields cover several billion dollars less than the actual cost of reclamation. The intergenerational obligation inside Social Security has no Foreclosure Bond standing against it at all. This is the accountability gap, stated as a measurable fact: the bond posted is a small fraction of the cost severed.

A last distinction, to keep the book inside its lane. The question of what is owed is a separate question from what any society is currently prepared to collect. The methodology applies regardless of political readiness; the framework's contribution here is accounting, not advocacy. The path from current bond levels toward the residual value of the commons is a political and philosophical matter, scoped to a later chapter as an open question rather than a single prescription this book legislates.

The chapter has named the severance and documented it across the cases, from a financial system to the compact between generations to the financing of medical care at the edge of a life. It has met the strongest counter-positions on their own terms. It has answered the commodification worry through Polanyi, Fraser, and Sandel, and shown in Pistor's own terms why accounting a cost is not coding an asset. It has restored the rent-seeking complement and credited the diagnostic tradition that named the asymmetry. And it has introduced the two-instrument response with the lineage that grounds it. What remains is the methodology that the next chapter supplies, the universality stress-test of the chapter after, the single fully worked number later still, the political economy of closing the gap, and the close. The question that carries the book from here forward is no longer whether cost severance exists. It is what we are going to do about it.
