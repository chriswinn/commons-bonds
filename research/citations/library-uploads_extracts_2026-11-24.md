## Hartwick (1977)

John M. Hartwick, "Intergenerational Equity and the Investing of Rents from Exhaustible Resources," *American
Economic Review* 67, no. 5 (Dec. 1977): 972–974. Read in full.

| Page | His words | Bears on |
|---|---|---|
| 972 | "Invest all profits or rents from exhaustible resources in reproducible capital such as machines. This injunction seems to solve the ethical problem of the current generation shortchanging future generations by 'overconsuming' the current product." | Ch 5 line 242; TA §14.2 |
| 972 | The question he sets: "if society invests all rents from exhaustible resources in reproducible capital goods, and invests only this amount … will consumption and output rise, remain constant, or fall over time?" | TA §14.2 |
| 972 | "I shall formally set this problem out below and solve it for the case of a Cobb-Douglas technology. The Cobb-Douglas technology has the important property that each input (in particular, the flow of minerals from an exhaustible resource) is essential for producing a positive output … Thus the economy cannot exhaust any natural resource and continue to have positive consumption and output." | TA §14.2 (what he assumes) |
| 973 | The result uses "Solow's definition of intergenerational equity—namely per capita consumption remaining constant over time." | Ch 5 line 242 |
| 974 | "Thus we have established, for general technologies, the rule: 'the investment of current exhaustible resource returns in reproducible capital implies per capita consumption constant.'" With depreciation of capital the rule "will not provide for the maintaining of per capita consumption constant over time." | TA §14.2 |

**What it settles.** The rule as Chapter 5 and TA §14.2 state it is his. TA §14.2's "Hartwick assumes that the
physical resource can be fully substituted by reproducible capital" says more than the paper: in his worked
case the resource is "essential" and can never be exhausted; capital stands in for a dwindling flow of it, not
for the resource altogether. He also assumes constant population and, for the clean result, capital that does
not wear out.

Nicholas Kaldor, "Welfare Propositions of Economics and Interpersonal Comparisons of Utility," *Economic
Journal* 49, no. 195 (Sept. 1939): 549–552. J. R. Hicks, "The Foundations of Welfare Economics," *Economic
Journal* 49, no. 196 (Dec. 1939): 696–712. Both read.

| Source, page | Words | Bears on |
|---|---|---|
| Kaldor, 550 | "it is always possible for the Government to ensure that the previous income-distribution should be maintained intact: by compensating the 'landlords' for any loss of income and by providing the funds for such compensation by an extra tax on those whose incomes have been augmented." Where a policy raises aggregate real income, "it is possible to make everybody better off than before, or at any rate to make some people better off without making anybody worse off." | TA §15.1.7 |
| Hicks, 699, 701 | He credits the way round to "Mr. Kaldor" and calls it "the third method." | TA §15.1.7 |

one citation is standing for two. Neither article has a bibliography entry. Hicks's page numbers above are
counted from the article's first page and should be confirmed against the PDF before they are printed.

## Rosen (1974)

Sherwin Rosen, "Hedonic Prices and Implicit Markets: Product Differentiation in Pure Competition," *Journal of
Political Economy* 82, no. 1 (Jan.–Feb. 1974): 34–55. Read; searched for wage, risk and value of life.

| Page | Words | Bears on |
|---|---|---|
| 34 | "Hedonic prices are defined as the implicit prices of attributes and are revealed to economic agents from observed prices of differentiated products and the specific amounts of characteristics associated with them." | TA §3.4 |

## Hotelling (1931)

Harold Hotelling, "The Economics of Exhaustible Resources," *Journal of Political Economy* 39, no. 2 (Apr.
1931): 137–175. Text searched and the passages below read in context; not read line by line.

| Page | His words | Bears on |
|---|---|---|
| 141 | "Here p is to be interpreted as the net price received after paying the cost of extraction and placing upon the market—a convention to which we shall adhere throughout. The formula (1) p = p₀e^{γt} fixes the relative prices at different times under free competition." | TA §4.1 (rent rises at the rate of interest: supported); §14.1 ("resource prices should rise at the rate of interest": his p is the price net of extraction cost) |
| 141 | "putting q = f(p, t) for the quantity taken at time t if the price is p" | TA Definition 1.3 (q for the extraction flow: supported) |
| 143 | Section 3, "Maximum Social Value and State Interference": under free competition there is "a certain tendency toward maximizing what might be called the 'total utility' but is better called the 'social value of the resource.'" | TA §4.1 |
| 144 | "The market rate of interest γ must be used by an entrepreneur in his calculations, but should it be used in determinations of social value and optimum public policy?" He also describes rushes on new discoveries as "immensely wasteful socially." | TA §4.1, §14.1 |
| 165 | On the "severance tax … of so much per unit of material extracted from the mine": it "tends to conservation." | Ch 5's note on the word "severance"; TA |

**What it settles.** Definition 1.3's symbol and §4.1's statement of the rule are his. §14.1's "resource
prices should rise at the rate of interest" needs "net of extraction cost," which is how he defines p. On
§4.1's "It does not contain the cost that extraction places on anyone else": a search of the whole text for
pollution, damage, nuisance and external found nothing, and his "social value" is the value to consumers. He
does treat monopoly, wasteful rushes, taxes and whether the market interest rate is right for public policy,
so "his model has no third-party damage" is supported and "he ignores the public interest" would not be.

## Spence (1976)

Michael Spence, "Product Differentiation and Welfare," *American Economic Review* 66, no. 2, Papers and
Proceedings (May 1976): 407–414. Read.

**What it settles.** TA Definition 1.3 cites "Spence 1976 *Review of Economic Studies*" for a contrast with
"Q-as-quality." The paper the appendix's own §18 lists is this one, in the *American Economic Review*, and it
is about which products a market selects ("product differentiation, product selection, and monopolistic
competition," p. 407). The word "quality" does not occur in it, and its quantity symbol is x. It does not
support the notation remark. Either another Spence paper was meant, which nobody has identified, or the
