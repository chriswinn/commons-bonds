# Long-absence, high-risk work: what the research supports (2026-11-10)

**Status:** research note (internal). **Verification level:** bibliographic details and abstract-level findings
from web search on 2026-11-10. **No full texts were read.** Read each source before quoting it or citing a number
from it in the TA or a chapter. Nothing here has been added to the TA or any chapter.

**Question asked:** what does academic research say about the family, psychological and relationship effects of
jobs that take people away for long stretches, especially jobs with high fatality rates (commercial fishing,
offshore oil, seafaring, fly-in fly-out mining, military deployment)?

## The short answer

The evidence is **more mixed than the intuition**. It supports a narrow claim well and a broad claim poorly.

- **Well supported:** these jobs kill people at many times the average rate; workers on long rotations report
  more psychological distress than comparable workers; separation from family is the stressor seafarers rank
  first; partners at home are lonelier and sleep worse while the worker is away; the people hit hardest are those
  new to the arrangement and those with young children.
- **Not supported:** that these jobs reliably damage marriages or children. The largest reviews call the effect on
  partners and children "unclear." One classic study concluded the effects "have been exaggerated." Reviews found
  no good evidence of higher family breakdown. Military-deployment meta-analyses find small or negligible average
  effects on children.
- **Not found:** any study that measures whether the wage premium in these jobs pays for the family cost. The
  wage literature measures pay for fatal risk. It does not measure pay for absence.

So the defensible statement is not "these jobs wreck families." It is: **the wage is known to price the danger;
whether it prices the absence has not been measured; the family cost is real for some, uneven, and hard for an
individual to predict before living it.**

Note: periods and denominators differ. Do not put two of these rates side by side without saying so. The decline
matters: "deadliest job" is truer of the 1990s crab fleet than of today's.

## 2. The worker's own mental health

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Parker, Fruhen et al. 2018, *Impact of FIFO work arrangements on the mental health and wellbeing of FIFO workers* (Curtin University, for the WA Mental Health Commission; about 3,000 workers) | 33% reported high or very high psychological distress, against 17% of non-FIFO workers. The gap held after controlling for demographics. |
| Brooks & Greenberg 2022, *BMC Psychology* (systematic review, seafarers) | Loneliness, long separation from home, fatigue and long voyages are the main risks to wellbeing. |
| Jonglertmontree et al. 2022, *BMC Public Health* (scoping review, seafarers) | Lists separation from spouses and families and social isolation among risk factors linked to stress, depression and burnout. |
| "Psychological stress in seafarers," *International Maritime Health* 63(4), 2012 (review) | Ranked separation from family as the most important stress factor. |
| Mellbye & Carter 2017, *International Maritime Health* 68(2) | **Caution:** data on seafarer depression and suicide are "scarce and fragmented"; several studies assume a causal link from isolation to depression without robust evidence. |

## 3. Partners and children

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Morrice & Taylor 1978 | Coined "intermittent husband syndrome" (anxiety, depression) among wives of North Sea oil workers. |
| Taylor, Morrice, Clark & McCann 1985, *Social Science & Medicine* 20(9): 877–885 | Follow-up epidemiological study. Concluded the psychosocial effects "have been exaggerated." Most affected: newly married wives with pre-school children and no earlier experience of absence. |
| Asare et al. 2023, *Psychology & Health* (systematic review, 19 studies, mining and offshore oil and gas) | Impact on partners' and children's mental health "remains unclear." Partners may be lonelier and sleep worse on away days. Support matters most with young children and when the rotation is new. |
| Australian Institute of Family Studies review (FIFO) | "Lack of empirical evidence" that FIFO work raises family breakdown. Few studies on children; one found no difference between FIFO children, military children and controls. |
| 2016 survey of 232 FIFO partners (*Journal of Child and Family Studies*) | Partners reported more emotional problems and harsher discipline than community mothers. |
| Study of Norwegian offshore-supply seafarers' wives (*International Maritime Health*) | No difference from controls in marital quality, self-rated health or wellbeing; being alone "may exaggerate" problems at home. |
| Canadian offshore-fishing study (cited in the same literature) | Wives had more anxiety and depression tied to separations; most coped; about 10% reported higher distress. |
| Zvonkovic, Solomon, Humble & Manoogian 2005, *Family Relations* 54(3): 411–422 | Qualitative study of families of men whose jobs require travel. **Sample occupations not confirmed by search** (believed to include commercial fishermen and long-haul truckers; check the paper). |
| Card et al. 2011, *Journal of Family Psychology* (meta-analysis, 16 studies, military deployment) | Small to negligible association between a parent's deployment and children's adjustment; strongest in middle childhood. |
| 2019 meta-analysis (military deployment) | More problem behavior in children of deployed parents; effect sizes small. |

## 4. Why a person cannot price it in advance

| Source | What it offers | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| L. A. Paul 2014, *Transformative Experience* (Oxford University Press) | Some experiences cannot be evaluated before you have them, because having them teaches you something and changes what you want. Becoming a parent is her main example. | Contested. Mathony & Messerli 2023 report that people predict "transformative" experiences about as well as ordinary ones. |
| Loewenstein, O'Donoghue & Rabin 2003, "Projection Bias in Predicting Future Utility," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 118(4): 1209–1248 | People underestimate how much their future tastes will differ from present ones. | Mainstream economics; safest anchor of the three. |
| Kahneman, Wakker & Sarin 1997, "Back to Bentham? Explorations of Experienced Utility," *QJE* 112(2): 375–405 | Separates what you choose (decision utility) from what you then live through (experienced utility). | Gives the vocabulary, not the finding. |
| Wilson & Gilbert 2003, "Affective Forecasting," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 35 | People mispredict their future feelings. | **Cuts the other way too:** the usual error is overestimating how bad things will feel. |

## 5. Does the wage pay for it?

- The compensating-differential literature finds a wage premium for **fatal** injury risk and only mixed evidence
  for non-fatal risk (Viscusi and others; the TA already cites Rosen 1974 and Viscusi 1993).
- A 2024 IZA discussion paper (dp16716) argues wages do not fully compensate for on-the-job risk. Not read.
- Viscusi & Moore's work on worker learning notes that younger workers assess job risk less accurately.
- **Gap:** the search found no study that tests whether rotation or at-sea pay compensates for family separation.
  That absence is itself usable: it is an honest reason to leave the slot Open.

## What this note does not cover

- **Concealed risk (the football player).** Not researched this session. It needs its own sourcing: the public
  litigation and settlement record and the published reporting on what the league knew. Do not write it from memory.
- First-person accounts. The research gives rates and averages. The texture (a missed first step) must come from
  the author's own material or from an attributed, citable account.
