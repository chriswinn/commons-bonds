**Status:** internal. Read by the session on 2026-11-26 for the open items in
`tools/audits/claims-and-sources_running-list_2026-11-25.md`. Each entry gives the words on the page, where

## 1. Mullen, Richardson, Bassett and Darity (2026): both authors, and the life-expectancy measure

A. Kirsten Mullen, Eugene T. Richardson, Mary T. Bassett and William A. Darity, "A plan for black American
reparations," *BMJ Global Health* (2026), DOI 10.1136/bmjgh-2024-017216; open access, PMC13034248. Full text
read through Europe PMC.

- "This paper proposes the Black-White wealth gap as the best summary measure of the ongoing costs of
  enslavement and discriminatory policies that followed for living descendants of the enslaved."
- "Elimination of this gap based on the 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances is currently estimated at $16
  trillion."
- "An alternative summary measure can be based on the life expectancy gap between Black and White Americans.
  Monetising the gap leads to an estimate of about $27 trillion." Its note 8 is Darity, Craemer, Berry et al.
  (2024).
- "We focus on restitution for Black American descendants of persons enslaved in the USA."
- "The roughly 5-year difference in life expectancy between Black and White Americans has been linked to the
  enduring legacy of racial subjugation in the USA."
- Other bases it names for a bill: "unpaid labour during the period of enslavement" and "the present value of
  the land" promised (40 acres).

**Bears on:** running-list V1 (Ch 5 lines 246, 252; item 2). A paper with **both Mullen and Darity** as
authors names the life-expectancy gap as an alternative measure and gives it a dollar figure. So "their
measures of the racial wealth gap, and of the gap in life expectancy" can stand with this citation, provided
the sentence keeps their ranking: the wealth gap is "the best summary measure," life expectancy the
"alternative." "Broken transmission of knowledge" and "trauma" are still not priced anywhere read.
**Discrepancy to note:** this paper says "about $27 trillion" and cites the 2024 essay, where the earlier
session read about $29 trillion (p. 20). Quote one, with its own citation; do not merge them.
**Also bears on:** the name "Restitution." They use the word for their own program.

## 3. McDowell County's drug-overdose death rate

National Center for Health Statistics, "NCHS – Drug Poisoning Mortality by County: United States"
(data.cdc.gov, dataset rpvx-m2md), model-based age-adjusted death rates per 100,000, drug poisoning of all
kinds (ICD-10 X40–X44, X60–X64, X85, Y10–Y14). Queried 2026-11-26.

- McDowell County, WV: 2015, **83.5**; 2016, 100.7; 2017, 110.5; 2020, **142.6**; 2021, **164.6**.
- Sorting every county by rate, McDowell is **first in the United States** in 2015, 2020 and 2021 (the three
  years queried). Wyoming County, WV, is second in each; Rio Arriba County, NM, third.
- The dataset's own caution: West Virginia's rates may be understated in 2005 and 2009.

**Bears on:** TA 1b item 7 ("the highest per-capita opioid death rate in the United States") and item 8
("141 overdose deaths per 100K"). Supported, with two corrections: the measure is **all drug poisonings**,
not opioids alone, and it is a **modelled** rate. The 141 does not match 2015 in this series (83.5); it is
close to 2020 (142.6), so the year attached to it in the appendix is probably wrong, or it comes from a
crude-rate source not yet identified. A sentence this supports: "By the CDC's county estimates, McDowell had
the highest drug-overdose death rate of any county in the United States in 2015, in 2020 and in 2021."
Decisions log 018 can close on this.

## 4. The Federal Reserve's emergency lending

Government Accountability Office, *Federal Reserve System: Opportunities Exist to Strengthen Policies and
Processes for Managing Emergency Assistance*, GAO-11-696 (July 2011). gao.gov refuses sessions; the full
report was read from the copy posted on a U.S. senator's site.

- Highlights page: "Loans outstanding for the emergency programs peaked at more than $1 trillion in late 2008."
- Table 8 ("Institutions with Largest Total Transaction Amounts (Not Term-Adjusted)"): total **$16,115**
  billion. The term-adjusted table that follows totals **$1,139** billion.
- Period covered: December 1, 2007, through July 21, 2010.

**Bears on:** Ch 5 item 7 (line 90, "trillions of dollars within days"). The $16 trillion is every loan added
up over about two and a half years, counted each time it was renewed; the report's own headline is "more than
$1 trillion" at the peak. The replacement in the report stands, now on the page itself. The project's
register figure "$1.1 trillion to $1.6 trillion" was not found in these words; use "more than $1 trillion."

## 6. Libby: what the mine earned

*EHS Today*, "Grand Jury Indictment: W.R. Grace Lied About Dangers of Asbestos Exposure," February 8, 2005,
read on the page: "According to the indictment, W.R. Grace 'enjoyed at least $140 million in after-tax
profits from its mining operations in Libby.'"

**Bears on:** running-list A4 (the unsourced "$100 million" revenue and the "six times" result). This is the
first figure found for what the mine earned. Cautions: it is profit after tax, not revenue; it is the
prosecution's allegation in an indictment that ended in acquittal; and it is read here in a trade magazine's
quotation, not in the indictment. Two searches found no revenue figure. **Next step for a session:** find the
indictment itself (*United States v. W.R. Grace*, D. Mont., February 2005) and read the sentence. If it
holds, the comparison becomes "cleanup has cost several times what the government said the mine earned in
profit," with the allegation named as one.

## 9. "Restitution" in the United Nations' principles on reparation

Theo van Boven, introductory note to the *Basic Principles and Guidelines on the Right to a Remedy and
Reparation* (General Assembly resolution 60/147, 2005), United Nations Audiovisual Library of International
Law (legal.un.org/avl), read on the page. The resolution's own text at ohchr.org refuses sessions.

- "The various forms of reparation and their scope and content, covering both monetary and non-monetary
  reparations, may be summarized as follows:"
- "Restitution refers to measures which 'restore the victim to the original situation before the gross
  violations…' (principle 19). Examples of restitution include: restoration of liberty, enjoyment of human
  rights, identity, family life and citizenship, return to one's place of residence, restoration of employment
  and return of property."
- "Compensation: 'should be provided for any economically assessable damage…' (principle 20)."
- Then rehabilitation (principle 21), satisfaction (22) and guarantees of non-repetition.

**Bears on:** running-list A10 (TA §5.1.1, §5.2: restitution "covers monetary, legal and structural
restoration and is broader than damages"). This source cuts **against** that sentence. In the UN's scheme
restitution is one of five forms and means putting things back as they were; a money payment for harm that
can be assessed is *compensation*, a different form. Darity et al. (2024), note 1, uses the same family of
terms ("Restitution, compensation, and rehabilitation all fall under the category of redress"). The safest
course is to cut the legal sentence and rest the name on Darity and Mullen's own usage (see entry 1). If a
legal footing is wanted, the sentence has to say that the book uses the word more widely than this body of
law does.

## 10. Baotou

- Sky News, "Clean energy's dirty secret: Pond filled with toxic material in northern China is by-product of
  rare earth processing," 17 April 2022 (read on a syndicated copy). A farmer near the pond: "It's called
  Dalahai village. The village was polluted - 30 to 40% of the villagers got cancer… After they found the
  pollution, the government moved all villagers to somewhere else. They banned local villagers from farming on
  the land." The reporter: "The village was moved about five miles away." Julie Klinger, University of
  Delaware: "public health researchers in China have identified a series of specific forms of cancer that are
  associated with exposure to contaminants from the rare earth and iron mining industry." The pond "is
  seeping into the ground below, poisoning the water."
- A 2020 study in *Scientific Reports*, "Geobiochemistry characteristics of rare earth elements in soil and
  ground water: a case study in Baotou, China" (DOI 10.1038/s41598-020-68661-4; open access; authors not yet
  noted): the pond "is located at 12 km distance from the west of Baotou City"; it "has a 13.60 km perimeters
  and its tailings impoundment covers an area of 12 km²"; "rare earth elements accumulated in ground water and
  soil around the tailings pond"; "The rare earth tailings pond is open to the environment, which damages the
  environment around the tailings pond."

**Bears on:** running-list A8 (TA §11.4; Ch 5 lines 130–138). Now sourced, as reported: one village
(Dalahai) relocated, farming banned on its land, residents and a scholar reporting cancers, groundwater
around the pond contaminated. Still with nothing behind them: "10 million tons" a year; the 10 km radius; the
Yellow River basin's 100 million people; the cancer types as listed; export revenue; "$1 trillion in consumer
goods"; any remediation figure. The villager's "30 to 40 percent" is one man's statement to a reporter and
should not be used as a rate.
